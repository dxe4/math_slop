
# References and Further Reading {#section-1-references-and-further-reading}

This bibliography identifies well-known, verifiable works in combinatorial game theory. The book is written under CC BY-SA 4.0; standard definitions and game conventions belong to shared practice. References are included for further reading, historical context, alternative treatments, and broader coverage.

A named result or study is a retrieval convention, not a complete history. Combinatorial game theory has been developed across multiple communities, languages, and institutions; a modern statement may differ from an early source.

<a id="section-5-foundational-texts"></a>
## Foundational texts {#section-7-foundational-texts}

- Berlekamp, E. R., Conway, J. H., & Guy, R. K. (2001–2004). *Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays* (2nd ed., 4 vols.). A K Peters. The standard comprehensive reference for combinatorial game theory; covers impartial and partizan games, Nim, Hackenbush, Domineering, and temperature theory. Claims should be read with its scope and notation conventions.
- Conway, J. H. (2001). *On Numbers and Games* (2nd ed.). A K Peters. The original construction of surreal numbers through games; denser than *Winning Ways* and essential for readers who want the foundational number-system perspective.
- Albert, M. H., Nowakowski, R. J., & Wolfe, D. (2007). *Lessons in Play: An Introduction to Combinatorial Game Theory*. A K Peters. A first-course text at the undergraduate level; self-contained, with many exercises and solutions. The closest sibling to this book in audience and goals.

These three works use overlapping but not identical notation. Comparison is most useful when conventions are recorded rather than blended.

<a id="section-13-impartial-games-and-sprague-grundy-theory"></a>
## Impartial games and Sprague–Grundy theory {#section-15-impartial-games-and-sprague-grundy-theory}

- Sprague, R. (1935). "Über mathematische Kampfspiele." *Tohoku Mathematical Journal, 41*, 438–444. The original statement of what became the Sprague–Grundy theorem, proved independently by Sprague and Grundy.
- Grundy, P. M. (1939). "Mathematics and games." *Eureka, 2*, 6–8. The companion independent proof; introduced the minimum-excluded-value construction.
- Bouton, C. L. (1901). "Nim, a game with a complete mathematical theory." *Annals of Mathematics, 3*(1/4), 35–39. The classical solution of Nim under both normal and misère play.

<a id="section-19-partizan-games-and-temperature"></a>
## Partizan games and temperature {#section-21-partizan-games-and-temperature}

- Berlekamp, E. R. (1988). "Blockbusting and domineering." *Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A, 49*(1), 67–116. Seminal paper on Domineering analysis and the computational approach to partizan games.
- Berlekamp, E. R. (1991). "Introductory overview of mathematical Go endgames." In *Combinatorial Games*, Proceedings of Symposia in Applied Mathematics, 43, 73–100. American Mathematical Society. The application of temperature theory and thermography to Go endgames.
- Wolfe, D. (1991). *Mathematics of Go: Chilling Gets the Last Point*. Ph.D. thesis, University of California, Berkeley. The definitive treatment of Go endgames as combinatorial games.

<a id="section-25-algorithmic-and-computational-game-solving"></a>
## Algorithmic and computational game solving {#section-27-algorithmic-and-computational-game-solving}

- Fraenkel, A. S., & Simonson, S. (1993). "Geography." *Theoretical Computer Science, 110*(1), 197–214. Complexity results for combinatorial games on graphs.
- Demaine, E. D., & Hearn, R. A. (2009). "Playing games with algorithms: Algorithmic combinatorial game theory." In *Games of No Chance 3*, Mathematical Sciences Research Institute Publications, 56, 3–56. Cambridge University Press. A survey of computational complexity results and open boundaries. It reports the complexity of determining Domineering outcome or value as open. Its generalized-game results keep their rule and input qualifications: Go from arbitrary $n\times n$ configurations is PSPACE-hard, the Japanese-rules version with ko is EXPTIME-complete, and the $n\times n$ generalization of Chess from arbitrary configurations is EXPTIME-complete.
- van den Herik, H. J., Uiterwijk, J. W. H. M., & van Rijswijck, J. (2002). "Games solved: Now and in the future." *Artificial Intelligence, 134*(1–2), 277–311. Survey of exact and heuristic solving methods for games.
- Hearn, R. A. (2009). “Amazons is PSPACE-complete.” In *Games of No Chance 3*, Mathematical Sciences Research Institute Publications, 56, 287--306. [MSRI volume contents](https://library.slmath.org/books/Book56/contents.html); preprint arXiv:cs/0502013. Establishes the result for generalized $n\times n$ Amazons, not a current state count or fixed-board feasibility claim.

<a id="section-32-chess-endgames"></a>
## Chess endgames {#section-34-chess-endgames}

- Nunn, J. (2002). *Secrets of Pawnless Endings* (2nd ed.). Gambit Publications. ISBN 978-1-901983-65-4. Exact analysis of pawnless endings, including database and retrograde methods; it is not a king-and-pawn reference.
- Müller, K., & Lamprecht, F. (2001). *Fundamental Chess Endings*. Gambit Publications. A comprehensive reference with combinatorial and practical perspectives.
- Stiller, L. (1996). "Multilinear algebra and chess endgames." In *Games of No Chance*, Mathematical Sciences Research Institute Publications, 29, 151–192. Cambridge University Press. The use of group theory and retrograde analysis in chess endgame databases.
- de Man, R. *Syzygy endgame tablebases: probing code and format documentation*. [Author repository](https://github.com/syzygy1/tb/blob/master/README.md). Documents exact W/D/L and DTZ results for positions covered by the tablebase and its rule conventions; it does not make tablebases necessary for every exact endgame proof.

<a id="section-39-historical-attribution-checklist"></a>
## Historical attribution checklist {#section-41-historical-attribution-checklist}

Every historical or priority claim should record:

1. a classification as contemporary exposition, secondary history, translated primary source, or primary source;
2. verified author, title, edition, publisher or archive, and stable locator;
3. a note explaining whether the citation supports provenance, formulation, evidence, example, or broader context;
4. a check that the modern terminology is not projected unchanged backward;
5. careful comparison of primary sources for culturally broad claims and disputed priority.

<a id="section-49-reading-route-recommendations"></a>
## Reading-route recommendations {#section-51-reading-route-recommendations}

For a deeper treatment of impartial games and Nim-like structures, proceed to *Winning Ways* or *Lessons in Play*. For surreal numbers and partizan algebra, continue with *On Numbers and Games*. For computational complexity and algorithmic solving, see Demaine and Hearn's survey. For Go endgames, consult Berlekamp and Wolfe. For chess endgames, Nunn and Müller provide exact analysis.

<a id="section-53-citation-policy-for-contributors"></a>
## Citation policy for contributors {#section-55-citation-policy-for-contributors}

Paraphrase sources and cite the job they perform. Do not copy exercise sequences, distinctive examples, diagrams, or prose merely because a theorem is standard. Direct quotations require exact locators and license or fair-use review. When sources disagree, record the disagreement. When provenance is unknown, label it unknown rather than inventing a clean lineage.
