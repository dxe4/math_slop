
# References and Further Reading

This bibliography identifies standard comparison texts, historical sources, and current research directions. The book is written under CC BY-SA 4.0; standard definitions and conventions belong to shared practice. References are included for further reading, historical context, alternative treatments, and broader coverage.

A named result or study is a retrieval convention, not a complete history. Continuum mechanical ideas are often the product of long development across languages, institutions, and communities; a modern statement may differ sharply from an early source.

## Continuum mechanics and elasticity

- Gurtin, M. E. (1981). *An Introduction to Continuum Mechanics*. Academic Press. A concise, mathematically careful treatment of kinematics, balance laws, and constitutive theory at the graduate level.
- Malvern, L. E. (1969). *Introduction to the Mechanics of a Continuous Medium*. Prentice Hall. A classic engineering-oriented text with extensive worked examples and physical interpretation.
- Ogden, R. W. (1997 reprint). *Non-linear Elastic Deformations*. Dover. The standard reference for finite elasticity; the linear elasticity chapters provide useful contrast with the infinitesimal theory treated here.
- Marsden, J. E., & Hughes, T. J. R. (1994 republication). *Mathematical Foundations of Elasticity*. Dover. A rigorous treatment of nonlinear elasticity, variational methods, and existence theory; the original Prentice-Hall work appeared in 1983.

These texts use different notational conventions for stress and strain. Comparison is most useful when those conventions are recorded rather than blended.

## Fluid mechanics

- Batchelor, G. K. (1967). *An Introduction to Fluid Dynamics*. Cambridge University Press. The standard first-course reference for incompressible and compressible flow, boundary layers, and low-Reynolds-number flows.
- Landau, L. D., & Lifshitz, E. M. (1987). *Fluid Mechanics* (2nd ed., Vol. 6). Pergamon Press. A concise theoretical treatment with distinctive physical insight; the chapters on viscous flow and turbulence are especially influential.
- Chorin, A. J., & Marsden, J. E. (1993). *A Mathematical Introduction to Fluid Mechanics* (3rd ed.). Springer. A mathematically oriented treatment emphasising the geometric and analytic structure of the Euler and Navier–Stokes equations.
- White, F. M. (2006). *Viscous Fluid Flow* (3rd ed.). McGraw-Hill. A practical engineering reference with extensive exact solutions, boundary-layer theory, and stability analysis.

## Partial differential equations and numerical methods

- Evans, L. C. (2010). *Partial Differential Equations* (2nd ed.). American Mathematical Society. The standard graduate reference for existence, uniqueness, and regularity theory; the chapters on elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic equations are directly relevant.
- LeVeque, R. J. (2002). *Finite Volume Methods for Hyperbolic Problems*. Cambridge University Press. A comprehensive treatment of conservation laws, shock-capturing methods, and Godunov-type schemes.
- LeVeque, R. J. (2007). *Finite Difference Methods for Ordinary and Partial Differential Equations: Steady-State and Time-Dependent Problems*. SIAM. A clear introduction to stability, consistency, and convergence for finite difference and finite volume methods.
- Braess, D. (2007). *Finite Elements: Theory, Fast Solvers, and Applications in Solid Mechanics* (3rd ed.). Cambridge University Press. A mathematically rigorous treatment of finite element methods with emphasis on error analysis and mixed methods.

## Mathematical methods

- Arfken, G. B., & Weber, H. J. (2005). *Mathematical Methods for Physicists: A Comprehensive Guide* (6th ed.). Elsevier Academic Press. A comprehensive reference for vector calculus, special functions, and transform methods used throughout continuum mechanics.
- Riley, K. F., Hobson, M. P., & Bence, S. J. (2006). *Mathematical Methods for Physics and Engineering: A Comprehensive Guide* (3rd ed.). Cambridge University Press. A broad first-course reference with many worked examples.

## Electromagnetism and coupled fields

- Jackson, J. D. (1998 product release; often cited as 1999 from the copyright/imprint). *Classical Electrodynamics* (3rd ed.). Wiley. This bibliography uses Wiley's August 1998 product-release date and records the alternate convention. The book is a standard graduate reference for Maxwell's equations, radiation, and relativistic electrodynamics.
- Davidson, P. A. (2001). *An Introduction to Magnetohydrodynamics*. Cambridge University Press. An accessible treatment of MHD with applications to astrophysics and engineering.

## Historical attribution checklist

Every historical or priority claim should record:

1. a classification as contemporary exposition, secondary history, translated primary source, or primary source;
2. verified author, title, edition, publisher or archive, and stable locator;
3. a note explaining whether the citation supports provenance, formulation, evidence, example, or broader context;
4. a check that modern terminology is not projected unchanged backward;
5. independent review of culturally broad claims and disputed priority.

## Reading-route recommendations

For a deeper treatment of continuum kinematics and balance laws, proceed to Gurtin or Marsden and Hughes. For fluid mechanics, Batchelor remains the standard first reference; Landau and Lifshitz vol. 6 provides theoretical depth. For PDE theory and weak forms, Evans is the natural next step. For numerical methods, LeVeque's finite volume text and Braess's finite element text cover the two main discretisation families used in computational mechanics. For electromagnetism and coupled systems, Jackson and Davidson provide the standard treatments.

## Citation policy for contributors

Paraphrase sources and cite the job they perform. Do not copy exercise sequences, distinctive examples, diagrams, or prose merely because a theorem is standard. Direct quotations require exact locators and license or fair-use review. When sources disagree, record the disagreement. When provenance is unknown, label it unknown rather than inventing a clean lineage.
