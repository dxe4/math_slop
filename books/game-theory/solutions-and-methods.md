Every primary chapter contains ten exercises. Each exercise retains its own stable semantic anchor and offers Hint 1, Hint 2 and Solution as separate reveals. The links below return to those authored exercises and their complete solutions; there is no second answer collection whose corrections could diverge from the local text.

Use the first hint to choose a representation or relevant result. Use the second when you have made a serious attempt but cannot connect that representation to the claim. Compare a complete solution only after writing enough of your own argument to locate a precise disagreement. A correct result obtained by assuming the desired conclusion needs repair even when its arithmetic agrees with the solution.

## Methods to bring to a solution

For a strategic-form problem, begin with players, action sets and the interpretation of each payoff. To establish Nash equilibrium, hold the opponents fixed and compare every available unilateral action. For a finite mixed game, linearity reduces this comparison to pure actions, but the probabilities must still be nonnegative and sum to one. Check unused actions explicitly; indifference among the actions you happened to include does not complete the proof.

For a zero-sum game, seek matching lower and upper certificates. A row mixture that guarantees v and a column mixture that permits at most v establish the value together. If a proposed calculation produces two different bounds, the gap identifies unfinished optimisation rather than permission to average the bounds. In a linear-programming solution, write the primal and dual with their variable signs before using weak duality or complementary slackness.

For an existence argument, identify the domain and the map. Show that the domain has the compactness and convexity properties actually needed and that the map stays in the domain. A fixed point must then be translated back into the desired strategic property. Chapter 7's gain-map calculation is an example of that final translation: simply displaying a continuous map is not the Nash existence proof.

For an extensive-form problem, a strategy must give actions at every information position where the player may be called upon to choose. A proposed equilibrium path omits the off-path actions needed to check credibility. In a repeated game, first specify the continuation after every observable deviation. Then compare the immediate gain with the discounted continuation loss under one consistent normalisation. An arithmetic threshold obtained from a noncredible punishment is not a subgame-perfect equilibrium.

For a Bayesian problem, label the type whose expectation you are taking. Condition on that type, preserve any dependence in the prior, and distinguish information available before and after an observed action. For a mechanism, state whether the comparison is pointwise against other reports or averaged over beliefs. Then check participation and the budget separately from incentive compatibility.

For a coalition game, enumerate the relevant coalition constraints before guessing an allocation. For social choice, distinguish a ranking from a selected winner and identify the precise domain of admissible profiles. For evolutionary games, carry the quantifier over every mixed mutant through the proof. Checking only the vertices may miss an invading interior mixture.

## Reading a counterexample

A counterexample must satisfy the hypotheses that remain in force. To refute an implication from Nash equilibrium to efficiency, supply an actual equilibrium and an available Pareto improvement. To show why a hypothesis matters in Arrow's theorem, change exactly the stated assumption and verify the surviving axioms. To separate ESS from an observed convergence claim, specify the update rule rather than assigning a dynamic conclusion to a static payoff comparison.

Small examples in this course are mathematical witnesses. They do not establish universal behaviour by repetition. A finite table can certify all unilateral pure deviations because linearity supplies the missing mixed deviations; the reason for completeness is that theorem, not the small size of the table.
