
# Subgame-Perfect Equilibrium and Credible Continuations

A Nash equilibrium can contain an instruction that would be abandoned if its decision point were reached. Such an instruction can deter an earlier move even though it is not a sensible response afterward. Subgame perfection asks for equilibrium in every continuation that is itself a game. It does not require kindness, efficiency or a unique outcome; it requires consistency of incentives after histories, including those absent from the predicted path.

Read [Extensive Form and Backward Induction](extensive-form-backward-induction.md) first. The main theorems here concern finite deterministic perfect-information trees and pure contingent plans. This scope makes conditioning on every node unambiguous and avoids hidden assumptions about beliefs at information sets. More general one-shot principles need their own hypotheses.

## Subgames and restrictions

**Definition 9.1 (Subgame in a perfect-information tree).** The subgame after a history $h$ consists of that node and all its descendants, with the inherited players, actions and terminal payoffs. A strategy profile restricts to this subgame by retaining only instructions at its nodes. The whole original game is the subgame at the root.

**Definition 9.2 (Subgame-perfect equilibrium).** A pure strategy profile is subgame perfect if its restriction to every subgame is a Nash equilibrium of that subgame.

A subgame is evaluated as a continuation starting at its root, regardless of whether the original profile reaches it. Conditional incentives there are not multiplied by the probability of reaching it from the original root. That is the critical difference from a root-only Nash comparison.

Since the whole game is included, every subgame-perfect profile is a Nash equilibrium. The converse fails when root equilibrium tolerates nonoptimal behavior at unreached nodes. Subgame perfection strengthens the solution concept without changing the underlying action rules or payoff function.

For imperfect-information games, not every history begins a subgame. A proper subgame must start at a singleton information set and must not cut across later information sets. Otherwise the continuation would reveal information or omit indistinguishable possibilities. We name that restriction to prevent applying the present nodewise theorem to hidden-information trees. This chapter's proofs use perfect information throughout.

## Backward induction and subgame perfection

**Theorem 9.3 (Finite perfect-information characterization).** In a finite perfect-information game, the pure profiles obtained by selecting maximizing continuations in backward induction are exactly the pure subgame-perfect equilibria.

*Proof.* The continuation theorem in the preceding chapter proves that every backward-induction profile is an equilibrium after every node, hence is subgame perfect.

Conversely, take a subgame-perfect profile. Its restrictions to all immediate child subgames are subgame perfect. By induction on height, each child restriction can be generated by backward induction. At the parent, the controlling player's prescribed action must maximize their payoff among these fixed child continuations. Otherwise changing just that action, with all later instructions fixed, would improve payoff in the parent subgame, contradicting its Nash condition. Thus the parent choice is a permitted backward-induction maximizer. Induction to the root proves the converse, including ties. ∎

This theorem gives existence and a complete characterization in the stated pure finite setting. It does not imply that the profile or outcome is unique. Different maximizing choices at a tied node can affect earlier players' incentives. Nor does it imply a welfare maximum: each node is optimized for its owner.

## Credibility in an offer game

**Worked example 9.4 (A positive ultimatum offer).** A proposer chooses Low or High. Low divides a four-unit resource as $(3,1)$ if accepted; High divides it as $(1,3)$. After observing the offer, the responder chooses Accept or Reject, with rejection giving $(0,0)$.

The responder strictly prefers accepting at either node, because one and three are both positive. Given those continuations, the proposer chooses Low and receives three rather than one. The unique subgame-perfect pure profile is therefore Low with acceptance after either possible offer.

There is also a Nash profile with High, rejection after Low and acceptance after High. Given those responder instructions the proposer prefers one from High to zero from a rejected Low offer. Given High, the responder optimally accepts and the unreached rejection instruction does not affect root payoff. Yet rejection after Low would be abandoned if that node were reached. Subgame perfection excludes the noncredible threat without assuming the responder has any preference for equal division.

The conclusion depends on the stated utility numbers. If rejection carries a benefit, if unequal outcomes create disutility, or if the interaction repeats, accepting a positive monetary amount need not maximize the responder's actual utility. A theorem about the displayed payoff model is not an empirical assertion that every real responder behaves this way.

## One-shot deviations at every history

**Definition 9.5 (One-shot deviation).** At a node $h$ controlled by player $i$, a one-shot deviation changes only the action prescribed at $h$ and follows the original profile at all later nodes. Its profitability is evaluated in the subgame starting at $h$.

This is not merely a change to one entire strategic-form action: such an action is already a complete plan. Nor is it a deviation evaluated only from the original root. A node reached with probability zero under the profile still has its own conditional comparison.

**Theorem 9.6 (Finite one-shot deviation principle).** A pure profile in a finite deterministic perfect-information game is subgame perfect if and only if no player has a profitable one-shot deviation at any node they control.

*Proof.* Necessity follows because a one-shot deviation is an available unilateral deviation in its subgame.

For sufficiency, induct on subtree height. Terminal subtrees are equilibria. Assume every proper child restriction is an equilibrium. The absence of a profitable one-shot deviation at the current root means its owner chooses a child maximizing their own payoff under the prescribed child continuations. A different player cannot change that root choice and cannot improve inside the selected child by the induction hypothesis.

The root owner might change both the root action and later instructions. But in any child, its equilibrium restriction bounds what that owner can gain by changing their later instructions while others keep theirs. The current root action already maximizes those continuation payoffs. Thus no complete-plan deviation improves either. The restriction is Nash at the current root, and induction establishes equilibrium in every subgame. ∎

The theorem is useful because it replaces arbitrary complete-plan deviations with local comparisons, while retaining all histories. Finiteness is not decorative: the proof needs a base case and induction through a bounded remaining horizon.

In finite perfect-information games allowing behavioral randomization, analogous expected-payoff reasoning checks supported actions at each node; a full extension requires explicitly defining those local randomizations. We do not silently transfer this pure-plan theorem to arbitrary imperfect-information information sets or infinite histories.

## Off-path choices and welfare

**Worked example 9.7 (A trust continuation with an inefficient early stop).** Player one chooses Stop for $(2,1)$ or Go. After Go, player two chooses Stop for $(1,3)$ or Continue for $(4,2)$. At the later node player two prefers three to two and stops. Player one consequently prefers the initial Stop payoff two to the Go continuation payoff one.

The unique subgame-perfect outcome is therefore $(2,1)$, even though the final Continue outcome $(4,2)$ would make both players better off. The latter is not stable at player two's actual decision node. Subgame perfection provides no commitment device that forces player two to take the jointly better action.

A contract changing player two's payoff or a binding commitment could alter the conclusion, but those would be new rules. Simply declaring the efficient path desirable does not make it sequentially rational in the original game. This is the same distinction between welfare and incentives encountered in strategic form, now applied after a history.

**Worked example 9.8 (A zero-payoff tie creates two continuations).** Modify the offer game so Low gives $(4,0)$ if accepted, High gives $(1,3)$, and rejection still gives zero to both. The responder is now indifferent between accepting and rejecting Low, while accepting High is strictly better than rejecting it.

If Low is accepted, the proposer chooses Low and obtains four. If Low is rejected, the proposer chooses High and obtains one. Both complete profiles are subgame perfect: acceptance and rejection at the Low node are both optimal for a responder receiving zero either way. Hence a superficially tiny payoff change introduces equilibrium multiplicity.

A refinement favoring acceptance, rejecting empty offers or perturbing the zero tie would select among these possibilities only after its rule is stated. Subgame perfection itself allows all maximizing choices. The example shows why numerical ties and omitted utility components can matter greatly for predicted bargaining outcomes.

## Why the finite scope matters

Consider a one-player infinite tree. At each date the player may Quit for payoff zero or Wait. Waiting forever gives payoff one. The plan Quit at every node has no profitable one-shot deviation: waiting once and then following the plan still leads to quitting for zero. But deviating to Wait forever improves payoff to one.

This is not a counterexample to the theorem, because the horizon is infinite and payoff at the infinite path is not approached continuously by long finite prefixes. It explains why an infinite-horizon one-shot principle needs conditions such as suitable discounting and continuity at infinity. Those conditions must be proved or invoked for the actual model, rather than assumed from the finite result.

Likewise, a finite set of physical states with a loop can create infinitely many histories. Calling the state space small does not recover the bounded-horizon induction. Repeated-game results in the next chapter introduce different strategy spaces and payoff aggregation.

## A practical comparison procedure

To analyze a finite perfect-information tree, first list all continuation games and identify their owners and exact terminal payoffs. Work backward to find maximizing actions, retaining ties. Then report a complete profile and its induced outcome. To check an arbitrary candidate, inspect one-shot deviations at every controlled node, not just those on the candidate path.

If a candidate is Nash but not subgame perfect, identify a specific history and a profitable local deviation there. This provides a clear witness of the failed continuation condition. Merely calling a threat implausible is less precise than displaying the payoff comparison that would make its owner abandon it.

If several profiles survive, report the multiplicity. If the question asks for every equilibrium, selecting one tie-breaking convention is insufficient. Conversely if only existence or one witness is needed, a declared maximizing selection is legitimate and need not enumerate all combinations.

The solution concept also depends on the permitted deviation class. A Nash deviation changes one player's plan, whereas a coalition can coordinate multiple plans. Subgame perfection imposes the unilateral condition after every subgame but does not prohibit every coordinated improvement. It also does not introduce beliefs, trembles or equilibrium selection beyond its definition.

These limitations are strengths of a precise model. They locate exactly what has been established: sequential credibility under finite perfect-information unilateral optimization. Behavioral prediction, fairness, coalition stability and infinite-horizon persistence require additional arguments. The repeated and Bayesian chapters broaden the setting while keeping those distinctions visible.

## Local continuation values as a certificate

For a proposed pure profile in the present finite setting, each node has a well-defined terminal continuation under that profile. Store its full payoff vector. At a node owned by i, a one-shot comparison evaluates every child using these prescribed continuation vectors and checks that the chosen child's i-coordinate is maximal.

This gives a concise mathematical certificate. The tree and terminal data determine all stored vectors, and the local inequalities determine whether the profile passes the one-shot criterion. Because the theorem handles complete-plan deviations, these local comparisons certify more than just the absence of an immediate impulsive improvement.

The stored values must correspond to the same full profile throughout. Combining one child's value from one tie selection with another child's value from a different, incompatible strategy can produce an apparent certificate for no actual plan. A complete strategy specifies how all shared downstream contingencies are handled.

In a genuine tree distinct child subtrees have no common history nodes, so independently chosen child profiles can be assembled. If a compressed graph merges states reached by different histories, the compression must preserve the relevant player information and strategy constraints. Otherwise a collection of statewise best actions may not represent a feasible history-dependent strategy.

Payoff uncertainty is also local. If every prescribed comparison has a positive margin, small enough changes preserve that particular profile's optimal choices. But an exact tie may be selected differently after an arbitrarily small perturbation, with consequences propagating backward. A statement that a subgame-perfect outcome is robust therefore needs margins at all strategically relevant continuations, not just at the initial node.

Finally, subgame perfection is not an instruction to punish deviations. A punishment is credible only when the punishing continuation is itself an equilibrium after the relevant history. The offer examples show an unreached rejection can fail that requirement. Repeated interaction can make different punishment schemes credible, but those results depend on additional payoff aggregation and monitoring assumptions introduced in the repeated-game chapter.

## Summary

Subgame perfection imposes Nash equilibrium in every continuation, including unreached ones. In finite perfect-information games it coincides with backward induction, and absence of profitable conditional one-shot deviations is equivalent. Credibility does not imply efficiency or uniqueness; ties can matter, and infinite horizons or hidden information require additional hypotheses.
