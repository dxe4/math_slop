
# Preface and Reader Guide {#section-1-preface-and-reader-guide}

The entry level is L4. Preparation is the L3 Information Theory book, finite fields and finite-field linear algebra in Computational Algebra. Field arithmetic is exact; a prime-power alphabet is not silently interpreted as integers modulo a composite number.

Distance theorems control all error patterns in a stated radius. A maximum-likelihood decoder instead uses a stated stochastic channel. Belief propagation is exact on a tree and requires separate analysis on loopy graphs. These are different claims, and each chapter states the applicable one.

The opening three chapters rebuild Euler’s original algebraic-coding source while preserving it. New exercises use separate stable identities. Later chapters introduce polynomial, graph and geometric tools at their point of use. The algebraic-geometry chapter names its extra preparation and proves the coding deductions from the imported curve theorem rather than assuming an unprepared geometry course.

The book has twelve chapters.

## Reading routes and proof scope {#section-11-reading-routes-and-proof-scope}

Chapters 1–3 establish distance, linear structure and the Hamming/Golay constructions. Chapters 4–7 develop cyclic, BCH and Reed–Solomon codes and prove explicit algebraic decoding procedures. Chapters 8–9 supply size bounds, existence bounds and the character proof of MacWilliams’ identities. Chapters 10–12 treat finite-state, sparse-graph and geometric constructions.

The binary Golay code is constructed from a displayed eleven-point incidence identity, and its distance is proved by excluding possible weight-four words. BCH decoding is proved through Hankel rank, a locator system, position roots and magnitude equations; the faster Berlekamp–Massey and Euclidean implementations are named continuations rather than omitted algorithms claimed as proved.

Convolutional decoding proves the finite-trellis optimum for declared start/end states. Sum-product proves exact marginals on trees; its loopy-graph use and fixed-depth ensemble density limit have narrower claims. The final chapter explicitly imports smooth-curve Riemann–Roch, principal-divisor degree, smooth-cubic genus and the global residue theorem, then proves evaluation dimensions, designed distances and residue duality from them. A reader without divisor theory can first complete the projective-line and explicit elliptic calculations while treating those statements as named geometric inputs.

Every chapter has three worked examples and ten stable-ID exercises with two revealed hints and full solutions. The original finite-algebra chapter and the L3 linear-code first look retain their sources and identities.
