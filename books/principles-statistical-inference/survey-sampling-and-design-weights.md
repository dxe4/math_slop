
# Survey Sampling and Design Weights {#section-1-survey-sampling-and-design-weights}

<a id="section-1-level-and-prerequisites"></a>
## Level and prerequisites {#section-3-level-and-prerequisites}

This is a chapter of the L3 inference route. Preparation is [Probability: Models, Bounds, and Proofs](#/books/probability-models-proofs), especially its [Gaussian-vector chapter](../probability-models-proofs/gaussian-vectors-and-multivariate-normal.md), and [Lucent linear algebra](#/books/lucent-linear-algebra). The L3 core is finite-population probability with explicitly positive inclusion probabilities and known sampling laws. Response-model transport and replicate-weight approximation require the additional assumptions stated beside them. General asymptotic survey theory and operational nonresponse modelling are optional advanced continuations; the finite unbiasedness proofs do not claim those results.


A survey sample is generated by a selection design, not merely by an iid population distribution. Known inclusion probabilities can identify finite-population totals and means even when units have unequal chances of selection. Ignoring those probabilities changes the target toward the distribution of sampled units.

This chapter proves Horvitz–Thompson unbiasedness and its design variance, derives simple and stratified random-sampling formulas, distinguishes base, calibration, nonresponse, and analysis weights, and treats clustering, multistage designs, effective sample size, replicate variance, and frame error. The population values remain fixed; probability comes first from the sample-selection mechanism.

<a id="section-10-1-a-sampling-design-is-a-law-on-subsets"></a>
## 1. A sampling design is a law on subsets {#section-12-1-a-sampling-design-is-a-law-on-subsets}

Let a finite population be $U=\{1,\dots,N\}$ with fixed values $y_i$. A probability sampling design assigns probability $p(s)$ to each possible sample $s\subseteq U$.

Define inclusion indicators

$I_i=1_{i\in s}$,

first-order inclusion probabilities

$\pi _i=P_d(I_i=1)$,

and joint inclusion probabilities

$\pi_{ij}=P_d(I_i=1,I_j=1)$.

The subscript $d$ denotes repetition of the sample design. A design is not fully described by sample size and marginal $\pi _i$; joint probabilities determine covariance and variance estimation.

The finite-population total and mean are

$t_y=\sum _{i=1}^N y_i$, $\bar{Y}_U=t_y/N$.

They are fixed features of the listed population. A superpopulation model can be added for prediction beyond this frame, but it is not needed for the first design-unbiased results.

<a id="section-34-2-horvitz-thompson-corrects-unequal-selection"></a>
## 2. Horvitz–Thompson corrects unequal selection {#section-36-2-horvitz-thompson-corrects-unequal-selection}

Assume every $\pi _i>0$. The Horvitz–Thompson estimator of the total is

$\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum _{i\in s}y_i/\pi _i=\sum _i I_i y_i/\pi _i$.

**Theorem 16.1.** $\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}$ is design-unbiased for $t_y$.

**Proof.** Since $E_d[I_i]=\pi _i$,

$E_d[\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}]=\sum _i E_d[I_i]y_i/\pi _i=\sum _i y_i=t_y$. ∎

If $N$ is known, $\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}/N$ is unbiased for the population mean. Positivity is essential: a unit with $\pi _i=0$ never appears, so its arbitrary value cannot be recovered from the design.

<a id="section-48-a-small-unequal-probability-example"></a>
### A small unequal-probability example {#section-50-a-small-unequal-probability-example}

<a id="section-50-worked-example-1-compute-a-realised-inverse-probability"></a>
### Worked example 1: compute a realised inverse-probability total {#section-52-worked-example-1-compute-a-realised-inverse-probability}

Suppose three units have values $(10,20,30)$ and inclusion probabilities $(1,1/2,1/2)$. A realised sample contains units 1 and 3. The estimate is

$10/1+30/(1/2)=70$.

The true total is $60$; one realised estimate need not equal it. Unbiasedness concerns its average across the stated sampling design.

<a id="section-58-3-exact-design-variance-uses-joint-inclusion"></a>
## 3. Exact design variance uses joint inclusion {#section-60-3-exact-design-variance-uses-joint-inclusion}

Let

$\Delta_{ij}=\pi_{ij}-\pi _i\pi _j$,

with $\pi_{ii}=\pi _i$. Then

$\operatorname{Var}_d(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})=\sum _i\sum _j \Delta_{ij} (y_i/\pi _i)(y_j/\pi _j)$.

**Derivation.** Write $\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum _i I_i a_i$ with $a_i=y_i/\pi _i$. Bilinearity of covariance gives

$\operatorname{Var}(\sum _iI_i a_i)=\sum _i\sum _j \operatorname{Cov}(I_i,I_j)a_ia_j$.

But $\operatorname{Cov}(I_i,I_j)=E[I_iI_j]-E[I_i]E[I_j]=\Delta_{ij}$. ∎

When every relevant $\pi_{ij}>0$, an unbiased variance estimator is

$\hat{V}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum _{i\in s}\sum _{j\in s} [\Delta_{ij}/\pi_{ij}](y_i/\pi _i)(y_j/\pi _j)$.

Its expectation replaces $I_iI_j/\pi_{ij}$ by one. If some pairs can never be sampled together, this estimator is unavailable and the design needs a specialised variance argument or conservative bound.

<a id="section-80-4-fixed-size-designs-have-a-difference-form"></a>
## 4. Fixed-size designs have a difference form {#section-82-4-fixed-size-designs-have-a-difference-form}

In a fixed-size design, $\sum _iI_i=n$ always. This implies $\sum _j\Delta_{ij}=0$ for every $i$. Algebra then rewrites the variance as the Sen–Yates–Grundy form

$\operatorname{Var}_d(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})=-1/2 \sum _i\sum _j \Delta_{ij}(y_i/\pi _i-y_j/\pi _j)^{2}$.

The diagonal terms vanish. In many without-replacement designs $\Delta_{ij}\le 0$ for $i\ne j$, making nonnegativity visible. The form emphasizes that variance arises from contrasts between weighted population values.

<a id="section-88-5-simple-random-sampling-recovers-familiar-formulas"></a>
## 5. Simple random sampling recovers familiar formulas {#section-90-5-simple-random-sampling-recovers-familiar-formulas}

For integers $N\ge2$ and $1\le n\le N$, under simple random sampling without replacement of $n$ units,

$\pi _i=n/N$, $\pi_{ij}=n(n-1)/[N(N-1)]$ for $i\ne j$.

The Horvitz–Thompson mean reduces to the ordinary sample mean $\bar{y}_s$. If

$S_y^{2}=(N-1)^{-1}\sum _i(y_i-\bar{Y}_U)^{2}$,

then

$\operatorname{Var}_d(\bar{y}_s)=(1-n/N)S_y^{2}/n$.

The factor $1-n/N$ is the finite-population correction. For $n\ge2$, the sample variance

\[s_y^{2}=(n-1)^{-1}\sum _{i\in s}(y_i-\bar{y}_s)^{2}\]

is design-unbiased for $S_y^{2}$, so $(1-n/N)s_y^{2}/n$ estimates the mean variance.

Sampling half a small population without replacement contains more information about its mean than $n$ independent draws from an effectively infinite population. Omitting the correction is conservative here but can be materially wasteful.

<a id="section-110-6-stratification-samples-known-subpopulations-separatel"></a>
## 6. Stratification samples known subpopulations separately {#section-112-6-stratification-samples-known-subpopulations-separatel}

Partition the population into strata $h=1,\dots,H$ with sizes $N_h\ge2$. Draw an independent simple random sample of integer size $1\le n_h\le N_h$ within each. Let $W_h=N_h/N$ and $\bar{y}_h$ be the stratum sample mean. Then

\[\bar{y}_{\mathrm{st}}=\sum _h W_h\bar{y}_h\]

is unbiased for $\bar{Y}_U$, with variance

$\operatorname{Var}_d(\bar{y}_{\mathrm{st}})=\sum _h W_h^{2}(1-n_h/N_h)S_h^{2}/n_h$.

Stratification guarantees representation of declared groups and can improve precision when within-stratum variation is small.

Ignoring finite-population corrections and assuming equal unit costs, minimising approximate variance subject to $\sum _hn_h=n$ gives Neyman allocation

$n_h\propto N_hS_h$.

Unknown $S_h$ must be anticipated from prior data or a pilot. Precision-optimal allocation may conflict with minimum subgroup sample sizes needed for separate reporting.

<a id="section-128-7-cluster-and-multistage-sampling-change-the-variance-u"></a>
## 7. Cluster and multistage sampling change the variance unit {#section-130-7-cluster-and-multistage-sampling-change-the-variance-u}

Surveys often sample primary units such as regions or schools, then households, then individuals. Units within a selected cluster can be similar, so the number of independently selected primary units can matter more than the raw respondent count.

At each stage, overall inclusion probability is built from conditional selection probabilities. Variance estimation must reflect every random stage. Treating all final respondents as a simple random sample destroys covariance induced by shared clusters.

Probability proportional to size can stabilise weights when cluster totals scale with a known size measure, but size errors and within-cluster subsampling still require recorded probabilities.

<a id="section-136-8-base-weights-normalised-weights-and-the-h-jek-ratio"></a>
## 8. Base weights, normalised weights, and the Hájek ratio {#section-138-8-base-weights-normalised-weights-and-the-h-jek-ratio}

The base design weight is

$d_i=1/\pi _i$.

For a known total, Horvitz–Thompson uses these weights without normalising. For a realised nonempty sample, a common mean estimator is the Hájek ratio

$\bar{y}_H=\sum _{i\in s}d_i y_i / \sum _{i\in s}d_i$.

It replaces known $N$ by its HT estimate $\sum d_i$. It is generally not exactly design-unbiased because it is a ratio of random quantities, but it can be more stable and is consistent under regular design sequences.

Multiplying every weight by the same constant leaves a normalised weighted mean unchanged but changes a total. Software options that interpret weights as frequency, precision, probability, or analytic weights are not interchangeable.

<a id="section-150-9-calibration-uses-known-auxiliary-totals"></a>
## 9. Calibration uses known auxiliary totals {#section-152-9-calibration-uses-known-auxiliary-totals}

Suppose population totals $t_x=\sum _Ux_i$ are known for auxiliary variables. Calibration chooses adjusted weights $w_i$ near base weights while satisfying

$\sum _{i\in s}w_i x_i=t_x$.

The resulting estimate $\sum _sw_i y_i$ exactly reproduces auxiliary totals and can reduce variance when $x$ predicts $y$. Poststratification and raking are calibration methods with different constraints and distance criteria.

Calibration does not guarantee removal of selection bias merely because margins match. Bias can remain within cells, through omitted interactions, frame exclusions, response error, or variables unrelated to the calibrated totals. Extreme calibrated weights can inflate variance and expose weak overlap.

<a id="section-160-10-unequal-weights-reduce-effective-information"></a>
## 10. Unequal weights reduce effective information {#section-162-10-unequal-weights-reduce-effective-information}

A common descriptive approximation for independent weighted observations is Kish's effective sample size

$n_{\mathrm{eff}}=(\sum _iw_i)^{2}/\sum _iw_i^{2}$.

Cauchy–Schwarz gives $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\le n$, with equality for equal weights. This number describes weight dispersion only. It omits clustering, stratification, finite-population corrections, outcome–weight association, and model-based gains from auxiliary information.

The design effect is a variance ratio relative to a named simple-random-sampling benchmark. It is estimand- and outcome-specific; there is no single permanent design effect for a dataset.

<a id="section-170-11-nonresponse-is-a-second-selection-process"></a>
## 11. Nonresponse is a second selection process {#section-172-11-nonresponse-is-a-second-selection-process}

Let $R_i$ indicate response after sampling, with response probability $r_i$ conditional on recorded design information. If response is independent of $y_i$ given that information and $r_i>0$, inverse response adjustment suggests weight

$1/(\pi _i r_i)$.

Unknown $r_i$ must be estimated, which adds model uncertainty. If response depends on the unobserved outcome after conditioning on recorded variables, weighting by an observed-data response model need not remove bias. Sensitivity analysis is then required.

Base weights correct the sampling design they encode. They do not automatically correct nonresponse, undercoverage, measurement error, duplicated frame entries, or respondent substitution.

<a id="section-180-12-replicate-weights-approximate-the-full-design-variat"></a>
## 12. Replicate weights approximate the full design variation {#section-182-12-replicate-weights-approximate-the-full-design-variat}

Replication methods repeatedly alter weights according to the design—by deleting groups, forming balanced half samples, or rescaling bootstrap selections—and recompute the complete estimator. Variation across replicates estimates sampling variance while propagating nonlinear calibration and analysis steps.

Replicate weights are not generic iid bootstrap weights. Their construction must match strata, primary units, finite-population treatment, and calibration. Analysts must use the supplied formula and replicate factor rather than taking an unqualified sample variance of replicate estimates.

<a id="section-186-13-frame-and-measurement-errors-lie-outside-design-unbi"></a>
## 13. Frame and measurement errors lie outside design unbiasedness {#section-188-13-frame-and-measurement-errors-lie-outside-design-unbi}

Design-unbiasedness targets the values attached to units in the sampling frame under the recorded selection law. If the frame omits unhoused people, duplicates businesses, uses outdated addresses, or links the wrong measurements, the target or observation map differs from the intended population.

Increasing the within-frame sample size shrinks design variance but not systematic undercoverage. A defensible report separates target population, frame population, eligible units, sampled units, respondents, and analysed records.

## 16. Development: enumerate a design before interpreting its weights {#section-742-16-development-enumerate-a-design-before-interpreting-i}

<a id="section-742-worked-example-2-compare-three-designs-with-the-same-ba"></a>
### Worked example 2: compare three designs with the same base weights {#section-744-worked-example-2-compare-three-designs-with-the-same-ba}

Take the finite population $U=\{1,2,3\}$, with fixed values $(y_1,y_2,y_3)=(1,2,3)$ and total six. The sample always has size two, but its law is unequal: select $\{1,2\}$ with probability $1/2$, $\{1,3\}$ with probability $1/3$, and $\{2,3\}$ with probability $1/6$. These probabilities sum to one and specify every possible sample. They are design probabilities, not fitted probabilities that a population value is large.

Adding the probabilities of samples containing each unit gives $\pi_1=5/6$, $\pi_2=2/3$ and $\pi_3=1/2$. The joint probabilities for distinct pairs are $\pi_{12}=1/2$, $\pi_{13}=1/3$, $\pi_{23}=1/6$. The weighted population values $a_i=y_i/\pi_i$ are $6/5,3,6$. Every estimate and its probability can now be written down.

| Sample $s$ | Design probability $p(s)$ | HT total $\widehat t(s)$ | Squared error from six | Unbiased difference-form variance estimate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\{1,2\}$ | $1/2$ | $21/5$ | $81/25$ | $9/25$ |
| $\{1,3\}$ | $1/3$ | $36/5$ | $36/25$ | $144/25$ |
| $\{2,3\}$ | $1/6$ | $9$ | $9$ | $9$ |

The expectation is $(1/2)(21/5)+(1/3)(36/5)+(1/6)9=6$. The design variance is the expectation of the squared error,
$$\frac12\frac{81}{25}+\frac13\frac{36}{25}+\frac16\,9=\frac{18}{5}.$$
The true finite-population total does not change across these repetitions. What changes is which pair the selection device reveals. This is why an estimate can exceed six in a particular sample without contradicting unbiasedness.

We can verify the variance from the joint-inclusion formula independently. The off-diagonal covariances are $\Delta_{12}=-1/18$, $\Delta_{13}=-1/12$ and $\Delta_{23}=-1/6$. Since sample size is fixed, the difference form gives one term for each unordered pair:
$$V_d(\widehat t)
 =\frac1{18}(6/5-3)^2+\frac1{12}(6/5-6)^2
       +\frac16(3-6)^2
 =\frac9{50}+\frac{48}{25}+\frac32=\frac{18}{5}.$$
The agreement is a check of two derivations of the same probability law, not evidence from a simulated survey.

For an actually selected pair, divide that pair's contribution by its inclusion probability. Thus
$$\widehat V_{\mathrm{diff}}(s)
 =-\sum_{i<j:\,i,j\in s}\frac{\Delta_{ij}}{\pi_{ij}}(a_i-a_j)^2.$$
This produces the final table column. Averaging that column over the three samples gives $(1/2)(9/25)+(1/3)(144/25)+(1/6)9=18/5$. A single variance estimate need not equal the design variance, just as a single total estimate need not equal the population total. In this design every pair can be selected and the off-diagonal covariances are negative, so this particular variance estimator is available and nonnegative.

The inverse marginal weights answer only the expectation question. To see what they leave unspecified, use four units with values $(0,0,2,2)$ and sample size two. Under design A, every pair has probability $1/6$. Under design B, choose $\{1,2\}$ or $\{3,4\}$, each with probability $1/2$. Under design C, choose one unit uniformly from $\{1,2\}$ and one independently from $\{3,4\}$. All three designs have $\pi_i=1/2$ for every unit and use the same base weight two.

Their HT totals behave very differently. Under A, the total is zero once, eight once and four on the other four pairs, giving variance $(16+16)/6=16/3$. Under B, it is zero or eight with equal probability, giving variance sixteen. Under C, it is always four, giving variance zero. All are unbiased for four. The first design spreads across all pairs; the second selects homogeneous clusters; the third forces representation from each value group. Equal weights do not make these designs interchangeable.

Designs B and C have some zero joint probabilities. An analyst cannot divide by those probabilities to estimate the corresponding covariance terms from observed pairs. A specialized argument can still exploit a declared design or additional structure, but plugging zero into the pairwise unbiased-variance formula is invalid. Knowing the marginal weights is therefore insufficient to reconstruct a variance estimator. Preserve strata, selection units and pairwise information, or use a supplied replicate system whose construction represents them.

<a id="section-776-17-development-fixed-size-identities-finite-populations"></a>
## 17. Development: fixed-size identities, finite populations and allocation {#section-778-17-development-fixed-size-identities-finite-populations}

The difference form follows from a structural identity rather than a lucky negative-covariance pattern. If $\sum_jI_j=n$ on every sample, then
$$\sum_j\Delta_{ij}
 =\operatorname{Cov}_d\!\left(I_i,\sum_jI_j\right)
 =\operatorname{Cov}_d(I_i,n)=0.$$
Let $a_i=y_i/\pi_i$. Expanding squared differences gives
$$-\frac12\sum_{i,j}\Delta_{ij}(a_i-a_j)^2
 =-\frac12\sum_{i,j}\Delta_{ij}a_i^2
  -\frac12\sum_{i,j}\Delta_{ij}a_j^2
  +\sum_{i,j}\Delta_{ij}a_ia_j.$$
The first two sums vanish by the zero row and column sums. What remains is the covariance expression for HT variance. Fixed sample size establishes the algebra; it does not assert that every off-diagonal $\Delta_{ij}$ is nonpositive. Nonnegativity of the whole quadratic form follows from its being a variance even for a design without that convenient sign pattern.

For simple random sampling, a centred derivation reveals the finite-population correction. Suppose $N\ge2$, $1\le n\le N$, and put $z_i=y_i-\bar Y_U$, so $\sum_i z_i=0$. Then $\sum_{i\ne j}z_iz_j=-\sum_i z_i^2$. Since $\pi=n/N$ and $\pi_{ij}=n(n-1)/[N(N-1)]$ for distinct units,
$$\operatorname{Var}_d(\bar y_s)
 =\frac{\pi-\pi_{ij}}{n^2}\sum_i z_i^2
 =\frac{N-n}{Nn}S_y^2.$$
Selecting without replacement makes inclusion indicators negatively correlated. At a census, those indicators stop varying, so variance is zero. The target values still can vary greatly across people; outcome variability and uncertainty about a fully observed finite mean are different quantities.

For $n\ge2$, sample-variance unbiasedness can also be derived by pairs. The identity
$$s_y^2=\frac1{n(n-1)}
   \sum_{i<j:\,i,j\in s}(y_i-y_j)^2$$
comes from expanding deviations about the sample mean. The corresponding population pair sum is $N(N-1)S_y^2$. Each pair is sampled with probability $n(n-1)/[N(N-1)]$, so the expectation of the displayed sample expression is $S_y^2$. A size-one sample still has a well-defined mean and design variance, but it cannot supply this sample variance with denominator $n-1$.

For stratification, assume each stratum has $N_h\ge2$, and choose independent SRS samples with $1\le n_h\le N_h$. Write $A_h=W_h^2S_h^2$. The exact mean variance is
$$\sum_h A_h/n_h-\sum_h A_h/N_h.$$
The second sum is constant as allocation changes. Thus, for positive anticipated $S_h$ and an interior continuous allocation with fixed total $n$, minimizing variance reduces to minimizing $\sum_h A_h/n_h$. Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$$\left(\sum_h\sqrt{A_h}\right)^2
 \le\left(\sum_h A_h/n_h\right)\left(\sum_h n_h\right).$$
Equality requires $n_h$ proportional to $\sqrt{A_h}=W_hS_h$, equivalently $N_hS_h$. The usual Neyman rule therefore also solves this unconstrained continuous problem with the finite-population terms retained; those terms affect the achieved variance but not the interior stationary allocation.

Actual sample sizes are integers and bounded by the stratum populations. A suggested allocation above $N_h$ must be capped, then the remaining budget reallocated among uncapped strata. Minimum sample sizes for domain reporting impose further constraints. If a stratum has zero anticipated variation, the unconstrained rule suggests zero, but a design that must estimate its mean or verify that anticipation still needs a declared sampling rule. None of these operational restrictions is solved by rounding proportional numbers independently.

The precision gain from increasing a stratum's integer sample size from $k$ to $k+1$ is $A_h/[k(k+1)]$, provided the increase is feasible. This exact difference helps compare the next unit after mandatory minima are allocated. With different unit costs $c_h>0$ and a continuous cost budget $\sum_hc_hn_h=C$, the corresponding interior calculation gives $n_h\propto W_hS_h/\sqrt{c_h}$. Cost and minimum-representation constraints express different objectives; a variance optimum alone is not a complete survey design.

<a id="section-811-18-development-calibration-and-the-factor-attached-to-a"></a>
## 18. Development: calibration and the factor attached to a replicate {#section-813-18-development-calibration-and-the-factor-attached-to-a}

<a id="section-813-worked-example-3-separate-calibration-from-outcome-iden"></a>
### Worked example 3: separate calibration from outcome identification {#section-815-worked-example-3-separate-calibration-from-outcome-iden}

Calibration should be read as a constrained estimator construction. Suppose $x_i$ is a column of auxiliary variables whose population total $t_x$ is known. For a selected sample, choose $q_i>0$ and minimize
$$\sum_{i\in s}\frac{(w_i-d_i)^2}{2d_iq_i}
 \quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{i\in s}w_ix_i=t_x,$$
where $d_i=1/\pi_i>0$. With
$$M_s=\sum_{i\in s}d_iq_ix_ix_i^\top$$
invertible, a Lagrange multiplier calculation gives
$$w_i=d_i(1+q_ix_i^\top\lambda),\qquad
 \lambda=M_s^{-1}(t_x-\widehat t_{x,\mathrm{HT}}).$$
The derivative with respect to each $w_i$ is zero at this solution, and the positive quadratic objective makes the feasible solution the unique minimum. Invertibility and feasibility are substantive conditions, not guarantees supplied by the notation.

Substitution gives the generalized regression identity
$$\widehat t_{\mathrm{cal}}
 =\widehat t_{y,\mathrm{HT}}
 +(t_x-\widehat t_{x,\mathrm{HT}})^\top\widehat B,\qquad
\widehat B=M_s^{-1}\sum_{i\in s}d_iq_ix_iy_i.$$
If every population value satisfies $y_i=x_i^\top B$ for one fixed coefficient vector, calibration reproduces the total exactly whenever the constraints can be satisfied. For general outcomes, $\widehat B$ is random and the product correction need not have expectation zero. Exact auxiliary balance therefore is not a theorem of exact outcome unbiasedness.

For a simple poststratification example, known cell sizes are three and one. Suppose one selected respondent represents each cell, with recorded outcomes two and ten. Weights three and one reproduce the two cell totals, giving estimated outcome total sixteen and mean four. Equal weights would give mean six. Neither numerical answer alone establishes the true finite-population mean: unseen outcomes within the larger cell may differ from its respondent's value. The known counts justify the balance equations; a model or design argument must justify transporting that respondent's outcome.

Quadratic calibration can produce negative weights. Imposing positive or bounded weights changes the optimisation and can make the constraints infeasible, particularly when a required cell has no sample member. Reporting that failure is more informative than silently omitting a constraint. Calibration variables, known totals, bounds and any trimming are part of the analysis record. The fact that a solver returned weights does not validate the frame or the response model.

Replication has its own equally explicit scale. For an SRS mean based on $n\ge2$, let $\bar y_{(-i)}$ be the mean after deleting observation $i$. Then
$$\bar y_{(-i)}-\bar y
 =\frac{\bar y-y_i}{n-1},\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^n(\bar y_{(-i)}-\bar y)^2=\frac{s_y^2}{n-1}.$$
Consequently the delete-one jackknife expression
$$\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_i(\bar y_{(-i)}-\bar y)^2
 =\frac{s_y^2}{n}$$
matches the usual variance estimate for an independent-sampling mean. For SRS without replacement from known $N$, multiplying this expression by $1-n/N$ gives the design-unbiased SRS mean-variance estimator. This identity is exact for this linear statistic and these designs; it does not establish a universal jackknife factor for arbitrary nonlinear surveys.

For recorded values $(1,2,6)$, $\bar y=3$, $s_y^2=7$, and the three delete-one means are $4,7/2,3/2$. Their squared deviations from three sum to $7/2$. Multiplication by $2/3$ gives $7/3=s_y^2/n$. If these are three units selected by SRS from six, the extra finite-population factor gives $7/6$. Simply taking the sample variance of the three replicate means would instead give $7/4$, a different answer. Replicate estimates are designed perturbations, not three independent observations from the original survey experiment.

For calibrated totals or nonlinear statistics, each replicate may require refitting the response adjustment, recalibrating weights and recomputing the entire statistic. Holding those steps fixed estimates a different variation. Supplied replicate systems also specify centering, scale factors and which primary units vary together. The elementary delete-one proof shows why that information is necessary; it does not authorize substituting its factor into balanced repeated replication or a multistage bootstrap.

Finally, selection and response are distinct stages. For a fixed population unit, let $r_i=P(R_i=1\mid I_i=1)$ under the joint sampling-and-response experiment. Then its observed inclusion probability is $\pi_ir_i$ by the product rule. That identity alone does not prove that an estimated response probability removes outcome-dependent nonresponse. Likewise a fully implemented replicate scheme can propagate declared uncertainty while leaving frame exclusions or a wrong response model untouched. A defensible variance report states the estimator, the target frame, the design stages and the mechanism being repeated.


<a id="section-852-a-sufficient-meaning-of-regularity-for-the-ratio"></a>
### A sufficient meaning of regularity for the ratio {#section-854-a-sufficient-meaning-of-regularity-for-the-ratio}

The consistency qualification in §8 can be made concrete. Consider a sequence of finite populations with sizes $N\to\infty$ and bounded population means $\mu_N=t_y/N$. Suppose the design-unbiased HT estimators satisfy $\operatorname{Var}_d(\widehat t_y)/N^2\to0$ and $\operatorname{Var}_d(\widehat N)/N^2\to0$, where $\widehat N=\sum_s1/\pi_i$. Chebyshev's inequality gives $(\widehat t_y-t_y)/N\to0$ and $\widehat N/N\to1$ in design probability. Therefore
$$\frac{\widehat t_y}{\widehat N}-\mu_N
 =\frac{(\widehat t_y-t_y)/N-\mu_N(\widehat N-N)/N}
            {\widehat N/N}\ \longrightarrow\ 0.$$
The denominator is positive with probability tending to one; define the ratio arbitrarily on any remaining zero-denominator event for this convergence statement. Bounded $\mu_N$ controls the second numerator term. These are sufficient conditions, not a conclusion for every sequence of unequal weights. Extreme weights or concentrated cluster selection can violate the required variance bounds even when the number of recorded units grows.

A singleton stratum is a separate finite convention: if its only unit is sampled with certainty, its total is observed and its design-variance contribution is zero. There is no need to define $S_h^2$ by dividing by $N_h-1=0$. For multiunit strata sampled in a census the contribution is also zero, while for a noncensus size-one sample the variance may be positive although its within-sample variance cannot be estimated by the usual formula. Certainty and lack of replication are different reasons for seeing only one unit.


<a id="section-863-outcome-specific-design-effects-and-two-stage-variance"></a>
### Outcome-specific design effects and two-stage variance {#section-865-outcome-specific-design-effects-and-two-stage-variance}

The equal-weight comparison also quantifies what a design effect measures. For $(0,0,2,2)$, the finite mean is one and $S_y^2=4/3$. Design A is SRS of two from four, with mean variance $(1-2/4)(4/3)/2=1/3$. Design B has HT mean zero or two with equal probability, so its variance is one and its design effect relative to A is three. Design C has mean one on every sample and design effect zero. Every realized sample nevertheless has two equal weights, giving Kish effective sample size two in all three designs.

Now change the outcome assignment to $(0,2,0,2)$ while keeping the designs. Both clusters used by design B contain a zero and a two, so its mean is always one and its variance is zero. The population value multiset and SRS benchmark are unchanged, but the relation of the outcome to clustering has changed. A design effect belongs to an outcome, estimand and benchmark together. A number based only on weight dispersion cannot encode that relation.

For a two-stage derivation, partition the population into clusters indexed by $g$, with fixed totals $t_g$. Select clusters with first-stage indicators $I_g$ and positive probabilities $\pi_g$. Conditional on selection, estimate each $t_g$ by $\widehat t_g$ using a second-stage design that is unbiased with variance $V_g$. Assume these second-stage selections are conditionally independent between selected clusters and their design laws depend only on the cluster, not on which other clusters were selected. The overall estimator is
$$\widehat T=\sum_g I_g\widehat t_g/\pi_g.$$
Its conditional expectation given the first-stage sample is $\sum_gI_gt_g/\pi_g$, so it is unbiased for $\sum_gt_g$. Total variance gives
$$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat T)
 =\operatorname{Var}_1\!\left(\sum_g I_gt_g/\pi_g\right)
       +\sum_g V_g/\pi_g.$$
The second term follows by averaging $\sum_gI_gV_g/\pi_g^2$ over first-stage selection. Both stages contribute unless the corresponding quantities are observed with certainty.

For two final units in the same cluster, joint inclusion involves the cluster's first-stage probability and their within-cluster joint probability. For units in different clusters, it involves the first-stage cluster-pair probability and both within-cluster marginal probabilities under these conditional-independence assumptions. Multiplying stagewise marginal probabilities produces a final unit's base weight, but cannot recover either kind of joint probability by itself. This is the algebra behind the instruction to preserve primary selection units when estimating variance.

A variable-size design provides another boundary. If units are selected independently with probabilities $\pi_i$, all off-diagonal inclusion covariances vanish, and
$$\operatorname{Var}_d(\widehat t_{\mathrm{HT}})
 =\sum_i\frac{1-\pi_i}{\pi_i}y_i^2.$$
The covariance row sums generally are not zero, because sample size is random; the fixed-size difference proof cannot be transplanted. For three independent inclusion decisions each with probability one-half, expected sample size is $3/2$ and the empty sample has probability $1/8$. The HT total is well defined as zero on that event and remains unbiased across the full design, but a normalised weighted mean has zero denominator there. Conditioning away empty samples changes the selection law and requires recalculating its probabilities.


A certainty unit has $\pi_i=1$, hence $I_i=1$ on every design repetition and $\pi_{ij}=\pi_j$ for every other unit. Thus $\Delta_{ij}=0$ across its entire covariance row. Its recorded value contributes to the total but contributes no sampling variation. This is different from a rare unit with a very large inverse weight, whose selection can dominate variance. It is also different from a certainty-selected unit whose outcome is subsequently missing: selection certainty does not make response certain. If that outcome is unobserved, its response mechanism or an explicit bound must enter the analysis, rather than treating a base weight of one as complete information.

These examples suggest what to retain with a survey result: the population and target, a probability law or design identifier, the weights and their stage definitions, the estimator formula, and the variance system with its scale factors. An array of final weights without this information is an incomplete description of the experiment, even when it reproduces a familiar weighted mean.

## Summary {#section-368-summary}

<a id="section-737-15-summary-and-forward-route"></a>
Probability sampling identifies finite-population totals through known inclusion probabilities. Horvitz–Thompson weighting is exactly unbiased, and joint inclusion probabilities determine variance. Stratification, clustering, stages, calibration, and replicate weights must match the actual design. Nonresponse and frame error add selection mechanisms that base weights do not solve. The next chapter treats missing-data identification and sensitivity directly.


<a id="section-740-16-development-enumerate-a-design-before-interpreting-i"></a>

<a id="section-964-summary"></a>
A survey design is a joint probability law on samples, not merely a list of weights. Positive inclusion permits unbiased Horvitz–Thompson estimation, while joint inclusion controls design variance. Stratification, clustering, normalised ratios, calibration and response weighting answer distinct questions. Reproducing auxiliary totals or a weight-only effective size cannot identify unseen outcomes or recover a subpopulation absent from the frame.

The main route continues to [Missing Data, Identification, and Sensitivity](missing-data-identification-and-sensitivity.md).

## Solutions {#section-996-solutions}

<a id="section-941-20-solutions-to-practice-extensions"></a>
<details>

<a id="section-944-si-19-e21-same-weights-different-design-solution"></a>
### SI-19-E21. Same weights, different design — solution {#section-946-si-19-e21-same-weights-different-design-solution}

In A each unit belongs to three of six equally likely pairs, so $\pi_i=1/2$. In B it belongs to one of two equally likely clusters, and in C it is selected with probability one-half within its own cell; again $\pi_i=1/2$. Thus every observed value receives weight two. In A the HT total takes values zero and eight with probabilities $1/6$ each and four with probability $4/6$, giving expectation four and variance $16/3$. In B the totals are zero and eight with probabilities one-half, giving expectation four and variance sixteen. In C every sample has one zero and one two, so the total is always four and variance zero. The marginal weights agree, but the pairwise inclusion probabilities differ. A method whose only design input is those four marginal weights receives identical inputs for designs with distinct sampling covariance, so it cannot recover all three variances correctly.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

<a id="section-952-si-19-e22-calibrate-before-naming-the-target-solution"></a>
### SI-19-E22. Calibrate before naming the target — solution {#section-954-si-19-e22-calibrate-before-naming-the-target-solution}

Weights three and one give $(3\cdot2+1\cdot10)/4=4$. One compatible population has size-three cell values $(2,2,2)$ and size-one cell value ten, with true mean four. Another has size-three values $(2,0,0)$, keeping the observed respondent as the unit with value two, and size-one value ten, with true mean three. Both have the same recorded sample and auxiliary counts. Calibration reproduces cell sizes and hence gives the same estimated mean four in both. It does not identify unseen within-cell outcomes from this realized record. A probability-sampling argument for the within-cell respondent, a credible response-exchangeability restriction, or a stated outcome model could support transport; none is supplied by the balance equations alone.

</details>
