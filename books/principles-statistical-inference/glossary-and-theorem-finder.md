
# Glossary and Theorem Finder

This index uses stable chapter numbers and identifiers rather than format-specific pages. Definitions are compact reminders, not substitutes for theorem hypotheses.

## Core glossary

**Action.** A possible output of a decision rule, such as an estimate, classification, interval, or intervention. Actions acquire meaning through a declared loss. Ch. 3.

**Admissible rule.** A rule not dominated everywhere in the parameter space under the stated loss. Admissibility is relative to the action space and procedure class. Ch. S1.

**Ancillary statistic.** A statistic whose distribution does not depend on the parameter in the declared model. Ancillarity alone does not prove independence from a sufficient statistic. Ch. 5.

**Asymptotic normality.** Convergence in distribution of a centred and scaled statistic to a normal law. It does not assert finite-sample normality or uniform accuracy. Ch. 12.

**Bayes factor.** A ratio of marginal likelihoods under two fully specified models. It depends on prior mass or density over all model-specific parameters. Ch. 18.

**Bayes rule.** A decision rule minimising posterior expected loss, equivalently Bayes risk under suitable integrability. Under squared-error loss and a finite posterior second moment, the posterior mean is the unique Bayes action; other losses generally select other summaries, and the same action can be optimal under more than one loss. Chs. 3, 18.

**Bias.** $E_\theta T-\tau(\theta)$. It is target- and model-relative; zero bias does not imply small variance or good decisions. Ch. 6.

**Bootstrap.** Approximation of a statistic's sampling law by recomputing it under an estimated data-generating law. Consistency depends on the statistic, model class, and resampling scheme. Ch. 13.

**Breakdown point.** The smallest contamination fraction capable of making an estimator arbitrarily bad under a declared contamination operation and metric. Ch. S3.

**Calibration.** Agreement between a nominal guarantee and its reference-frequency statement. Calibration may be exact, conservative, asymptotic, conditional, or design-based. Chs. 8, 9, 10, 11, S2, 12, 13.

**Causal estimand.** A contrast of potential outcomes or intervention laws. Identification requires design or assumptions beyond a regression coefficient alone. Ch. 15.

**Completeness.** A property of a statistic family: if every state gives expectation zero to $g(T)$, then $g(T)=0$ almost surely under every state. It supports uniqueness of unbiased estimators. Ch. 6.

**Confidence set.** A random set whose coverage probability is controlled under repeated data generation. After observation, ordinary frequentist coverage is not a posterior probability about a fixed parameter. Ch. 11.

**Consistency.** Convergence of an estimator to its target under a stated sequence of laws. It supplies neither a finite-sample bound nor automatic robustness. Ch. 12.

**Contiguity.** A relationship between sequences of laws under which asymptotically null events transfer. This advanced tool is not developed in the present volume and therefore has no chapter locator here.

**Decision rule.** A measurable mapping from data to actions, possibly randomised. Ch. 3.

**Design-based inference.** Probability statements generated by a known sampling or treatment-assignment mechanism, conditional on finite-population values. Chs. 15, 16.

**Effect size.** A target-scale magnitude. It is distinct from a standard error, p-value, and decision utility. Chs. 2, 8, 15.

**Estimand.** The mathematical target, such as a mean, risk difference, quantile, projection coefficient, or finite-population contrast. Ch. 2.

**Estimating equation.** An equation in the parameter formed from data, commonly $\sum \psi(X_i,\theta)=0$. Under misspecification it may target a population root rather than a generative truth. Chs. 12, S3.

**Exchangeability.** Invariance of a joint law under specified permutations. It can justify a permutation distribution only for transformations preserving the null law. Ch. 13.

**Exponential family.** A dominated family with density of the form $h(x)\exp(\eta^{\mathsf T} T(x)-A(\eta))$. Regularity and parameter-space openness matter for differentiation and completeness claims. Ch. 7.

**False discovery rate.** Expected false-discovery proportion, with a declared convention when there are no rejections. It is not familywise error. Ch. S2.

**Fisher information.** Under regularity, score variance or negative expected Hessian. Moving support or failed interchange can break familiar identities. Chs. 4, 6, 7.

**Frequentist risk.** Expected loss at a fixed state under repeated data from that state. Ch. 3.

**Identification.** The property that observationally equivalent states agree on the estimand. Estimation cannot repair nonidentification without additional information or assumptions. Ch. 2.

**Influence function.** A first-order derivative of a statistical functional under infinitesimal contamination. Bounded influence is local and does not imply high breakdown. Ch. S3.

**Likelihood.** The observation density or mass considered as a function of the parameter for fixed data. It is not a probability distribution over parameter values without further structure. Ch. 4.

**Loss.** A numerical consequence assigned to a state–action pair. Loss makes optimality comparisons explicit. Ch. 3.

**MAR.** Missing at random: after conditioning on observed variables, the missingness law does not depend on missing values in the specified full-data model. It is not empirically verifiable from observed data alone. Ch. 17.

**Maximum likelihood estimator.** A parameter value maximising likelihood over the declared parameter space. Existence, uniqueness, boundary behavior, and misspecification target require separate checks. Ch. 4.

**Minimax rule.** A rule minimising maximum risk over a stated parameter space. Minimaxity need not imply admissibility or good local performance. Chs. 3, S1.

**Monte Carlo error.** Variation due to finite simulation conditional on the analysis inputs. It is different from sampling uncertainty and model error. Chs. 13, 19.

**Nuisance parameter.** A component needed to specify observation laws but not itself the primary estimand. Profiling, conditioning, pivots, or worst-case calibration handle nuisance parameters under different conditions. Chs. 4, 10.

**Observed-data law.** The distribution of variables actually recorded, after sampling, assignment, missingness, censoring, stopping, and measurement. Chs. 1, 2, 17.

**p-value.** A statistic whose lower tail is super-uniform under every state in a declared null family. It is not the probability that the null is true. Chs. 8, 10, S2.

**Parameter.** An index or functional of a model law. Different parameterisations may describe the same family; not every parameter is identifiable. Chs. 1, 2.

**Permutation test.** A test calibrated by transformations under which the null distribution is invariant. Arbitrary shuffling is not automatically valid. Ch. 13.

**Pivot.** A data-and-parameter function whose distribution is free of unknown states in the stated model. Pivots can be inverted into tests or confidence sets. Chs. 10, 11.

**Posterior predictive distribution.** The future-observation law averaged over the posterior. Posterior predictive checks reuse data and therefore are not automatically uniform p-values. Ch. 18.

**Power.** Rejection probability as a function of the state. Size is its supremum over the null set. Chs. 8, 9, 10.

**Prior predictive distribution.** The observation law obtained by averaging the likelihood over a prior. It is central to prior checking and marginal likelihood. Ch. 18.

**Randomisation test.** A test using a known assignment distribution under a sharp or otherwise specified null. Ch. 15.

**Regular estimator/model.** An informal shorthand whose precise differentiability, support, information, and limit conditions must be stated. The book avoids treating “regular” as one universal assumption. Chs. 4, 6, 12.

**Robustness.** Stability under a named perturbation, measured by a named performance criterion. Influence, breakdown, sandwich variance, and sensitivity analysis address different perturbations. Ch. S3.

**Sandwich covariance.** The misspecification-aware asymptotic covariance $A^{-1}BA^{-T}$ for a regular estimating equation. It does not repair bias in the target or dependence omitted from $B$. Chs. S3, 14.

**Selection bias.** A change in the target-relevant distribution caused by inclusion, observation, or reporting mechanisms. It is not synonymous with all model bias. Chs. 1, 2, S2, 16, 17.

**Selective inference.** Inference whose reference law accounts for a data-dependent selection event or whose target is defined after selection. Ch. S2.

**Sensitivity analysis.** A map from unverified assumptions or perturbation values to resulting estimates, intervals, or decisions. It displays dependence but does not choose the true assumption. Chs. S3, 17, 19.

**Sufficiency.** A statistic retains all model-relative information about the parameter in the conditional-distribution sense. It need not retain information for diagnosing whether the model is credible. Ch. 5.

**Survey weight.** A quantity derived from inclusion, response, and sometimes calibration mechanisms. Its inferential meaning depends on how those components were constructed. Ch. 16.

**Type-I error.** Rejection probability under a null state. Composite-null control requires a supremum or another declared reference. Ch. 8.

## Theorem finder

**Identification impossibility.** If two states induce the same observed-data law but different target values, no data-based estimator can be consistent for both. Ch. 2 `estimands-identification-and-design`.

**Rao–Blackwell improvement and factorisation.** Conditional expectation given a sufficient statistic preserves unbiasedness and weakly improves convex loss; the factorisation criterion supplies sufficiency under domination. Chs. 5, 6.

**Cramér–Rao information bound.** Under common support, differentiability, interchange, and finite nonsingular information, covariance identities yield a variance lower bound for unbiased regular estimators. Ch. 6.

**Lehmann–Scheffé uniqueness.** A complete sufficient statistic turns an unbiased function of it into the unique uniformly minimum-variance unbiased estimator. Ch. 6.

**Exponential-family derivative identities.** On an open natural parameter space with licensed differentiation, gradient and Hessian of the log partition give the sufficient statistic's mean and covariance. Ch. 7.

**Neyman–Pearson lemma.** For two simple dominated hypotheses, a likelihood-ratio threshold test is most powerful at its achieved size, with randomisation at ties when needed. Ch. 9.

**Test–confidence duality.** Inverting a level-controlled family of tests yields a confidence set with corresponding coverage, and conversely under a coherent indexing. Ch. 11.

**Bonferroni and Holm control.** Union bounds give familywise control without dependence assumptions; Holm's step-down procedure preserves it while improving rejection opportunities. Ch. S2.

**Benjamini–Hochberg control.** The step-up rule controls false discovery rate under independence and stated positive-dependence conditions; arbitrary dependence needs modification. Ch. S2.

**Delta method and estimating-equation expansion.** Differentiability plus a stochastic first-order approximation transfers an estimator's limiting law; singular derivatives and boundaries require other rates. Ch. 12.

**Permutation calibration.** Under invariance of the null law to a finite transformation group, the orbit rank gives an exact or conservative test with tie handling. Ch. 13.

**M-estimator sandwich limit.** Smooth population identification, stochastic equicontinuity, a nonsingular derivative, and a central limit theorem yield asymptotic covariance $A^{-1}BA^{-T}$. Ch. S3.

**Gauss–Markov theorem.** Conditional on a full-rank fixed design with mean-zero uncorrelated equal-variance errors, least squares has minimum covariance among linear unbiased estimators. It is not an optimality theorem among all estimators. Ch. 14.

**Randomisation unbiasedness.** Under the declared assignment mechanism and consistency/no-interference setup, the difference in treatment-arm means is unbiased for the finite-sample average treatment effect. Ch. 15.

**Horvitz–Thompson unbiasedness.** Positive first-order inclusion probabilities make inverse-probability weighted totals design-unbiased; exact variance uses joint inclusion probabilities. Ch. 16.

**Observed-data nonidentification under missingness.** Different full-data laws can yield the same observed-data law while disagreeing on the missing outcomes and target. Ch. 17.

**Posterior expected-loss theorem.** Minimising posterior expected loss pointwise in observed data minimises integrated Bayes risk when expectations exist. Ch. 18.

For failure modes and hypothesis-removal examples, use Ch. 19 `counterexample-atlas-and-inference-capstones` alongside the theorem's home chapter.


## Additional distinctions used in the development

**Common conditional kernel.** A parameter-independent conditional law of the full observation given a statistic, valid under every model state in the declared scope. In finite spaces it can be checked by conditional mass calculations. In continuous spaces, existence and the version shared across the model need to be justified or assumed explicitly. Chs. 5, 6.

**Basu independence.** Under the stated completeness, sufficiency and common-kernel conditions, a complete sufficient statistic is independent of an ancillary statistic. Ancillarity alone is insufficient. The bounded-indicator proof and its conditional-expectation hypotheses belong to Ch. 6.

**Randomised p-value.** A null-calibrated rank using a predeclared independent uniform draw to distribute probability inside a tie. The auxiliary draw belongs to the procedure and its probability space. A deterministic mid-p adjustment has a different guarantee and need not be super-uniform. Chs. 8, 9, 10.

**Reference instantiation.** A completely specified model and response used to illustrate an open investigation. Its assumptions and synthetic calculations do not alter the original assignment or supply observations from an uncollected dataset. Chs. 2, 3, 5, 8, 11, 12, 13, 19.

**Pointwise and uniform statements.** A pointwise guarantee fixes a state before taking a limit or probability. A uniform guarantee controls an indicated collection of states simultaneously in the specified supremum sense. Neither follows by replacing an exact distribution with a familiar limiting shape. Chs. 11, S2, 12.
