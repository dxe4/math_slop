<a id="section-3-glossary"></a>
## Glossary {#section-5-glossary}

**Adaptive reasoning.** The capacity for logical thought, reflection, explanation, and justification. Ch. 1 (*Mathematical proficiency is braided*).

**Claim-status ledger.** A running record classifying each major statement as mathematical result, external research finding, local observation, or teaching conjecture. Front matter.

**Common content knowledge.** Mathematical knowledge and skill used in settings other than teaching. “Common” means not teaching-specific, not universally possessed. Ch. 10 (*Teacher mathematical knowledge is specialised*).

**Componentwise addition error.** The misconception that fraction addition proceeds by adding numerators and adding denominators, overgeneralised from whole-number place-value addition. Ch. 5 (*A misconception is not just a wrong answer*).

**Conceptual understanding.** Comprehension of mathematical concepts, operations, and relations, including knowing why a procedure works. Ch. 1.

**Conjecture map.** A table linking lesson moments to mathematical purposes, anticipated activities, evidence criteria, and contingent responses. Ch. 2 (*Begin with an explicit mathematical goal*).

**Construct validity.** The argument that a measure represents the intended idea and not construct-irrelevant demand. Ch. 9 (*Formative assessment is an inference process*).

**Coverage trap.** Treating a list of operations or standards as separate goals, obscuring the underlying mathematical idea that unifies them. Ch. 2.

**Cross-multiplication rule.** The valid rule $a/b < c/d$ iff $ad < bc$ for positive denominators; dangerous when taught without attention to sign conditions. Ch. 2.

**Derived fact.** A computation using known facts and structural relations rather than counting. Ch. 1.

**Distributive property.** $a(b + c) = ab + ac$; foundational for expansion, factoring, and area models. Chs. 3, 4, 8.

**Effect size.** A standardized measure of contrast magnitude, useful for comparison but not a substitute for raw-scale interpretation. Ch. 12 (*Read educational evidence with mathematical care*).

**Enactive representation.** A representation in which learners act out or build a mathematical relationship physically. Ch. 3 (*Representations are tools, not decorations*).

**Evidence trap.** Stating a conceptual goal but assessing only computation. Ch. 2.

**External research finding.** A bounded result reported by a named study under stated design, sample, and analysis. Front matter; Ch. 12.

**Formative assessment.** Assessment *for* learning: used during instruction to adjust teaching and learning. Ch. 9.

**Heterogeneity.** Variation in true effects or study characteristics that may explain differences in observed results. Ch. 12.

**Hinge question.** A brief assessment item used during instruction to decide whether the class is ready to move on. Ch. 9.

**Hypothesis ledger.** A record of the assumptions built into each task, measure, or design. Front matter.

**Iconic representation.** A representation using tables, diagrams, or pictures to record quantities. Ch. 3.

**Local observation.** Something seen, heard, or recorded in a specific setting without a controlled design. Front matter; Chs. 5, 9, 12.

**Mathematical Knowledge for Teaching (MKT).** The specialised knowledge teachers need beyond general mathematical knowledge, including common content knowledge, specialised content knowledge, knowledge of content and students, and knowledge of content and teaching. Ch. 10.

**Mathematical result.** A statement true by definition, deduction, or calculation within stated axioms. Front matter; Chs. 2, 8.

**Misconception.** A stable, coherent, but incorrect way of reasoning that produces predictable patterns of error. Ch. 5.

**Nonexample.** A deliberate boundary probe close to a concept but violating a defining property. Ch. 4 (*Examples define the conceptual boundary*).

**PEMDAS.** A mnemonic for order of operations; can mislead learners about equal precedence of addition and subtraction. Ch. 7 (*Problem solving can be taught without reducing it to a mnemonic*).

**Productive disposition.** The habitual inclination to see mathematics as sensible, useful, and worthwhile, coupled with a belief in one's own efficacy. Ch. 1.

**Procedural fluency.** Skill in carrying out procedures flexibly, accurately, efficiently, and appropriately. Ch. 1.

**Proof by induction.** A method proving a statement for all positive integers by establishing a base case and an inductive step. Ch. 8 (*Proof teaching coordinates examples and necessity*).

**Prototype trap.** Showing only a narrow range of examples, leading learners to treat incidental features as defining properties. Ch. 4.

**Representational fluency.** The ability to move among connected representations and recognise the invariant mathematical object across them. Ch. 3.

**Specialised content knowledge.** The mathematical knowledge unique to teaching, such as choosing representations, evaluating alternative methods, and explaining why procedures work. Ch. 10.

**Strategic competence.** The ability to formulate, represent, and solve mathematical problems. Ch. 1.

**Summative assessment.** Assessment *of* learning: used to evaluate achievement at the end of a unit or course. Ch. 9.

**Teaching conjecture.** A reasoned prediction about what may help a particular group, to be tested locally. Front matter; Chs. 2, 5.

**Transfer task.** A problem whose surface form differs from worked examples, testing whether structure is recognised. Ch. 2.

**Universal Design for Learning (UDL).** A framework for designing instruction that provides multiple means of representation, action, and expression. Ch. 11 (*Inclusion changes design, not mathematical ambition*).

**Validity threat.** A feature of a task or context that may cause the assessment to measure something other than the intended construct. Ch. 9.

**Variation framework.** A design principle holding the structural target constant while varying superficial features, then varying one structural feature at a time to test boundaries. Ch. 4.

### Mathematical results {#section-87-mathematical-results}

**Fraction comparison by common denominator (Ch. 2).** For positive denominators, $a/b < c/d$ exactly when $ad < bc$. Cross-multiplication is valid but can obscure the multiplicative structure.

**Distributive property (Chs. 3, 4, 8).** $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ for all real numbers $a, b, c$. The property does not hold when multiplication replaces addition inside the parentheses.

**Sum of first $n$ odd positive integers (Ch. 8).** $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n - 1) = n^{2}$. Proved by induction and by visual "growing a square" argument.

**Sum of first $n$ positive integers (Ch. 8).** $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$. Proved by induction and by the "two triangles make a rectangle" visual proof.

**Maximising area for fixed perimeter (Ch. 7).** For fixed sum $S = a + b$, the product $ab$ is maximised when $a = b$. Proved by the identity $ab = ((a + b)/2)^{2} - ((a - b)/2)^{2}$.

**Even square implies even integer (Ch. 8).** For all integers $n$, if $n^{2}$ is even then $n$ is even. Proved by contrapositive.

<a id="section-99-external-research-findings-bounded"></a>
### External research findings (bounded) {#section-101-external-research-findings-bounded}

**NCTM Principles to Actions (Chs. 1, 11).** The National Council of Teachers of Mathematics articulates teaching practices and an access-and-equity position statement. It is professional guidance, not an intervention-effect estimate.

**Adding It Up proficiency strands (Ch. 1).** The National Research Council identifies five interdependent strands of mathematical proficiency. The report is a consensus synthesis; it does not prescribe a single instructional sequence.

**Feedback and formative assessment evidence (Chs. 9, 12).** The EEF Feedback toolkit reports a positive average months-of-progress estimate across a heterogeneous feedback literature, with wide variation and possible negative effects. Formative assessment is one component of feedback; the estimate is not a standalone standardized effect size for every formative practice.

<a id="section-107-teaching-conjectures"></a>
### Teaching conjectures {#section-109-teaching-conjectures}

**Container hypothesis (Ch. 5).** Providing a container for counted objects may reduce spatial memory demand and allow one-to-one coordination to become visible. It does not guarantee transfer to open arrangements.

**Prediction-before-discussion hypothesis (Ch. 2).** Asking learners to predict a result before discussion may make initial reasoning visible and reduce partner dominance. Needs local testing.

**Tactile-graph equivalence hypothesis (Ch. 11).** A tactile graph preserves the cognitive demand of interpreting a two-variable relationship for a blind learner, provided the spatial features are accurately represented. Depends on the quality of the tactile production.
