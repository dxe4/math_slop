
# References and Further Reading {#section-1-references-and-further-reading}

This bibliography identifies standard comparison texts, synthesis bodies, and professional guidance. The book is written under CC BY-SA 4.0; standard definitions and educational conventions belong to shared practice. References are included for further reading, historical context, alternative treatments, and broader coverage.

A named result or study is a retrieval convention, not a complete history. Educational ideas are often the product of long development across languages, institutions, and communities; a modern statement may differ sharply from an early source.

<a id="section-5-mathematical-proficiency-and-teaching"></a>
## Mathematical proficiency and teaching {#section-7-mathematical-proficiency-and-teaching}

- National Research Council, Mathematics Learning Study Committee; Kilpatrick, J., Swafford, J., & Findell, B. (Eds.). (2001). *Adding It Up: Helping Children Learn Mathematics*. National Academies Press. [doi:10.17226/9822](https://doi.org/10.17226/9822). Chapter 4, especially p. 116, introduces the five interdependent strands of mathematical proficiency; claims should be read with the report's scope and synthesis methods.
- Hiebert, J., & Carpenter, T. P. (1992). "Learning and teaching with understanding." In D. A. Grouws (Ed.), *Handbook of Research on Mathematics Teaching and Learning* (pp. 65--97). Macmillan. A theoretical account of how understanding develops through connected representations.
- Watson, A., & Mason, J. (2005). *Mathematics as a Constructive Activity: Learners Generating Examples*. Lawrence Erlbaum Associates. ISBN 978-0-8058-4344-6. [Publisher record](https://www.routledge.com/Mathematics-as-a-Constructive-Activity-Learners-Generating-Examples/Watson-Mason/p/book/9780805843446). Chapters 1 and 5 develop learner-generated examples and dimensions of variation used in Chapter 4.

These works use different conventions for developmental progression, task design, and evidence quality. Comparison is most useful when those conventions are recorded rather than blended.

<a id="section-13-teacher-knowledge-and-specialised-practice"></a>
## Teacher knowledge and specialised practice {#section-15-teacher-knowledge-and-specialised-practice}

- Ball, D. L., Thames, M. H., & Phelps, G. (2008). "Content knowledge for teaching: What makes it special?" *Journal of Teacher Education* **59**(5), 389--407. [doi:10.1177/0022487108324554](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022487108324554). Defines common and specialised content knowledge and distinguishes knowledge of content and teaching; pp. 399--401 supply the boundaries used here.
- Hill, H. C., Rowan, B., & Ball, D. L. (2005). "Effects of Teachers' Mathematical Knowledge for Teaching on Student Achievement." *American Educational Research Journal* **42**(2), 371--406. [doi:10.3102/00028312042002371](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312042002371). Reports a covariate-adjusted association between teachers' measured mathematical knowledge for teaching and learner gains; the study does not establish that association by randomized assignment.

<a id="section-18-assessment-and-formative-practice"></a>
## Assessment and formative practice {#section-20-assessment-and-formative-practice}

- Wiliam, D. (2011). *Embedded Formative Assessment*. Solution Tree Press. A practical treatment of formative assessment strategies grounded in research evidence.
- Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). "Assessment and classroom learning." *Assessment in Education: Principles, Policy & Practice, 5*(1), 7--74. [doi:10.1080/0969595980050102](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969595980050102). An influential heterogeneous review of classroom assessment evidence; it does not establish one context-free effect size for every formative practice.

<a id="section-23-problem-solving-and-reasoning"></a>
## Problem solving and reasoning {#section-25-problem-solving-and-reasoning}

- Schoenfeld, A. H. (1985). *Mathematical Problem Solving*. Academic Press. A cognitive account of problem-solving heuristics, metacognition, and belief systems.
- Schoenfeld, A. H. (1992). "Learning to think mathematically: Problem solving, metacognition, and sense making in mathematics." In D. A. Grouws (Ed.), *Handbook of Research on Mathematics Teaching and Learning* (pp. 334--370). Macmillan. A comprehensive review of problem-solving research and its instructional implications.
- What Works Clearinghouse. (2012). *Improving Mathematical Problem Solving in Grades 4 Through 8: A Practice Guide* (NCEE 2012-4055). Institute of Education Sciences. [Official guide and evidence ratings](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/16/Published). The ratings are recommendation-specific: monitoring and reflection receive strong evidence, teaching multiple strategies moderate evidence, and preparing problems minimal evidence.
- Education Endowment Foundation. (2017). *Improving Mathematics in Key Stages Two and Three*. [Guidance report](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/maths-ks-2-3/). A separate synthesis for English school contexts, not a direct common comparator trial with the IES guide.

<a id="section-30-equity-mindset-and-inclusive-design"></a>
## Equity, mindset, and inclusive design {#section-32-equity-mindset-and-inclusive-design}

- Boaler, J. (2016). *Mathematical Mindsets: Unleashing Students' Potential through Creative Math, Inspiring Messages and Innovative Teaching*. Jossey-Bass. A treatment of growth mindset, visual mathematics, and equitable teaching practices.
- National Council of Teachers of Mathematics. (2014). *Principles to Actions: Ensuring Mathematical Success for All*. NCTM, 139 pp., ISBN 978-0-87353-774-2. [Publisher record](https://www.nctm.org/store/Products/Principles-to-Actions--Ensuring-Mathematical-Success-for-All/). Professional guidance on teaching practices.
- National Council of Teachers of Mathematics. (April 2014). *Access and Equity in Mathematics Education*. [Official position statement](https://www.nctm.org/Standards-and-Positions/Position-Statements/Access-and-Equity-in-Mathematics-Education/). The position invoked in Chapter 11; professional guidance rather than an intervention-effect estimate.
- CAST. (2018). *Universal Design for Learning Guidelines Version 2.2*. [Archived version locator](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/more/downloads/). This historical version supplied the framework used here; CAST released version 3.0 in 2024.

<a id="section-37-learning-and-cognition"></a>
## Learning and cognition {#section-39-learning-and-cognition}

- Bransford, J. D., Brown, A. L., & Cocking, R. R. (Eds.). (2000). *How People Learn: Brain, Mind, Experience, and School* (Expanded ed.). National Academies Press. A consensus synthesis on learning, transfer, and expert performance.
- National Research Council. (2009). *Mathematics Learning in Early Childhood: Paths Toward Excellence and Equity*. National Academies Press. DOI: [10.17226/12519](https://doi.org/10.17226/12519). Part II, beginning on p. 121, synthesizes typical teaching-learning paths and adapting activities to assessed levels; it does not establish one optimal path across all learners and tasks.

<a id="section-42-research-methods-and-evidence-appraisal"></a>
## Research methods and evidence appraisal {#section-44-research-methods-and-evidence-appraisal}

- What Works Clearinghouse. (2022, revised December 2022). *Procedures and Standards Handbook, Version 5.0*. A transparent standard for specified evidence reviews, not the only form of education inquiry.
- Education Endowment Foundation. (reviewed June 2021). “Feedback.” *Teaching and Learning Toolkit*. [Living toolkit page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/feedback), retrieved 6 September 2026. The page summarizes 155 studies as an average of six months' additional progress, warns that effects vary widely and may be negative, and treats formative assessment as one component of feedback rather than a standalone standardized effect size.

<a id="section-47-historical-attribution-checklist"></a>
## Historical attribution checklist {#section-49-historical-attribution-checklist}

Every historical or priority claim should record:

1. a classification as contemporary exposition, secondary history, translated primary source, or primary source;
2. verified author, title, edition, publisher or archive, and stable locator;
3. a note explaining whether the citation supports provenance, formulation, evidence, example, or broader context;
4. a check that the modern terminology is not projected unchanged backward;
5. careful comparison of primary sources for culturally broad claims and disputed priority.

<a id="section-57-reading-route-recommendations"></a>
## Reading-route recommendations {#section-59-reading-route-recommendations}

For a deeper treatment of mathematical proficiency and its assessment, proceed to the Math Atlas volume on mathematics teaching and learning evidence. For problem-solving heuristics and metacognition, retain the framework from Chapter 7 and seek opportunities to apply it across domains. For teacher knowledge, continue into measurement and professional development through dedicated texts. For inclusive design, seek opportunities to audit your own materials against the UDL guidelines.

<a id="section-61-citation-policy-for-contributors"></a>
## Citation policy for contributors {#section-63-citation-policy-for-contributors}

Paraphrase sources and cite the job they perform. Do not copy exercise sequences, distinctive examples, diagrams, or prose merely because a concept is standard. Direct quotations require exact locators and license or fair-use review. When sources disagree, record the disagreement. When provenance is unknown, label it unknown rather than inventing a clean lineage.
