## Chapter 1: Mathematical proficiency is braided {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided}

[Chapter context](mathematical-proficiency-is-braided.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-1}
A learner solves $15\times 12$ by writing $15\times 12=(10+5)\times 12=120+60=180$. Name the strand most clearly demonstrated, and describe a follow-up question that would elicit a different strand.

**Hint 1.** Name what the displayed decomposition explains.

**Hint 2.** Change the task from obtaining an answer to justifying a relation.

**Solution.** The decomposition demonstrates **conceptual understanding** of the distributive property. A follow-up question for **adaptive reasoning** might ask: "If you replace $12$ by $11$, will the answer decrease by $15$? Prove your claim without calculating $15\times 11$." A follow-up for **strategic competence** might ask: "You need to calculate $15\times 12$ mentally while walking. Is this decomposition the fastest method? Describe an alternative and compare."

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-2}
Explain why fluency practiced without attention to meaning can transfer poorly, using a specific mathematical example.

**Hint 1.** Try the verbal rule on a noninteger.

**Hint 2.** Compare appending a decimal zero with multiplying the quantity.

**Solution.** Consider the rule "to multiply by $10$, add a zero." A learner who memorises this without understanding place value may write $3.5\times 10=3.50$ or, worse, $0.5\times 10=0.50$. The rule was valid only for whole numbers, but the learner has not learned to check the domain. The underlying structure---that multiplication by $10$ shifts each digit one place left in a base-ten system---would have prevented the error. Fluency without meaning is context-bound; meaning without fluency is slow and effortful.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-3}
Design a single task about comparing $\frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{5}{6}$ that engages at least three of the five proficiency strands. Label each part with the strand it targets.

**Hint 1.** Keep the referent whole fixed for a direct comparison.

**Hint 2.** Use distances from one as a second argument.

**Solution.**

(a) Rename both fractions with a common denominator and state which is larger. *(Procedural fluency)*

(b) Draw two rectangles of equal size, shade $\frac{3}{4}$ of one and $\frac{5}{6}$ of the other, and explain visually why one shading covers more area. *(Conceptual understanding)*

(c) Without computing, decide whether $\frac{3}{4}$ or $\frac{5}{6}$ is closer to $1$, and use that observation to justify the comparison. *(Adaptive reasoning)*

(d) Write a realistic word problem in which someone must choose between $\frac{3}{4}$ of a large pizza and $\frac{5}{6}$ of a small pizza, and discuss what additional information is needed. *(Strategic competence)*

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-4}
A teacher notices that a learner can factor $x^{2}-9$ as $(x+3)(x-3)$ but cannot factor $x^{2}-7$. What coefficient domain must be clarified, what competing explanations fit this response, and what follow-up task would distinguish them?

**Hint 1.** Factorisation is relative to an allowed coefficient field.

**Hint 2.** Compare radical factors with rational factors before inferring a gap.

**Solution.** First ask which coefficients are allowed. Over $\mathbb{R}$, $x^{2}-7=(x-\sqrt7)(x+\sqrt7)$; over $\mathbb{Q}$ it has no rational linear factors because $\sqrt7$ is irrational. The learner may be correctly restricting factors to rational coefficients, may not yet be fluent with radicals, or may not recognise the difference-of-squares structure. One response does not identify a weakest proficiency strand. Ask the learner to compare $x^{2}-4$, $x^{2}-25$, $x^{2}-2$, and $x^{2}-y^{2}$, state the coefficient domain for each claim, expand proposed factors, and explain the general rule $a^{2}-b^{2}=(a-b)(a+b)$. Use their explanations to choose further conceptual or procedural support.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-5}
Prove the factor-compensation rule over real numbers and state its excluded case. Explain why it cannot justify replacing eighteen times twenty-five by thirty-six times five.

**Hint 1.** Name the common multiplier and its reciprocal.

**Hint 2.** Compare the product of the two actual change factors with one.

**Solution.** For nonzero real $c$, associativity and $c/c=1$ give $(ca)(b/c)=ab$. Division by zero is undefined, so zero is excluded. In the proposed calculation the factors change by two and one fifth, whose product is two fifths rather than one. The transformed product is therefore two fifths of the original. A correct compensating second factor is twelve and a half. Asking a learner to name both multipliers distinguishes knowing this invariant from merely remembering the phrase “double and halve”; it still does not establish why an earlier incorrect answer occurred.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-6}
A fictional learner says that two thirds of a large pizza is less food than three quarters of a small pizza because two thirds is the smaller fraction. What additional information is needed, and how can the comparison go either way?

**Hint 1.** Fractions here multiply different whole quantities.

**Hint 2.** Try large-to-small area ratios of two and of eleven tenths.

**Solution.** We need the pizza areas, assuming comparable thickness and food density if “food” means amount rather than area. Let the small area be one unit. If the large area is two units, its two-thirds portion is four thirds, exceeding three quarters. If the large area is eleven tenths, its portion is eleven fifteenths, which is less than three quarters because forty-four is less than forty-five after multiplying by sixty. Both choices respect “large” as larger area. Comparing fraction numerals alone is insufficient when the whole changes. A task that supplies only the fractions cannot justify a unique numerical conclusion.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-7}
A table lists outputs one, three, five at inputs zero, one, two. Construct two polynomial rules agreeing there but disagreeing elsewhere. State a premise making the linear rule uniquely determined.

**Hint 1.** Find a nonzero polynomial vanishing at all listed inputs.

**Hint 2.** Add it to twice the input plus one.

**Solution.** Take $f(x)=2x+1$ and $g(x)=2x+1+x(x-1)(x-2)$. Each added term is zero at every listed input, so the rules agree there. At input three they give seven and thirteen respectively. If the rule is assumed affine on the real numbers, write it as $mx+b$. The zero row forces $b=1$, and the one row forces $m=2$; the remaining row is consistent. Thus the affine premise establishes uniqueness within that class, whereas the table alone does not. The task concerns adaptive reasoning about evidence as well as fluent substitution.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-8}
Find the positive integer pair with sum twenty-two having largest product. Give a proof that avoids enumerating every pair and explain one follow-up for a learner who proposes eleven and eleven without justification.

**Hint 1.** Write the factors as eleven plus and minus the same integer.

**Hint 2.** A square is nonnegative and vanishes only at zero.

**Solution.** Write the factors $11+d$ and $11-d$, with integer $d$ and $|d|<11$ from positivity. Their product is $121-d^2$, at most one hundred twenty-one, with equality exactly when $d=0$. Hence eleven and eleven uniquely maximise the unordered pair. Ask the learner to show how changing both factors while preserving their sum changes the product. This keeps the correct conjecture while eliciting its warrant. A correct unreasoned answer supports a narrower claim than the same answer accompanied by a proof; neither response measures all five strands.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-9}
A learner produces a correct written parity proof but declines to present it orally. What can be recorded about the mathematics, and why is a negative judgement of productive disposition unsupported?

**Hint 1.** Separate the mathematical artefact from the mode of presentation.

**Hint 2.** List at least two explanations unrelated to mathematical value or effort.

**Solution.** Record the actual proof and whether it covers arbitrary allowed integers with valid deductions. Oral participation is a separate observation. Translation time, speech access, concern about public evaluation, or a preference for writing could explain declining to speak. None implies that the learner sees mathematics as senseless or lacks persistence. Offer an accessible explanation mode and, if relevant, ask privately about the presentation barrier. This supplies no guarantee of improved participation and no diagnosis of a hidden trait; it is a proposal for collecting evidence without confusing access with mathematical competence.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-mathematical-proficiency-is-braided-exercise-10}
Design a pair of questions distinguishing correct use of a common denominator from understanding why it is needed. Supply the mathematics expected in each answer and state one limit of the inference.

**Hint 1.** Use a routine sum followed by a question about the size of its parts.

**Hint 2.** A picture must use the same whole for both addends.

**Solution.** First ask for two fifths plus one third of the same litre. Renaming gives six fifteenths plus five fifteenths, hence eleven fifteenths. Next ask why the denominator is not eight and request a partition or unit explanation. A fifteenth-litre is a common measure: each fifth contains three such units and each third contains five. Fifths and thirds cannot be counted together as identical parts. A correct second explanation supports understanding of this common-unit argument. It does not establish transfer to negative fractions, unlike wholes, or all rational operations; further tasks would be needed for those claims. The pair should inform the next teaching decision rather than become two supposedly independent proficiency scores.

## Chapter 2: Begin with an explicit mathematical goal {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals}

[Chapter context](begin-with-explicit-goals.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-1}
Rewrite the vague goal "Learners will understand linear functions" into a usable goal with object, operation, and evidence.

**Hint 1.** Specify one relation within the broad topic.

**Hint 2.** Require evidence connecting its parameters to the chosen context.

**Solution.** A usable goal might be:

> Learners will interpret the slope and intercept of a linear function in a realistic context, and predict the output for a given input by explaining how the rate of change and initial value contribute.

Object: linear function (specifically slope and intercept). Operation: interpret and predict. Evidence: an explanation that connects the parameters to the context.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-2}
A teacher states the goal: "Learners will solve quadratic equations." Design a task that assesses procedural fluency, and a second task that assesses conceptual understanding of the same goal. Explain how the two tasks differ in what they reveal.

**Hint 1.** Solving and modelling are different operations.

**Hint 2.** Check which algebraic roots are permitted by the rectangle domain.

**Solution.**

Procedural fluency task: Solve $2x^{2}-5x-3=0$ by any method.

This reveals whether the learner can execute factoring, the quadratic formula, or completing the square. It does not reveal whether the learner knows why the methods work or when each is most efficient.

Related structural task: For $x>3$, a rectangle has area $2x^{2}-5x-3$ and one side length $x-3$. Find the other side length and justify the factorisation. Then explain why the roots of the polynomial do not describe nondegenerate rectangles in this domain.

Factoring gives $(x-3)(2x+1)$, so the other side is $2x+1$. Both sides are positive for $x>3$. The polynomial roots are three and negative one half; neither belongs to that domain. This task investigates factorisation and the model's restrictions, but alone it does not assess solving a quadratic equation. To align with the original goal, also ask for all real roots of the displayed polynomial equation and a justification using the zero-product property. The two settings must be distinguished rather than silently treating every algebraic root as a physical rectangle.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-3}
Explain why the rule "cross multiply and see which product is bigger" can be a misconception if taught without derivation, and give a numerical example where a learner might misapply it.

**Hint 1.** Multiplying an inequality by a negative quantity reverses its direction.

**Hint 2.** Compute the sign of the product of the denominators.

**Solution.** For nonzero denominators, multiplying $a/b<c/d$ by $bd$ preserves the direction when $bd>0$ and reverses it when $bd<0$. Therefore the inequality is equivalent to $ad<bc$ if the denominators have the same sign, and to $ad>bc$ if they have opposite signs. For example, compare $1/(-2)$ with $1/3$. Here $bd=-6<0$, so the correct equivalent inequality is $1\cdot3>1\cdot(-2)$; indeed $3>-2$, and $-1/2<1/3$. A memorized rule that always reads the cross-products in the original direction would reach the wrong conclusion.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-4}
A unit plan lists the following goals for one week: (a) add fractions with like denominators, (b) add fractions with unlike denominators, (c) subtract fractions, (d) simplify fractions, (e) convert mixed numbers. Identify the coverage trap, and propose a single central goal that unifies the week.

**Hint 1.** Find a common meaning beneath the listed procedures.

**Hint 2.** Make the common measuring unit part of the evidence.

**Solution.** The coverage trap is that each operation is treated as a separate skill, obscuring the underlying idea that fractions are quantities that can be decomposed and recomposed only when expressed in common units. A central goal might be:

> Learners will interpret fraction addition and subtraction as combining or removing quantities measured in the same unit, and will justify renaming fractions as finding an equivalent measure in a larger or smaller unit.

This goal makes the operations consequences of a single concept rather than isolated procedures.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-5}
Rewrite “learn proportional reasoning” as a goal distinguishing a proportional price rule from a fixed charge plus a unit rate. Supply a contrast and the expected justification.

**Hint 1.** Keep the unit rate the same in both rules.

**Hint 2.** Compare ratios, not only equal input increments.

**Solution.** A usable goal is to classify each price model, compute its invariant, and explain the fixed charge in units of money. For masses two and four, prices six and twelve fit the proportional rule of three pounds per kilogram. Prices eleven and seventeen fit the same rate plus a five-pound charge; their ratios to mass differ. The equal six-pound increment is common to both, so it cannot distinguish direct proportion. The expected evidence names the constant ratio in the first case and its failure in the second. Extrapolating beyond the rows still requires a model premise.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-6}
Solve $(x^2-4)/(x-2)=4$ over the reals. Explain what evidence a goal about domain-preserving transformations should require.

**Hint 1.** State the forbidden input before cancelling.

**Hint 2.** The transformed equation can have a candidate outside that domain.

**Solution.** The original domain excludes two. On that domain factorisation and cancellation give $x+2=4$, whose only candidate is two. Since it is excluded, there are no solutions. The evidence should include the domain, the relative equivalence of the cancelled equation on that domain, and rejection of the candidate for the explicit denominator reason. An answer of “no solution” without this reasoning supports a weaker inference; it could have been guessed. An answer of two shows that the supplied work did not preserve the original domain, without determining why.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-7}
A task asks whether some positive integer square ends in six. A second asks which final decimal digits an integer square can have. Explain why the evidence requirements differ, and solve both.

**Hint 1.** One witness suffices for an existential statement.

**Hint 2.** Every integer has one of ten remainders modulo ten.

**Solution.** Four squared is sixteen, giving the requested witness. To classify every final digit, examine the possible remainders zero through nine. Squaring them gives final digits zero, one, four, nine, six, five, six, nine, four, one. Thus the possible digits are zero, one, four, five, six, and nine, each actually attained by an entry. This finite argument proves a universal classification because every integer is represented by exactly one of the exhaustive remainder classes; it is not merely a list of arbitrary examples. A goal should specify whether constructing a witness or establishing exhaustive coverage is required.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-8}
A learner factors a quadratic correctly but gives only one of its two real roots. Design a follow-up that investigates solution completeness without presuming the cause of the omission.

**Hint 1.** Ask what the zero-product property says about both factors.

**Hint 2.** Include a repeated-root comparison.

**Solution.** Present $(x-2)(x+5)=0$ and ask for every real solution with a reason that no others occur. The answers are two and negative five: a product of real numbers is zero exactly when at least one factor is zero. Then compare $(x-2)^2=0$, which has only two as a distinct root. Ask the learner to explain why the first has two distinct roots and the second one. This can distinguish an omitted factor, a repeated-root convention, or an incomplete reading, but no single predicted explanation should be recorded as established before the follow-up response.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-9}
A lesson targets interpreting slope in units, but its final task supplies the slope and asks learners to plot points. Explain the alignment gap and propose a replacement.

**Hint 1.** Identify which operation the supplied value has already performed.

**Hint 2.** Require both a quotient and its contextual meaning.

**Solution.** Plotting a supplied rule can show coordinate fluency without interpreting the rate. Instead give a tank volume of twelve litres at minute two and twenty-four litres at minute five, with an explicitly constant filling rate. Ask for the rate and its meaning. The volume increase is twelve litres over three minutes, so the rate is four litres per minute: each extra minute adds four litres under the model. This addresses the goal. It does not by itself assess interpreting the intercept, which would require a further question and a domain discussion.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-begin-with-explicit-goals-exercise-10}
During a lesson on comparing fractions, several learners correctly compute products but cannot identify the original whole. What should a revised next goal say, and what must the teacher avoid claiming about the original goal?

**Hint 1.** Name the prerequisite that the responses leave uncertain.

**Hint 2.** Keep the original intended evidence separate from the new plan.

**Solution.** A next goal might require learners to identify a common referent whole, express two fractions as quantities of that whole, and justify which quantity is greater with a shared unit. Supply matched-whole and changed-whole cases so the referent matters. Record that the original work showed successful product calculations but did not yet establish the intended quantity comparison. Do not retrospectively claim complete understanding merely because arithmetic was correct, and do not claim every learner lacks the prerequisite. The revision is an instructional response to limited evidence, not an empirical finding that the new approach will necessarily succeed.

## Chapter 3: Representations are tools, not decorations {#solutions-representations-are-tools}

[Chapter context](representations-are-tools.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-1}
A teacher shows a graph of $y=x^{2}$ and asks, "What happens to $y$ when $x$ increases by $1$?" A learner answers, "It goes up by $1$." Diagnose the error and design a representation that would repair it.

**Hint 1.** Compute the change for several starting inputs.

**Hint 2.** The general change is obtained by expanding the next square.

**Solution.** The learner may be treating the graph as a linear object or may be confusing the change in $x$ with the change in $y$ without examining the curve. A table showing $x=0,1,2,3$ and $y=0,1,4,9$ makes the non-constant increase visible: the differences are $1,3,5$. A second representation, the difference table, can lead to the observation that the first differences themselves increase linearly, foreshadowing the derivative.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-2}
For $y=2x+1$, use the inputs $x=0,1,3,7$ to distinguish raw output differences from the rates $\Delta y/\Delta x$. Then compare the changed table $x=0,1,3$ and $y=1,2,10$. What do the calculations establish about the sampled points, and what does the known rule establish beyond its table?

**Hint 1.** Pair every output difference with its input difference.

**Hint 2.** Distinguish a sampled relationship from a known function rule.

**Solution.** The first outputs are $1,3,7,15$. Their consecutive differences $2,4,8$ are unequal, while the input differences are $1,2,4$. The rates are $2/1=4/2=8/4=2$. Uneven spacing does not make this quotient misleading: for any distinct real inputs, $((2x_2+1)-(2x_1+1))/(x_2-x_1)=2$. The known affine rule proves pair-independence on the whole stated domain.

For the changed table, the rates are $(2-1)/(1-0)=1$ and $(10-2)/(3-1)=4$. They differ, so no single affine function fits all three points. Conversely, equal rates in a finite table establish only that its sampled points are collinear; without a stated rule or further argument they do not prove that every unlisted value follows the same affine law. Compare raw output differences only when the input steps are equal; otherwise compare the normalised rates.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-3}
Design a sequence of three representations for teaching that $(-3)\times(-4)=12$. Justify each choice.

**Hint 1.** Say what reflection represents and what algebra justifies it.

**Hint 2.** A continued pattern is not a proof of every signed case.

**Solution.**

1. **Number-line reflection:** Start with $3\times(-4)=-12$, which can be shown as three jumps of $-4$ to the left from $0$. Reflect across $0$ to argue that $(-3)\times(-4)$ is the opposite of $3\times(-4)$, hence $12$. This representation uses geometric intuition but requires accepting that reflection preserves magnitude while reversing sign.

2. **Pattern continuation:** Present the sequence $4\times(-4)=-16$, $3\times(-4)=-12$, $2\times(-4)=-8$, $1\times(-4)=-4$, $0\times(-4)=0$. Each step decreases the first factor by $1$ and increases the product by $4$. Continuing the pattern gives $(-1)\times(-4)=4$, $(-2)\times(-4)=8$, $(-3)\times(-4)=12$. This representation uses inductive reasoning but does not prove the rule for all integers.

3. **Algebraic proof:** Use the distributive property: $0=(-3+3)\times(-4)=(-3)\times(-4)+3\times(-4)=(-3)\times(-4)-12$. Therefore $(-3)\times(-4)=12$. This representation is deductive and general but requires prior acceptance of the distributive property for negative numbers.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-4}
A learner says, "The equation $y=2x+1$ and the graph are just two ways of saying the same thing, so I only need to learn one." Respond in a way that respects the learner's insight while correcting the oversimplification.

**Hint 1.** Ask different mathematical questions of the same object.

**Hint 2.** Exact calculation and qualitative comparison expose different features.

**Solution.** The learner is correct that the equation and graph represent the same function, and recognising that equivalence is a significant achievement. However, different representations are better suited to different questions. The equation makes it easy to find the output for any input or to solve for the input given the output. The graph makes it easy to see where the function is increasing, where it crosses the axes, and how it compares to other functions. A table makes it easy to spot patterns in discrete data. A verbal description makes the meaning accessible to someone who does not read equations fluently. Proficiency includes choosing the right representation for the question at hand, not merely knowing that they are equivalent.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-5}
A line rises three centimetres for each centimetre to the right. The horizontal scale is two seconds per centimetre and the vertical scale four metres per centimetre. Find the represented speed and explain why the drawn ratio alone is insufficient.

**Hint 1.** Convert each physical length to its labelled quantity.

**Hint 2.** Divide the distance change by the time change.

**Solution.** The displayed rise represents twelve metres and the run two seconds. The speed is six metres per second. The drawn ratio of three centimetres to one centimetre is dimensionless and depends on plotting scales. It is not the speed until the axis conversions are applied. Redrawing the same motion with a different vertical scale changes the angle without changing the coordinates or speed. An assessment of rate should therefore require both the quotient and its units, not a judgement based only on visual steepness.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-6}
A flat contains one hundred cubes and a rod ten. A collection contains one flat, four rods, and seven cubes. Find its value when the rod is one whole, and when the flat is one whole.

**Hint 1.** Assign a value to one cube in each setting.

**Hint 2.** Compute a sum of counts times values.

**Solution.** With the rod as one whole, the flat is ten and each cube one tenth, giving fourteen and seven tenths. With the flat as one whole, each rod is one tenth and each cube one hundredth, giving one and forty-seven hundredths. The physical collection has not changed; its numerical measure changes because the reference unit changes. Neither answer can be selected without the unit convention. A teacher should state that convention explicitly before interpreting a learner's decimal recording.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-7}
Construct a nonlinear polynomial agreeing with $y=2x+1$ at zero, one, two, and three, but not at four. Explain what the construction shows about tables.

**Hint 1.** Multiply the four factors vanishing at the listed inputs.

**Hint 2.** Add that product to the affine rule.

**Solution.** Set $g(x)=2x+1+x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)$. The extra term vanishes at each listed input, so all four rows agree. At four the affine rule gives nine, whereas the product is twenty-four and $g$ gives thirty-three. The table does not uniquely specify a function on the reals. If an affine premise is supplied, two distinct rows do determine its rule; without a model restriction, any finite collection of exact values still leaves other possibilities. This is a counterexample to an inference, not a rejection of using tables to conjecture models.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-8}
Two groups contain twelve and forty-eight people. Each has a quarter represented by a shaded disk sector. If disk area is to encode total population, what radius ratio is needed? What error results from a radius ratio of four?

**Hint 1.** Disk area is proportional to radius squared.

**Hint 2.** The population ratio is four, not sixteen.

**Solution.** The larger disk needs four times the area, so twice the radius. With a radius ratio of four, its area and its quarter-sector area are sixteen times as large, exaggerating the count comparison by a factor of four. The subgroup counts represented by the quarters are three and twelve, so they too have ratio four. Correct area scaling preserves this ratio. Equal-sized disks would instead communicate only equal proportions within the groups, omitting the difference in group sizes. State which of these quantities the display is intended to compare before selecting the encoding.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-9}
Two exact values are forty-nine hundredths and five hundred one thousandths. Explain why rounding both to one decimal place cannot establish their exact order, and give an exact comparison.

**Hint 1.** Determine the two rounded displays.

**Hint 2.** Express both originals in thousandths.

**Solution.** Both round to five tenths, so the rounded representation merges their distinction. Exactly, the first is four hundred ninety thousandths and the second five hundred one thousandths. The second exceeds the first by eleven thousandths. Equality of rounded values does not imply equality of original values. If the goal is approximate reporting to a tenth, the common display may be adequate; if it is exact comparison, it is insufficient. The representation's suitability is relative to the question.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-representations-are-tools-exercise-10}
An accessible replacement for a scatterplot says only “the variables have a positive association.” Identify a mathematical task for which this is sufficient and one for which it is not. Propose additional source information for the second.

**Hint 1.** Distinguish reporting a conclusion from evaluating it.

**Hint 2.** Independent evaluation needs the underlying pairs and scales.

**Solution.** The sentence can answer “what association does the author report?” It cannot by itself support independently judging the pattern, identifying outliers, or checking whether a few points drive the association. Supply a structured table of paired values, variable names, units, and any scale transformations, alongside a faithful description of the plotted encoding. The learner can then examine the evidence rather than merely repeat the interpretation. This preserves the mathematical ambition while changing the access route; it does not imply that any one format is sufficient for every learner or every graphical goal.

## Chapter 4: Examples define the conceptual boundary {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries}

[Chapter context](examples-define-boundaries.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-1}
Design an example set for teaching “isosceles triangle” that includes a boundary pair for each potential misconception: (a) the base must be horizontal and the apex must point up, (b) the triangle must be acute, and (c) exactly two, rather than at least two, sides must be equal.

**Hint 1.** Declare whether the definition is inclusive.

**Hint 2.** Vary orientation and angle type independently of side equality.

**Solution.**

(a) Orientation: Pair a conventional upright isosceles triangle with a rotated isosceles triangle whose base is vertical and whose apex points sideways. Both are examples; orientation is irrelevant.

(b) Angle type: Pair an acute isosceles triangle with an obtuse isosceles triangle whose vertex angle is $120^{\circ}$. Both satisfy the side-length definition.

(c) Inclusive definition: Pair an equilateral example with a scalene nonexample. Under the convention “at least two equal sides,” the equilateral triangle is isosceles; the scalene triangle is not.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-2}
A learner says, "Every rectangle is a square because it has four sides and four right angles." Use the variation framework to design a response that repairs the misconception without simply stating the definition.

**Hint 1.** A rectangle can have unequal adjacent sides.

**Hint 2.** State which conjunction defines a square.

**Solution.** Present a sequence of rectangles: a square, a long thin rectangle, and a rectangle with sides $2$ and $3$. Ask the learner to measure the diagonals. In the square, diagonals are equal and bisect at $90^{\circ}$. In the long thin rectangle, diagonals are equal but do not bisect at $90^{\circ}$. Ask: "If every rectangle were a square, would this difference be possible?" Then present a rhombus with equal sides but non-right angles. Ask: "This has four equal sides. Is it a square? What is missing?" The sequence varies side equality and angle equality separately, making the conjunction "four equal sides AND four right angles" salient.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-3}
Design a sequence of five expressions for teaching the difference between $(a+b)^{2}$ and $a^{2}+b^{2}$. Include examples and nonexamples.

**Hint 1.** Expand before comparing.

**Hint 2.** Find when the cross term vanishes over the reals.

**Solution.**

1. $(3+4)^{2}=49$ versus $3^{2}+4^{2}=25$. Numerical counterexample.
2. $(x+2)^{2}=x^{2}+4x+4$ versus $x^{2}+4$. Algebraic expansion shows the missing middle term.
3. $(a+b)^{2}=a^{2}+2ab+b^{2}$. General identity.
4. $a^{2}+b^{2}$. Nonexample: this is not equal to $(a+b)^{2}$ unless $ab=0$.
5. $(5+0)^{2}=25$ and $5^{2}+0^{2}=25$. Boundary case where the equality holds because one term is zero.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-4}
Explain why the set $\{2,4,6,8,10\}$ is a poor example set for teaching "even number," and propose a better one.

**Hint 1.** Evenness is defined for integers by a factor of two.

**Hint 2.** Include zero, a negative integer, and a noninteger.

**Solution.** The set varies only magnitude, not structure. All examples are positive, greater than $1$, and less than $11$. A learner might overgeneralise that even numbers are positive counting numbers ending in $2,4,6,8,0$. A better set includes: $2$ (smallest positive even), $0$ (boundary), $-4$ (negative), $14$ (two digits), $1$ (nonexample, odd), and $3.5$ (nonexample, not an integer). The improved set tests sign, magnitude, and integer status.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-5}
Under the integer definition that $a$ divides $b$ if $b=ak$ for some integer $k$, classify zero divides zero, zero divides seven, and negative two divides six. Give witnesses or impossibility arguments.

**Hint 1.** Do not replace the definition by division.

**Hint 2.** Every product with a zero factor is zero.

**Solution.** Zero divides zero, with multiplier zero or indeed any integer. Zero does not divide seven because zero times any integer remains zero. Negative two divides six with multiplier negative three. This use of “divides” involves an existential multiplication statement, so it defines no quotient by zero. If an exercise instead excludes zero as a divisor by convention, its first classification changes; a fair task must state which definition is being used. The response cannot be interpreted independently of that convention.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-6}
Explain why one is not prime under the positive-integer definition. Give an example set separating “odd,” “prime,” and “greater than one.”

**Hint 1.** Count distinct positive divisors of one.

**Hint 2.** Include two, an odd composite, and one.

**Solution.** One has only one positive divisor, so it fails the requirement of exactly two distinct positive divisors. Two is prime but not odd. Nine is odd and greater than one but not prime, since three is an additional divisor. One is odd but neither greater than one nor prime. Five supplies an odd prime. These cases refute identifying primality with oddness or with simply exceeding one. Negative five can additionally probe whether the positive domain has been retained, but its role should be explained rather than silently judged by a different ring-theoretic convention.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-7}
Classify the statements “the principal square root of sixteen is four” and “the equation $x^2=16$ has only four as a real solution.” Repair the false statement and explain the distinction.

**Hint 1.** A principal root is nonnegative.

**Hint 2.** Substitution can check both signs in the equation.

**Solution.** The first is true by the definition of the principal square root. The second is false: both four and negative four have square sixteen. Factorisation as $(x-4)(x+4)=0$ shows these are all real solutions. The radical names a single function value; solving the equation asks for its complete solution set on the given domain. If the domain were restricted to nonnegative reals, four would be the only solution. The example set should therefore vary the requested object and state the domain explicitly.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-8}
Give a counterexample to “every quadrilateral with equal diagonals is a rectangle,” verifying equal diagonals exactly. State an added family premise under which the implication is true.

**Hint 1.** Use a symmetric trapezoid rather than a parallelogram.

**Hint 2.** Compare squared coordinate distances.

**Solution.** The vertices $(-2,0),(2,0),(1,1),(-1,1)$ form a simple trapezoid. Each diagonal has squared length ten, but a lower horizontal edge meets a sloping edge at a nonright angle. Thus it is not a rectangle. Within nondegenerate parallelograms, equal diagonals do imply a rectangle: for adjacent vectors $u,v$, equality of the squared lengths of $u+v$ and $u-v$ forces their dot product to be zero. The added premise changes the logical family; the trapezoid is excluded because it is not a parallelogram.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-9}
A learner proposes a scalene triangle as a counterexample to “every square is a rectangle.” Explain why it is not a counterexample and why no counterexample exists under the inclusive definitions.

**Hint 1.** A counterexample must satisfy the premise.

**Hint 2.** Compare the defining conditions of square and rectangle.

**Solution.** A scalene triangle is not a square, so it cannot refute an implication whose premise requires being a square. Every square is a simple quadrilateral with four right angles, which is exactly the rectangle condition plus additional equal-side information. The implication is therefore true by these definitions. A non-square rectangle instead refutes the converse. Asking which premise a proposed object satisfies can distinguish the direction of implication without treating an unusual object as automatically a valid counterexample.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-examples-define-boundaries-exercise-10}
Design a three-object boundary set for the statement “a real number equals its absolute value.” Explain the complete classification and one limitation of the finite set.

**Hint 1.** Include a positive input, zero, and a negative input.

**Hint 2.** Use the piecewise definition for the general result.

**Solution.** Use three, zero, and negative three. The first two equal their absolute values; the third does not. In general absolute value equals the input for nonnegative inputs, and equals its opposite for negative inputs. A negative input cannot equal its opposite unless it is zero, which contradicts negativity. Thus the exact boundary is nonnegativity. The three cases illustrate that boundary but do not alone prove the universal classification; the piecewise definition supplies the exhaustive reasoning. A learner's correct labels on those three examples still need an explanation before general understanding is inferred.

<a id="chapter-5-a-misconception-is-not-just-a-wrong-answer"></a>
## Chapter 5: A misconception is not just a wrong answer {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic}

[Chapter context](misconceptions-are-systematic.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-1}
A learner writes $\frac{3}{5}+\frac{2}{5}=\frac{5}{10}=\frac{1}{2}$. Diagnose the misconception and design a three-step repair.

**Hint 1.** Separate the visible operation from possible reasons for using it.

**Hint 2.** Keep the size of a fifth fixed across the model.

**Solution.** The learner may be overgeneralising the "add tops and bottoms" rule, or may be confusing fraction addition with fraction simplification. Alternatively, the learner may think that the denominator represents the total number of parts in the combined whole.

Step 1: Test with a concrete model. Draw two circles each divided into fifths. Shade $3/5$ of one and $2/5$ of the other. Ask whether the shaded amount is half of one circle or more than half.

Step 2: Connect to unit reasoning. "These are fifths. How many fifths are shaded altogether? If you had three apples and two apples, would you have five apples or five tenths of an apple?"

Step 3: Generalise. "When the denominators are the same, we are counting the same-sized pieces. We add the counts, not the sizes." Then test transfer with $\frac{2}{7}+\frac{3}{7}$ and a contrast with $\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}$.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-2}
Explain why the misconception "multiplication always makes bigger" is sensible, and give a numerical counterexample that would help repair it.

**Hint 1.** State the sign and domain of the original quantity.

**Hint 2.** Compare a factor above, equal to, and below one.

**Solution.** Multiplying a positive whole number by a whole-number factor greater than one increases it. The learner has correctly generalised from a large and familiar domain. A counterexample should be chosen to make the violation salient. Consider $0.5\times 8=4$. In a measurement context: "You have $8$ metres of ribbon. You need half that length. How much do you cut?" The result, $4$ metres, is smaller than $8$. Another effective counterexample is multiplication by $1$: $1\times 8=8$, which is neither bigger nor smaller. And multiplication by $0$: $0\times 8=0$. The sequence $2\times 8=16$, $1\times 8=8$, $0.5\times 8=4$, $0\times 8=0$ shows the continuum from enlargement to reduction to elimination.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-3}
A learner says, "$0.25$ is bigger than $0.5$ because $25$ is bigger than $5$." Design a representational contrast that repairs this misconception.

**Hint 1.** Rename both quantities in hundredths of one common whole.

**Hint 2.** A currency representation requires contextual familiarity.

**Solution.** Use a money representation: $0.25$ dollars is a quarter, $0.5$ dollars is a half-dollar. Place both coins on a table and ask which has greater value. Then use a number line from $0$ to $1$, marking $0.25$ and $0.5$. The visual distance from $0$ makes the order clear. Finally, connect to place value: $0.25=\frac{25}{100}$ and $0.5=\frac{50}{100}$. The denominator is the same, so the numerators can be compared directly. The stated explanation compares digit strings as whole numbers. The common-unit representation addresses that explanation; it does not prove that the proposed teaching sequence will change future responses. A money example also requires familiarity with the currency, so a labelled length or area model may be more accessible.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-4}
Why is it insufficient to tell a learner "that's wrong, here's the right answer" when a misconception is diagnosed?

**Hint 1.** A correct answer alone does not identify a reasoning rule.

**Hint 2.** Ask what evidence would distinguish a stable pattern from a slip.

**Solution.** A misconception is a coherent, stable model of how mathematics works. Telling the learner the right answer adds a correct procedure without displacing the incorrect model. The learner may now have two competing procedures---one intuitive and one imposed---and may apply the wrong one under pressure or in unfamiliar contexts. Repair requires making the learner's current model explicit, testing its limits with counterexamples, and building a replacement model that is more general and more consistent with evidence. This is why contrast, concrete testing, and transfer problems are essential.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-5}
Classify exactly when multiplying a negative real number by a real factor produces a larger number in the usual order. Contrast this with increasing its magnitude.

**Hint 1.** Subtract the original from the product.

**Hint 2.** For magnitude, use the absolute value of the factor.

**Solution.** If $x<0$, then $kx>x$ exactly when $(k-1)x>0$. Dividing by negative $x$ reverses the inequality, giving $k<1$. For magnitude, $|kx|=|k||x|$, and the positive value $|x|$ can be cancelled without reversing order, so magnitude increases exactly when $|k|>1$. A factor of three therefore decreases a negative input in order while increasing its magnitude. The distinction prevents replacing an imprecise phrase with another unrestricted rule.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-6}
Repair $6+7=13+2=15$ in two ways, preserving the intended running calculation. Give a follow-up probing relational equality without assuming a diagnosis.

**Hint 1.** Either include all terms from the outset or separate the stages.

**Hint 2.** Ask whether each neighbouring pair has the same value.

**Solution.** A valid chain is $6+7+2=13+2=15$. Alternatively write $6+7=13$ and $13+2=15$ as separate equations. In the original chain six plus seven is thirteen, whereas thirteen plus two is fifteen, so the first equality is false. A follow-up such as $6+7=8+\square$ requires five in the box and an explanation that both sides must equal thirteen. This probes the relation; it does not establish from the original notation alone whether the learner held a stable answer-sign interpretation or simply recorded stages carelessly.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-7}
Solve $\sqrt{x+6}=x$ over the real numbers and explain why squaring requires a check.

**Hint 1.** The right side must be nonnegative.

**Hint 2.** Factor the squared equation and inspect both candidates.

**Solution.** Any solution has $x\geq0$. Squaring gives $x^2-x-6=0$, which factors as $(x-3)(x+2)=0$. Three works in the original equation; negative two does not because its right side is negative while the left is two. Hence three is the only solution. Squaring preserves the original equality in one direction but can equate opposite-sign values, so it can introduce a candidate. The valid factorisation should be retained when discussing the extra root; the missing warrant concerns reversibility and sign.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-8}
A learner gives three sevenths for one third plus two fourths. Name three possible explanations and design a follow-up separating at least two of them.

**Hint 1.** One written result is compatible with several processes.

**Hint 2.** Ask for an explanation and use equal-denominator addition as a contrast.

**Solution.** The learner may intentionally add numerators and denominators, may be recording the fraction of selected parts across differently partitioned objects, or may have copied an unrelated denominator. Ask for the intended rule and present one half plus one half of the same unit. Componentwise addition predicts one half, while counting halves gives one. A request to identify the whole and the piece sizes can distinguish the first two accounts. These follow-ups do not guarantee an unambiguous diagnosis; record the actual explanation rather than simply choosing the most familiar misconception label.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-9}
A learner says that $0.400$ exceeds $0.4$ because it has more digits. Give an exact argument and propose a contrast that does not rely on currency knowledge.

**Hint 1.** Express both in thousandths.

**Hint 2.** Fix a single unit length before subdividing it.

**Solution.** Four tenths equals four hundred thousandths, so the decimals denote the same number. On a unit-length strip, mark four tenths, then subdivide each tenth into one hundred equal parts. The same marked length covers four hundred of the resulting thousandths. Compare also four hundred one thousandths, which is strictly larger by one thousandth. This varies numerical value while keeping the representation comparable. The proposed contrast addresses the stated digit-length rule; it supplies no measured evidence about whether that rule persists after instruction.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-misconceptions-are-systematic-exercise-10}
Explain why a counterexample settles a universal mathematical claim but does not settle the cause of a learner's response or the success of an intervention. Use one concrete example.

**Hint 1.** Separate three different claims: truth, diagnosis, and change.

**Hint 2.** A later correct answer can arise without a revised general rule.

**Solution.** Half of eight is four, so it falsifies the unrestricted real-number claim that multiplication always increases its positive input. It does not show why a particular learner predicted an increase: domain overgeneralisation, a misread factor, and a computation slip remain possible. If the learner later answers this same item correctly, that may reflect memory of the example rather than a general rule about factors between zero and one. Additional explanations and new-domain tasks can investigate transfer, but neither the mathematical counterexample nor one corrected answer establishes a causal instructional effect. Those are different evidential questions.

<a id="chapter-6-learning-progressions-are-hypotheses"></a>
## Chapter 6: Learning progressions are hypotheses {#solutions-learning-progressions}

[Chapter context](learning-progressions.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-1}
A learner computes $100-37$ by counting up from $37$ to $100$: $37$ to $40$ is $3$, $40$ to $100$ is $60$, so the answer is $63$. Describe the reasoning visible here, state what the response does not establish, and suggest a follow-up that tests a place-value hypothesis.

**Hint 1.** Counting up measures a difference without requiring the written subtraction algorithm.

**Hint 2.** Keep the visible strategy separate from a hypothesised stable stage.

**Solution.** Counting up computes the difference as the distance from $37$ to $100$: $3+60=63$. This is valid and efficient. It is consistent with coordinating tens and ones, but one successful calculation does not establish a stable progression stage or show how the learner would explain another decomposition. Ask the learner to show the two jumps on a number line and explain $100-37=(60+40)-(30+7)=63$. Then try $83-46$: the jumps $46\to50\to80\to83$ total $4+30+3=37$. Compare that explanation with regrouping, recording any prompts needed. The aim is to test and connect the learner's reasoning rather than force one written method or assign a fixed label.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-2}
Explain why a learner who understands fractions only as "parts of a whole" might struggle with $\frac{5}{4}$.

**Hint 1.** A single bounded whole does not contain five of its own four parts.

**Hint 2.** Repeated unit intervals or equal sharing can extend the interpretation.

**Solution.** If a fraction is defined as a part of a single whole, then $\frac{5}{4}$ is impossible: you cannot take five parts from a whole divided into four. The learner needs to expand their conception to include fractions as measures on the number line (where $\frac{5}{4}$ is simply a point to the right of $1$) or as quotients (where $\frac{5}{4}$ is the result of dividing $5$ by $4$). Without this expansion, the learner may write $\frac{5}{4}=1\frac{1}{4}$ mechanically without understanding that it represents a quantity greater than one.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-3}
Design a brief assessment that tests whether a learner can explain and transfer a quantity-preserving regrouping, while keeping a progression-stage judgment provisional.

**Hint 1.** An exchange preserves a quantity; compensation preserves a difference.

**Hint 2.** Use a changed example and record the supports supplied.

**Solution.** Begin with regrouping itself: ask the learner to show $43$ with tens and ones, exchange one ten for ten ones without changing the total, and explain $43=4\times10+3=3\times10+13$. Then subtract $18$ using that representation:

$$43-18=(30+13)-(10+8)=20+5=25.$$

Invite a drawing or objects as well as speech. Change the case to $52-27$: $52=40+12$ gives $(40+12)-(20+7)=20+5=25$. Record what the learner preserves and which prompts or materials are needed.

A separate comparison can investigate compensation: "Why do $43-18$ and $33-8$ have the same difference?" Subtracting $10$ from both numbers gives

$$43-18=(43-10)-(18-10)=33-8=25.$$

That explanation preserves a difference; the tens/ones exchange preserves the minuend's value. Both are valid, but a compensation explanation alone does not answer the regrouping question. Keep their evidence separate. A single correct or unsuccessful explanation does not establish a fixed progression stage.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-4}
A teacher says, "My year-seven class is still at the part-whole stage for fractions, so I cannot teach them fraction multiplication yet." Critique this reasoning.

**Hint 1.** A proposed route is not an admission requirement.

**Hint 2.** Half of a half provides a meaningful multiplication case within a part-whole model.

**Solution.** The teacher is committing the checkpoint trap. While part-whole understanding is limited, learners at that stage can still explore fraction multiplication in concrete contexts. For example, "half of a half" is accessible through paper folding and connects to the part-whole model. The teacher might introduce multiplication as an extension of "finding a part of a part" before learners have fully developed the measure or operator conceptions. Progressions are hypotheses about sophistication, not barriers to exposure. The teacher should ask what fraction multiplication means in a part-whole context rather than assuming it is impossible.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-5}
Prove the one-group exchange identity in base $b\geq2$ and use it to subtract the base-five numeral one-three from three-one.

**Hint 1.** Replace one group by five units in the example.

**Hint 2.** Check the result by converting both original numerals to decimal values.

**Solution.** Distributivity gives $b(t-1)+(u+b)=bt-b+u+b=bt+u$. In base five, three-one is three groups and one unit. Exchange to two groups and six units; removing one group and three units leaves one group and three units, the numeral one-three. The original decimal values are sixteen and eight, and the result has value eight, confirming the difference. Six is an intermediate unit count, not a legal final base-five digit. This distinguishes the value-preserving representation from standard numeral syntax.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-6}
Distinguish regrouping forty-three as thirty plus thirteen from changing forty-three minus eighteen to thirty-three minus eight. State the invariant in each.

**Hint 1.** Ask whether one number changes or both arguments change.

**Hint 2.** Expand each expression algebraically.

**Solution.** Regrouping uses $43=30+13$, preserving the minuend's value while changing its decomposition. Compensation uses $(43-10)-(18-10)=43-18$, preserving the difference while changing both numbers. Both lead to twenty-five but provide different evidence about reasoning. A learner who explains compensation has not thereby explained the exchange of one ten for ten ones. A useful follow-up asks for both invariants and their connection rather than requiring one method as the sole sign of understanding.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-7}
Explain five quarters as a measure, a quotient, and an operator on twelve litres. State why these are connected without being identical tasks.

**Hint 1.** Keep the relevant unit or input explicit.

**Hint 2.** For equal sharing, distribute five units among four recipients.

**Solution.** As measure it is five quarter-unit intervals from zero, located at one and a quarter units. As quotient, sharing five unit quantities among four people gives each five quarters of a unit. As an operator on twelve litres, it gives fifteen litres: one quarter is three litres and five quarters are fifteen. The same rational coefficient occurs, but the operator's output depends on the input quantity. Success in one task supplies evidence about that interpretation, not an automatic certificate for all others or a required order of acquisition.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-8}
A learner proves that two positive odd counts sum to an even count by pairing objects. What must be added to cover all odd integers, including negative ones?

**Hint 1.** Literal object counts do not represent negative cardinalities.

**Hint 2.** Use arbitrary integer parameters in the odd-number definition.

**Solution.** Write arbitrary odd integers as $2m+1$ and $2n+1$ with integer parameters, which may be negative. Their sum is twice $m+n+1$, an integer, and is therefore even. This extends the domain without inventing negative collections of objects. The original pairing argument remains valid for its count domain. The next step is a mathematical domain extension, not evidence that the learner must abandon visual reasoning or pass through a fixed global stage.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-9}
A proposed progression says every learner must master number-line fractions before operator fractions. A learner repeatedly solves operator tasks but needs help plotting fractions. What can and cannot be concluded?

**Hint 1.** Distinguish a strict prerequisite claim from a typical-route claim.

**Hint 2.** Examine whether the tasks and support conditions are comparable.

**Solution.** If the observations are secure and the tasks represent the intended meanings, they challenge the strict universal prerequisite claim for those conditions. They do not show that number-line work is useless, that the learner understands every operator task, or that typical-route hypotheses are all false. Record the actual tasks, explanations, and supports; investigate whether plotting access or scale interpretation contributes to the difficulty. A revised route can connect the successful operator meaning to measure without withholding further fraction work until a supposed checkpoint is passed.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-learning-progressions-exercise-10}
Design a record for a learner who solves a regrouping problem only after a teacher supplies the exchange. Explain why the record should not simply say “regrouping mastered.”

**Hint 1.** Separate selecting the exchange from executing it.

**Hint 2.** Record both the prompt and the mathematical explanation given.

**Solution.** Record the original numbers, the teacher's supplied decomposition, the learner's subsequent subtraction, and whether the learner explained why the decomposition preserves value. State that the learner completed the calculation with an exchange prompt. Selecting an exchange independently, executing a supplied exchange, and justifying it are distinct pieces of evidence. A changed problem without the same prompt could investigate independence, with accessible representation options maintained. The initial supported success is useful, but it does not establish a stable stage, general transfer, or a causal effect of the prompt.

<a id="chapter-10-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-is-specialised"></a>
## Chapter 7: Teacher mathematical knowledge is specialised {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge}

[Chapter context](teacher-mathematical-knowledge.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-1}
A learner says, "Dividing by a fraction is easy: just flip the second fraction and multiply." Explain why this rule is insufficient for teaching and what additional knowledge a teacher needs.

**Hint 1.** A rule needs a reason and a domain.

**Hint 2.** Relate the operation to a uniquely solvable missing-factor equation.

**Solution.** The rule is computationally efficient but conceptually opaque. A learner who applies it without understanding may struggle with problems like $\frac{1}{2}\div 3$ (where there is no obvious "second fraction" to flip) or may invert the wrong fraction. The teacher needs to know why the rule works (multiplication by the reciprocal is the inverse of multiplication by the original fraction), when it applies (nonzero divisors), and how to explain it at different levels (measurement, missing factor, algebraic derivation). The teacher also needs to know common errors, such as inverting the dividend instead of the divisor, and how to diagnose them.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-2}
A learner solves $\frac{2}{3}\div\frac{1}{6}$ by drawing a rectangle divided into thirds, then splitting each third into two equal parts of the original whole, and counting that there are four sixths in two-thirds. Evaluate this method mathematically and pedagogically.

**Hint 1.** Each third must become two parts, each one sixth of the original whole.

**Hint 2.** Check what unit the diagram counts.

**Solution.** Mathematically, the method is correct with that unit convention. Each third splits into two original-whole sixths; splitting a third into six equal pieces would instead produce original-whole eighteenths. Two-thirds of the whole contains four one-sixths because $\frac{2}{3}=\frac{4}{6}$. Pedagogically, the method is powerful because it connects the abstract operation to a concrete visual representation. It demonstrates the measurement interpretation of division. However, it may be difficult to extend to cases where the dividend is not a multiple of the divisor, such as $\frac{1}{2}\div\frac{1}{3}$. The teacher should praise the method, connect it to the numerical answer, and ask whether the same drawing would work for $\frac{1}{2}\div\frac{1}{3}$.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-3}
Explain why a teacher who knows only the algebraic derivation of the quadratic formula may struggle to help a learner who completes the square geometrically.

**Hint 1.** Map every added algebraic term to a geometric piece.

**Hint 2.** State when a literal area model needs nonnegative lengths.

**Solution.** The algebraic derivation manipulates symbols: $ax^{2}+bx+c=0$, divide by $a$, complete the square, isolate $x$. The geometric completion of the square involves arranging $x^{2}$ and $bx$ terms into a square with a missing corner, then adding the corner to complete it. A teacher who knows only the algebraic derivation may not recognise the geometric reasoning as valid, may not see how the "add and subtract $(b/2a)^{2}$" step corresponds to filling in the missing corner, and may inadvertently dismiss a correct but unconventional method. Specialised knowledge includes understanding multiple derivations and their connections.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-4}
A teacher is asked why the area of a triangle is $\frac{1}{2}bh$. Give three explanations at increasing levels of sophistication, and identify the prerequisite knowledge for each.

**Hint 1.** Specify the geometric hypotheses of each explanation.

**Hint 2.** For an outside altitude, use a difference of containing right triangles.

**Solution.**

1. **Dissection:** Cut a triangle and rearrange the pieces to form a rectangle of half the height. Prerequisite: understanding of area conservation under rearrangement.

2. **Duplicate and form a parallelogram:** Make a copy of the triangle, rotate it $180^{\circ}$, and fit it with the original to form a parallelogram of base $b$ and height $h$. The parallelogram has area $bh$, so the triangle has half that. Prerequisite: knowing that the area of a parallelogram is $bh$.

3. **Calculus:** Assume $b>0$, $h>0$, and $0<p<b$. The upper boundary is piecewise linear, so

$$\int_0^p\frac{h}{p}x\,dx+\int_p^b\frac{h}{b-p}(b-x)\,dx
=\frac{hp}{2}+\frac{h(b-p)}{2}=\frac{bh}{2}.$$

The endpoint cases $p=0$ or $p=b$ use only the corresponding one-piece integral. Prerequisite: integral calculus.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-5}
Evaluate the componentwise division method on zero fifths divided by two thirds. Explain why reversing the two fractions changes the domain status.

**Hint 1.** A zero dividend is permitted.

**Hint 2.** A zero divisor cannot determine a unique quotient.

**Solution.** The original quotient is zero. Componentwise division gives zero divided by two in the numerator and five divided by three in the denominator, hence zero, with no undefined intermediate operation. Reversing the original fractions asks for division by zero fifths, which is zero. The missing-factor equation would require zero times a candidate to equal two thirds, impossible over the rationals. Thus the reverse quotient is undefined. Merely banning every written zero would reject the valid first calculation and conceal the actual divisor restriction.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-6}
A triangle has base endpoints $(0,0),(4,0)$ and third vertex $(7,3)$. Prove its area without treating the sloping side as the height.

**Hint 1.** The perpendicular altitude meets the base line beyond the segment.

**Hint 2.** Subtract a smaller right triangle from a larger one.

**Solution.** The right triangle spanning horizontal distance seven and height three has area twenty-one halves. Remove the right triangle spanning distance three from four to seven and the same height, whose area is nine halves. The remainder is the given triangle, of area six. Equivalently half of base four times perpendicular height three is six. The sloping sides have different lengths and are not perpendicular to the chosen base, so they cannot replace its height. A diagram should show the extended base line and name the containing regions used in the subtraction.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-7}
Solve $x^2+4x=12$ over the reals by completing the square. Explain what an ordinary positive-length area picture captures and what requires algebraic extension.

**Hint 1.** Add four to both sides.

**Hint 2.** A square equation has two possible signs for its base expression.

**Solution.** Adding four gives $(x+2)^2=16$, so $x+2=4$ or $x+2=-4$. The roots are two and negative six, both confirmed by substitution. For nonnegative $x$, a square and two width-two strips explain the added corner of area four and the positive-length root. The negative root belongs to the real polynomial equation but is outside that literal side-length model. Distributivity proves the completed-square identity for all real inputs, allowing the algebraic solution to cover both signs without inventing a negative geometric length.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-8}
Explain two thirds divided by one fourth using a common measure. State the quotient's unit and connect it to a missing-factor equation.

**Hint 1.** Measure both quantities in twelfths of the same whole.

**Hint 2.** Ask how many three-twelfth units make eight twelfths.

**Solution.** Two thirds is eight twelfths and one fourth three twelfths. Their quotient is eight thirds: that many quarter-whole units make two thirds of the original whole. The missing-factor equation is one fourth times the quotient equals two thirds; multiplying by four gives eight thirds. The numerical quotient is dimensionless when both quantities have the same physical unit, but it counts divisor-sized units. Distinguishing the original whole from the new measuring unit prevents interpreting the answer as eight thirds of the original quantity without specifying what is counted.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-9}
A learner uses the valid unfamiliar fraction-division method in a lesson targeting measurement interpretation. How should a teacher assess its correctness and its alignment with the goal separately?

**Hint 1.** Verify the algebra before making a pedagogical judgement.

**Hint 2.** Ask what relation to the divisor-sized unit remains unshown.

**Solution.** First verify the method under nonzero denominator and divisor conditions, explaining why its value agrees with reciprocal multiplication. State that the mathematics is valid. Then ask the learner to interpret the quotient as a count of divisor-sized units, using a common measure or the missing-factor equation. The original calculation may not yet provide that evidence. A method can be correct without fully displaying the targeted connection; conversely, failing to use the teacher's preferred algorithm is not an error. The follow-up should address the unshown relation rather than erase valid learner work.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-teacher-mathematical-knowledge-exercise-10}
Choose an explanation that is valid in a restricted domain but insufficient as a proof in a broader one. State its domain, the unproved extension, and a bridge that supplies the missing warrant.

**Hint 1.** Area multiplication or repeated addition are suitable choices.

**Hint 2.** Name an algebraic law rather than extrapolating from more pictures.

**Solution.** A rectangle of nonnegative sides illustrates multiplication and an area split illustrates distributivity for nonnegative lengths. It does not prove distributivity for all signed real inputs, because an ordinary rectangle has no negative side length or unsigned negative area. State distributivity as an adopted real-number law, then derive sign rules using additive inverses, for example $r(-s)+rs=r((-s)+s)=0$. This connects the restricted illustration to a general algebraic warrant without presenting the picture as a proof outside its domain. Other choices are valid if they state the same three boundaries explicitly.

<a id="chapter-7-problem-solving-can-be-taught-without-reducing-it-to-a-mnemonic"></a>
## Chapter 8: Problem solving can be taught without reducing it to a mnemonic {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving}

[Chapter context](teaching-problem-solving.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-1}
A learner is taught that "fewer than" always means subtract. They encounter the problem: "Alice has $12$ apples, which is $5$ fewer than Bob. How many does Bob have?" Predict the error and design a repair.

**Hint 1.** Translate who has fewer before choosing an operation.

**Hint 2.** Check the proposed answer against the comparison sentence.

**Solution.** A keyword-based response could subtract: $12-5=7$. That response is consistent with the proposed rule, but an actual learner might instead have misread who has fewer. Ask for their interpretation before attributing the cause. The repair is to use a bar model or equation: $A=B-5$, so $12=B-5$, hence $B=17$. The teacher should ask: "Who has more, Alice or Bob? If Alice has fewer, can Bob have fewer too?" This forces attention to the relationship, not the word.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-2}
Solve the following problem using the plan-monitor-evaluate framework, and identify where a learner might get stuck.

> The sum of three consecutive integers is $48$. What are the integers?

**Hint 1.** Represent all three integers from one parameter.

**Hint 2.** Verify consecutiveness as well as the sum.

**Solution.**

**Plan:** Let the integers be $n$, $n+1$, $n+2$. Their sum is $3n+3=48$.

**Monitor:** Solve $3n+3=48$. Subtract $3$: $3n=45$. Divide by $3$: $n=15$. The integers are $15,16,17$.

**Evaluate:** Check: $15+16+17=48$. Yes. Also check that they are consecutive: $16-15=1$, $17-16=1$.

A learner might get stuck at the planning stage by trying to guess three numbers rather than representing them algebraically. Another might write $n+(n+1)+(n+2)=48$ but make an arithmetic error in solving. A third might find $n=15$ but forget to list all three integers, answering "$15$" instead of "$15,16,17$."

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-3}
Explain why the algebraic identity $ab=((a+b)/2)^{2}-((a-b)/2)^{2}$ proves that for fixed sum, area is maximised when $a=b$.

**Hint 1.** Hold the sum term fixed and inspect the squared difference.

**Hint 2.** Separate the real optimum from an integer restriction.

**Solution.** For fixed sum $S=a+b$, the term $((a+b)/2)^{2}=(S/2)^{2}$ is constant. The area $ab$ is therefore equal to a constant minus $((a-b)/2)^{2}$. Since $((a-b)/2)^{2}$ is always non-negative, subtracting it reduces the area. The reduction is smallest---in fact, zero---when $a-b=0$, that is, when $a=b$. Therefore the maximum area occurs when the sides are equal. This proof is more powerful than the table because it applies to all real numbers, not just integers.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-4}
A teacher gives learners the mnemonic "Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally" (PEMDAS) for order of operations. A learner computes $8-3+2$ as $8-(3+2)=3$. Diagnose the error and discuss whether the mnemonic is responsible.

**Hint 1.** Addition and subtraction share precedence.

**Hint 2.** Reading an incorrect result does not uniquely establish its cause.

**Solution.** One possible explanation is interpreting PEMDAS as “addition before subtraction” because $A$ comes before $S$. The displayed work alone does not establish that cause. In fact, addition and subtraction have equal precedence and are evaluated left to right. The mnemonic is partly responsible because it suggests a hierarchy that does not exist between operations of equal precedence. The repair is to teach that addition and subtraction are inverse operations at the same level, and that the correct reading of $8-3+2$ is $(8-3)+2=7$. A more accurate representation is grouping: terms are separated by $+$ and $-$, so $8-3+2$ is $8+(-3)+2$.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-5}
Prove that an eight-by-eight board with opposite-corner squares removed cannot be covered by edge-adjacent dominoes. State why unsuccessful search is insufficient.

**Hint 1.** Colour the board with alternating colours.

**Hint 2.** Count what each legal domino must cover.

**Solution.** Opposite corners have the same colour. Removing them leaves thirty squares of that colour and thirty-two of the other. Every domino covers exactly one of each, so any covering would require equal colour counts, a contradiction. A finite list of failed arrangements does not cover every possible arrangement unless exhaustiveness is proved. The colouring argument supplies the universal obstruction directly. A drawing should identify the colour convention and removed squares so that the counts can be checked rather than accepted from the picture alone.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-6}
Give a covering when the two adjacent leftmost top-row squares are removed instead. Explain why the existence proof differs from the impossibility proof.

**Hint 1.** Begin with a horizontal covering of the full board.

**Hint 2.** Remove exactly the domino occupying the deleted pair.

**Solution.** Pair columns one with two, three with four, five with six, and seven with eight in every row. This covers the full board by thirty-two horizontal dominoes. Remove the first pair in the top row; the remaining thirty-one cover precisely the remaining squares without overlap. One complete construction proves existence. The opposite-corner problem instead requires ruling out every construction, which the colour imbalance accomplishes. The logical demand changes with the claim, even though both tasks use the same board and pieces.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-7}
Find every nonnegative integer solution of $3a+5b=30$. Explain why your list is complete.

**Hint 1.** Bound $b$ using nonnegativity.

**Hint 2.** Reduce the equation modulo three.

**Solution.** Nonnegativity gives $0\leq b\leq6$. Modulo three, twice $b$ must be zero, so $b$ is a multiple of three. The only choices are zero, three, and six. Substitution yields $(a,b)=(10,0),(5,3),(0,6)$. Each pair satisfies the equation and bounds. Every solution must have one of the three permitted values of $b$, so none are omitted. Supplying only one pair would answer an existence question but not this exhaustive classification.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-8}
Seventy-one objects are packed into boxes holding at most twelve each. Find the minimum number of boxes, then answer how many full twelve-object sets can be formed. Explain the different rounding directions.

**Hint 1.** Capacity and complete-group counting ask different questions.

**Hint 2.** Compare five and six boxes with the total.

**Solution.** Five boxes hold only sixty objects, while six hold seventy-two, so six are necessary and sufficient. An allocation with five full boxes and eleven objects in the sixth witnesses sufficiency. Only five complete twelve-object sets can be formed, with eleven left over; six complete sets would require seventy-two objects. Upward rounding answers the capacity question, downward rounding the complete-set question. Neither direction follows from division alone without interpreting the output constraint.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-9}
A teacher supplies the token equation before learners solve it. Which problem-solving evidence has been scaffolded away, and what meaningful evidence can remain?

**Hint 1.** Distinguish formulation from solving an already formulated constraint.

**Hint 2.** Completeness still needs an argument.

**Solution.** Selecting variables and translating the value context into an equation have been supplied. Learners may still bound integer counts, choose a modular or tabular strategy, find candidates, check them, and establish that the list is exhaustive. Those are substantive mathematical achievements. Record the equation prompt so the work is not later described as independent formulation. To investigate formulation separately, give a changed context without the equation while preserving accessible language and notation. No claim that one scaffold always improves learning follows from this analysis.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-teaching-problem-solving-exercise-10}
For the fixed-sum rectangle problem, identify what a table, a feasible pair, and a balancing proof each establish. Explain why “I checked my answer” is underspecified.

**Hint 1.** Existence and optimality are different obligations.

**Hint 2.** A table needs an exhaustive range to prove a maximum.

**Solution.** A feasible pair establishes that at least one rectangle satisfies the constraints and has the stated area. A complete table of all unordered positive integer pairs, with correct products, establishes the integer maximum. A partial table only compares its listed cases. A balancing proof shows that any pair with a gap of at least two can improve, forcing an optimum to the nearest equal pair. “Checked” could refer merely to recomputing a product, verifying the perimeter, or proving optimality. State which obligation the evidence actually meets before choosing the next teaching question.

## Chapter 9: Proof teaching coordinates examples and necessity {#solutions-proof-teaching}

[Chapter context](proof-teaching.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-1}
A learner conjectures that $n^{2}-n+41$ is prime for all positive integers $n$ after checking $n=1,2,3,4,5$. Exhibit a counterexample and explain what it teaches about the role of examples.

**Hint 1.** Choose an input making the polynomial a visible product.

**Hint 2.** One counterexample refutes universality without being the smallest.

**Solution.** For $n=41$, we have $41^{2}-41+41=41^{2}=1681$, which is composite ($41\times 41$). This exhibits a counterexample; no claim that it is the smallest is needed. A smallest-counterexample claim would also require ruling out every smaller allowed input. One counterexample already shows that a pattern holding for many cases does not guarantee universality. The polynomial was chosen precisely because it produces primes for a long initial run, making it a classic illustration of the gap between empirical evidence and proof.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-2}
Prove by induction that the sum of the first $n$ positive integers is $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. Then give a visual proof using a triangular array of dots.

**Hint 1.** State a base case and a step covering every successor.

**Hint 2.** Two matching dot triangles form a rectangle.

**Solution.**

**Inductive proof:**

Base case: $n=1$ gives $1=\frac{1\cdot 2}{2}$.

Assume $1+2+\cdots+k=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}$. Add $k+1$ to both sides:

$$1+2+\cdots+k+(k+1)=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+(k+1)=\frac{k(k+1)+2(k+1)}{2}=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}.$$

Thus the statement holds for $k+1$, and by induction for all $n$.

**Visual proof:** Arrange $n$ rows of dots, with $1$ dot in the first row, $2$ in the second, ..., $n$ in the $n$-th. This forms a triangle. Make a copy, rotate it $180^{\circ}$, and fit the two together. The result is an $n\times(n+1)$ rectangle with $n(n+1)$ dots. Since the rectangle comprises two identical triangles, each triangle has $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ dots.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-3}
A learner says, "I checked ten cases, so I'm pretty sure it's true. Why do I need a proof?" Respond in a way that respects the learner's intuition while explaining the necessity of proof.

**Hint 1.** Respect conjecturing while identifying the unverified cases.

**Hint 2.** Separate confidence from a warrant covering the entire domain.

**Solution.** Checking cases is a powerful way to discover patterns and build confidence. Many famous conjectures were found this way. But mathematics aims for certainty, not just high probability. A statement that is true for ten cases, or a million cases, might fail at the next case. Proof is what turns a pattern into knowledge. Moreover, a good proof explains *why* the pattern holds, which can reveal connections to other ideas and suggest new questions. Checking selected cases establishes those instances and can suggest a general explanation. A proof warrants the stated generality, though not every proof is equally explanatory for every reader.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-4}
Explain why the statement "For all integers $n$, if $n^{2}$ is even then $n$ is even" cannot be proved by examples alone, and give a proof by contraposition.

**Hint 1.** Write an arbitrary odd integer in a definitional form.

**Hint 2.** The contrapositive has the same truth conditions as the original implication.

**Solution.** The statement is universal over infinitely many integers, so no finite set of examples can cover all cases. A single unchecked odd integer with an even square would falsify the statement, and examples give no assurance that such an integer does not exist.

**Proof by contraposition.** Assume $n$ is odd. Then $n=2k+1$ for some integer $k$, and

$$n^{2}=(2k+1)^{2}=4k^{2}+4k+1=2(2k^{2}+2k)+1,$$

which is odd. Therefore, if $n$ is odd, $n^{2}$ is odd. By contrapositive, if $n^{2}$ is even, $n$ is even.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-5}
Prove that every integer square has remainder zero or one modulo four. Explain why two generic cases suffice while two numerical examples do not.

**Hint 1.** Every integer is even or odd.

**Hint 2.** Expand each general form and retain an integer multiplier.

**Solution.** If $n=2k$, then $n^2=4k^2$. If $n=2k+1$, then $n^2=4(k^2+k)+1$. In both cases the parenthesised multiplier is an integer. Every integer has one of these forms, so the cases exhaust the domain. Zero and one each occur, for instance at inputs zero and one. Two isolated calculations lack the coverage argument; the generic forms provide it. A finite case split can therefore prove an infinite-domain claim when each case represents all members of its class.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-6}
Identify the precise failure in the same-colour horses induction. Why does checking the base case alone not protect an induction argument?

**Hint 1.** Inspect the transition from one object to two.

**Hint 2.** The alleged overlap must actually exist.

**Solution.** For a two-horse group, removing the first or last leaves disjoint singleton groups. No horse lies in both, so their colours need not agree. The asserted induction step therefore fails at the first transition. A true base case proves only that starting case; induction also requires a valid implication from every covered case to its successor. The overlap argument works only at later sizes conditional on a hypothesis that has not been established. Adding the missing two-horse base case would require a false assertion rather than repairing the proof.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-7}
Prove by induction that three divides $n^3-n$ for all nonnegative integers. Identify exactly where the induction hypothesis is used.

**Hint 1.** Subtract the expression at $k$ from the one at $k+1$.

**Hint 2.** A sum of multiples of three remains a multiple of three.

**Solution.** At zero the expression is zero. Assume $k^3-k=3m$ for an integer $m$. Expansion gives $(k+1)^3-(k+1)=(k^3-k)+3k^2+3k=3(m+k^2+k)$. The final multiplier is an integer, proving the next case. The hypothesis supplies the first term's divisibility; the difference contributes another multiple of three. The step works for every nonnegative $k$, including zero, so induction covers the whole stated domain. The result for negative integers can be added separately using the sign change, rather than silently extending this induction domain.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-8}
A learner measures five Euclidean triangles and reports angle sums close to one hundred eighty degrees. State what this evidence supports and supply the missing general argument.

**Hint 1.** Measurement has both sampling and precision limits.

**Hint 2.** Draw a parallel through one vertex to the opposite side.

**Solution.** The measurements support an approximate pattern in the five instances, subject to drawing and measuring error. They do not establish every exact triangle sum. Through a vertex, construct the parallel to the opposite side. Alternate interior angles transfer the other two vertex angles to the chosen vertex, where the three angles make a straight angle. This proves the claim for an arbitrary nondegenerate Euclidean triangle using Euclidean parallel properties. State that geometric setting; a spherical geodesic triangle is outside the theorem's hypotheses.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-9}
A correct parity proof is given orally without algebraic notation. What should a teacher inspect before judging its generality? Give both a sufficient account and an insufficient one.

**Hint 1.** Format and scope are different properties.

**Hint 2.** Check the domain represented by a pairing account.

**Solution.** Inspect whether the learner covers arbitrary allowed inputs and supplies a valid reason. “Each positive odd collection consists of pairs and one leftover; two leftovers form another pair” proves the positive-count version. “Three plus five is eight, so it always works” is insufficient for the universal claim. If the target includes all integer signs, require an extension such as arbitrary odd integers being twice integers plus one. An oral explanation can supply that extension. The judgement should follow the warrant and domain rather than whether symbols appear on paper.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-proof-teaching-exercise-10}
A teacher supplies the induction hypothesis and the required difference identity, and a learner completes a valid proof. What can be credited, and what further evidence would investigate independent construction?

**Hint 1.** Verification and completion are substantive but scaffolded.

**Hint 2.** A changed claim can require choosing the missing structure.

**Solution.** Credit the learner's valid use of the supplied hypothesis, integer closure, base case and conclusion to the extent actually shown. Record the two prompts; the work does not establish independent selection of the induction structure or discovery of the identity. A related new divisibility claim with no supplied difference can investigate those choices, with access supports maintained. Failure there would not erase the earlier valid reasoning, and success would still be evidence about specified tasks rather than a permanent global proof level. No empirical teaching effect follows from either record alone.

## Chapter 10: Formative assessment is an inference process {#solutions-formative-assessment}

[Chapter context](formative-assessment.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-1}
Design a hinge question comparing how an extreme added value changes the mean, median, and range. Include four options and explain what each distractor reveals.

**Hint 1.** Calculate each measure before designing the options.

**Hint 2.** Treat distractors as possible routes rather than unique diagnoses.

**Solution.**

> The five scores are $60,70,80,90,100$. A sixth score of $0$ is added. Which measure changes most in absolute value?

**A.** Mean *(Distractor: the mean changes from $80$ to $400/6\approx66.67$, about $13.33$.)*

**B.** Median *(Distractor: it changes from $80$ to $(70+80)/2=75$, a change of $5$.)*

**C.** Range *(Correct: it changes from $100-60=40$ to $100-0=100$, a change of $60$.)*

**D.** All measures change equally *(Distractor: learners who do not distinguish the measures.)*

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-2}
A learner solves $2(x+3)=14$ by dividing both sides by $2$ to get $x+3=7$, then subtracting $3$ to get $x=4$. On a subsequent problem, $2(x+3)+5=19$, the learner writes $x+3+5=9.5$ and gets stuck. Diagnose the error and design a formative assessment that would have caught it earlier.

**Hint 1.** Division of an equation applies to the whole side.

**Hint 2.** A valid first step need not follow one preferred operation order.

**Solution.** The learner divided only the product term and the right side, while leaving $5$ unchanged. Division must apply to every term: dividing the equation by $2$ gives $x+3+5/2=19/2$. Subtracting $5$ first is also valid and often cleaner, but it is not the only valid first step. The error concerns equivalence and distribution of division, not a required operation order.

A formative assessment that would catch this earlier might present a sequence: $2(x+3)=14$, then $2(x+3)+1=15$, then $3(x+2)-4=11$. After each problem, ask: "What did you do first, and why?" This reveals whether the learner is attending to structure or applying a memorised sequence.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-3}
Explain why a multiple-choice item with the options "always, sometimes, never" can be more diagnostically powerful than a computation item for assessing conceptual understanding.

**Hint 1.** A selected category alone is insufficient.

**Hint 2.** Require both an example where the claim holds and a warrant for its condition.

**Solution.** A computation item supplies evidence about an execution on that task, while an “always, sometimes, never” item can investigate a claim's domain if it also requests a warrant. Selecting the category alone may be guessed and does not establish the reasoning. For example: "Is $(a+b)^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}$ always, sometimes, or never true?" To justify “sometimes,” the learner must identify the conditions under which it holds ($ab=0$) and the conditions under which it fails. This reveals conceptual understanding of the distributive property and the role of the cross term $2ab$. A computation item such as "Expand $(3+4)^{2}$" does not reveal whether the learner knows why the middle term matters.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-4}
A teacher gives a timed quiz and finds that several learners who usually perform well scored poorly. List three validity threats that might explain the discrepancy, and suggest how to test each.

**Hint 1.** Change one administration feature at a time where feasible.

**Hint 2.** A comparison can investigate a hypothesis without proving a unique cause.

**Solution.**

1. **Time pressure:** The learners understood the material but could not work fast enough. Test by giving an untimed version of the same quiz and comparing scores.

2. **Test anxiety:** The timed, graded format triggered anxiety that interfered with reasoning. Test by administering a parallel quiz in a low-stakes, ungraded format.

3. **Misalignment:** The quiz tested procedures that were not the instructional focus. Test by comparing the quiz items to the stated learning goals and asking whether the items require knowledge not taught.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-5}
Find every real input at which $-(x-3)$ and $x-3$ agree. Explain why choosing that input is an inconclusive identity check.

**Hint 1.** Equate the two expressions and solve.

**Hint 2.** One matching value is not agreement for all values.

**Solution.** Expanding gives $-x+3=x-3$, so $2x=6$ and the only coincidence is three. At that input both expressions are zero. The substitution is correct, but it cannot distinguish the rules. At five their values are negative two and positive two, refuting the proposed identity. A matching instance supports that instance only; the distributive law establishes the actual identity $-(x-3)=-x+3$ for every real input. Feedback should distinguish a poor choice of checking input from invalid arithmetic.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-6}
Prove that two affine real functions agreeing at two distinct inputs are identical. Explain why the same two-point argument fails without the affine premise.

**Hint 1.** Subtract the two equations of agreement.

**Hint 2.** A nonlinear added factor can vanish at both checked inputs.

**Solution.** Write the functions as $mx+b$ and $nx+c$. Agreement at distinct inputs $r,s$ yields $(m-n)r+(b-c)=0$ and the same at $s$. Subtraction gives $(m-n)(r-s)=0$. Since $r-s$ is nonzero, $m=n$, then $b=c$. Without the affine premise, add $(x-r)(x-s)$ to either rule. It vanishes at the two inputs but is nonzero elsewhere. The structural class supplies the warrant, so an assessment should record that premise rather than treating two successful substitutions as a universal identity method.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-7}
In a constructed set of response records, sixty target-route records include fifty-four correct answers, and forty other-route records include twenty correct answers. Find the share of correct answers coming from the target route and state why this is not an empirical diagnostic estimate.

**Hint 1.** Condition on the total correct count, not the full population.

**Hint 2.** The route labels and frequencies were assumed.

**Solution.** There are seventy-four correct answers, of which fifty-four are target-route records. The share is twenty-seven thirty-sevenths. This is exact arithmetic within an invented construction. It estimates no actual classroom rate because the frequencies, route labels and conditional response counts were supplied rather than observed and justified. Other routes may also be valid mathematics. The example demonstrates compatibility of correctness with multiple processes; it does not license assigning that numerical probability to a learner's understanding.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-8}
Construct two four-learner score distributions with mean five on a ten-point task, one uniform and one maximally split. Explain one further distinction the totals still hide.

**Hint 1.** Both totals must be twenty.

**Hint 2.** Equal totals can come from different item-level performances.

**Solution.** The uniform distribution is five, five, five, five. The split distribution is zero, zero, ten, ten. Both total twenty and have mean five. Even an individual five can represent correct calculations without explanations or complete work on only part of the content. A decision about the negative-sign target needs the relevant response and warrant, not the mean alone. These distributions are fictional mathematical examples and supply no evidence that a particular grouping or instructional response improves outcomes.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-9}
A learner corrects an equation after the teacher points to the exact un-divided term. What should be recorded, and what follow-up could investigate independent use of equivalence?

**Hint 1.** The correction is supported performance.

**Hint 2.** Vary the equation without supplying the error location.

**Solution.** Record the original response, the teacher's location-specific hint, the corrected transformation, and the learner's explanation of dividing the whole side. The correction is valid evidence of work with that support. A changed equation, with accessible notation but no pointed term, can investigate whether the learner independently preserves equivalence. Its result should be recorded under its own conditions. Neither one supported correction nor one later success establishes every algebraic transformation skill, and neither identifies the causal effect of the hint without a suitable empirical design.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-formative-assessment-exercise-10}
A teacher plans to move on if eighty percent select the correct option. Identify two unshown assumptions and propose a more explicit action plan.

**Hint 1.** Correctness need not establish the intended warrant.

**Hint 2.** The remaining learners still need access to later work.

**Solution.** The rule assumes that selection measures the relevant understanding and that the chosen share is sufficient for the next task's prerequisites. Neither follows mathematically from eighty percent. Ask a brief explanation or contrast where plausible routes differ, identify the unresolved mathematical issue, and plan how the next activity can revisit it with supports. State what evidence will trigger a whole-class clarification versus individual follow-up. This makes the decision inspectable without claiming a universally optimal threshold or treating those who answered incorrectly as barred from further mathematics.

## Chapter 11: Inclusion changes design, not mathematical ambition {#solutions-inclusion-and-design}

[Chapter context](inclusion-and-design.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-1}
A teacher is planning a lesson on circle geometry that relies heavily on dynamic geometry software with drag-and-drop interaction. A learner in the class has limited fine motor control. Design an inclusive adaptation that preserves the mathematical goal.

**Hint 1.** Name whether the target is construction, exploration or justification.

**Hint 2.** Offer controls or specified configurations retaining the relevant choices.

**Solution.** The mathematical goal is to explore properties of circles, such as the relationship between radius, diameter, and circumference, or the angle properties of chords and tangents. Adaptations might include:

- Keyboard-operable controls that allow the learner to adjust parameters with arrow keys or voice commands;
- Pre-set configurations that the learner can select and observe, rather than constructing from scratch;
- A partner activity where one learner manipulates the software and the other records observations and conjectures;
- Static diagrams with labelled measurements that the learner can analyse and compare;
- A tactile circle with raised chords and angles for manual exploration.

The goal of forming and testing conjectures about circle properties is preserved; only the interaction mode changes.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-2}
Explain why removing graph-reading entirely for a blind learner would change the mathematical goal, while providing a tactile graph preserves it.

**Hint 1.** Distinguish spatial interpretation from interpreting an underlying relationship.

**Hint 2.** A usable tactile graph needs labels and scale information.

**Solution.** Graph reading involves interpreting the relationship between two variables from a spatial representation. If the goal is to understand that relationship, removing the graph removes the opportunity to engage with the concept spatially. A tactile graph, by contrast, makes the spatial relationship available through touch: the learner can feel the slope, the intercept, and the curvature. The cognitive demand---interpreting a two-variable relationship from a spatial display---is preserved. The intended spatial relation can be preserved through touch if the tactile display is usable, labelled and scaled, and the learner can interpret it. This is a design requirement, not a guarantee that any tactile diagram works for every blind learner. If the target concerns the underlying function rather than spatial decoding, a structured table and explicit coordinate mapping may offer another valid route.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-3}
A learner who is an English language learner struggles with word problems not because of the mathematics but because of complex syntax and unfamiliar vocabulary. Design three adaptations that preserve the mathematical demand.

**Hint 1.** Preserve all givens and the requested output.

**Hint 2.** A supplied intermediate formula can change the assessed demand.

**Solution.**

1. **Simplify syntax without simplifying mathematics:** Replace "If a train departs at 8:15 a.m. and travels at a constant speed of 60 miles per hour, at what time will it reach its destination 150 miles away?" with "A train leaves at 8:15 a.m. It travels at a constant speed of 60 miles per hour. Its destination is 150 miles away. At what clock time does it arrive?" Both versions retain the departure time, units and arrival-time target: $150/60=2.5$ hours is 2 hours 30 minutes, so arrival is 10:45 a.m. Removing 8:15 a.m. would leave the duration calculable but the arrival time undetermined; shorter wording must preserve the givens and the mathematical target. Asking for the duration first would add an intermediate-step hint; use that scaffold only when selecting the intermediate quantity is not part of the assessment target.

2. **Provide a bilingual glossary:** Key terms (rate, distance, constant speed) are provided in the learner's first language with mathematical definitions. This removes the language barrier without changing the problem.

3. **Use diagrams and symbols:** Offer a timeline and distance bar labelled with the same departure time, distance and rate. The learner can reason from the representation even if the text is difficult. Supply $d=rt$ only if selecting the relation is not part of the target; otherwise keep that choice with the learner. Record which support was used instead of assuming every scaffold leaves the assessed demand unchanged.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-4}
Critique the following statement: "Universal Design for Learning is too much work for the benefit of a few learners."

**Hint 1.** Separate a design framework from a guaranteed outcome claim.

**Hint 2.** Use concrete barriers and access routes rather than assumed learner categories.

**Solution.** This statement misunderstands both the scope and the mechanism of Universal Design for Learning. UDL is not a set of individual accommodations for specific learners; it is a design framework that creates flexible options from the outset. Captions, clear navigation, multiple response formats, and structured notation can remove barriers for learners beyond those with identified needs, provided the options suit the task and preserve its mathematical demand. Moreover, the "few learners" argument ignores the variability of all learners: any class contains learners with temporary or situational barriers (a broken arm, a noisy environment, a migraine) as well as permanent ones. Anticipating barriers is a design rationale for offering these options. Whether a particular design improves access, learning, or efficiency requires evidence about its use, outcomes, and costs; the framework alone does not establish those effects.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-5}
Two affine relationships pass through $(2,7),(5,16)$ and $(1,8),(5,16)$ respectively. Compare their rates using an accessible structured-data response. State what a sentence saying only “the first is steeper” would omit.

**Hint 1.** Compute both changes, including the different input intervals.

**Hint 2.** A conclusion is not the evidence needed to judge it.

**Solution.** The first rate is nine divided by three, or three output units per input unit. The second is eight divided by four, or two. Thus the first rate is larger. A structured response names each pair, both differences, both quotients and their units. “The first is steeper” supplies the conclusion without exact coordinates, scales or calculations, so it cannot support independent evaluation of the rate comparison. If spatial graph reading is itself targeted, a table also changes that part of the operation; state the intended goal before selecting the alternative.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-6}
A learner dictates a correct general parity proof, and a scribe inserts a missing algebraic reason without asking. Explain what should be recorded and how to preserve learner authorship in a future task.

**Hint 1.** Distinguish transcription from mathematical prompting.

**Hint 2.** Read back ambiguous grouping for the learner's confirmation.

**Solution.** Record which reason the scribe supplied and describe the completed proof as supported at that step. The learner's own valid portions remain evidence of their reasoning. In future, transcribe faithfully, read back notation and grouping, and ask the learner to supply missing reasons rather than silently adding them. Any conceptual prompt should be identified. This allows dictation to support expression while keeping authorship inspectable. It does not require handwriting when proof validity is the target, nor does it make a completed assisted derivation evidence of independent construction.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-7}
Solve the two-tank problem from the worked case and identify a language adaptation that preserves formulation and a scaffold that supplies it.

**Hint 1.** Track initial quantities and signed changes separately.

**Hint 2.** Equate the two volume rules and check the valid time interval.

**Solution.** Equality gives $12+4t=30-2t$, hence $6t=18$ and $t=3$ minutes. Both volumes are twenty-four litres, and the time is nonnegative and before B empties at fifteen minutes. A short-sentence description or table of initial volumes and rates can preserve formulation. Giving the equality equation supplies that operation and should be recorded as a scaffold if modelling is assessed. It may still leave meaningful equation solving and interpretation, but those are narrower claims than independently translating the situation.

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-8}
A calculator is proposed for two tasks: investigating how a function changes across many inputs, and demonstrating unaided fluency with a specified arithmetic procedure. Explain why the same tool can have different effects on the evidence contract.

**Hint 1.** Identify which operation each goal assigns to the learner.

**Hint 2.** A tool's supplied computation may be incidental in one task and central in another.

**Solution.** In the function investigation, a calculator may supply routine evaluations while leaving selection of inputs, comparison of outputs and justification of a pattern to the learner. Record its role and retain the need for a general warrant when a universal claim is made. In the unaided procedure task, the calculator supplies the very computation being assessed, so its output does not demonstrate that unaided skill. This distinction concerns the stated target, not a judgement that calculator use is generally easier or mathematically inferior. Adaptations should be described by the choices they preserve and supply.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-9}
A partner controls a geometry tool while a learner with limited motor access gives instructions. What evidence would show that the learner selected and justified the construction, and what would remain uncertain?

**Hint 1.** Record who chooses each mathematical step.

**Hint 2.** Successful manipulation alone is not the construction argument.

**Solution.** Record the learner's requested points, constraints and transformations, the reasons given, and whether the partner followed those instructions or supplied additional strategy. A valid learner-directed construction with a warrant can show mathematical planning despite delegated motor action. If the partner chooses a crucial auxiliary line, that step is supported and should be labelled. Tool operation, independent strategy selection and proof of the resulting property remain separate questions. The arrangement is a possible access route, not evidence that every partnership preserves the intended demand or improves learning.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-inclusion-and-design-exercise-10}
Design an alternative format for a graph task whose target is identifying an exact maximum from a finite set of plotted data. Explain how to preserve the evidence without supplying the answer.

**Hint 1.** Provide every relevant data pair with variable names and units.

**Hint 2.** Avoid a description that already identifies the largest output.

**Solution.** Supply a structured table containing all plotted input-output pairs, clear column headers and units, and state that the task concerns the finite displayed dataset. Ask the learner to compare the outputs and identify every input attaining the largest value, with the supporting comparison. Do not replace the graph by “the maximum is at input four,” which gives the conclusion. If the target instead requires spatial graph decoding or interpolation on a continuous curve, the table may omit a relevant operation; a usable tactile or other spatial representation may then be needed. The format follows the precise mathematical target.

## Chapter 12: Teaching Fractions and Proportional Reasoning {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning}

[Chapter context](teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-1}
Two drawings shade $2/3$ of an area of $9$ square units and $3/4$ of an area of $4$ square units. Compare the shaded areas and the two fraction numbers. Explain the different answers.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Calculate each shaded area with its own labelled whole.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

For the numbers alone, use denominator twelve.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The shaded areas are $6$ and $3$ square units, so the first is larger. The numbers satisfy $2/3=8/12<9/12=3/4$. The different comparisons concern different quantities: the first scales different areas; the second uses a common unit number. A diagram without whole labels would not distinguish these interpretations. Ask the learner which comparison they intend before treating either answer as an error.

</details>

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-2}
Prove that $(a+1)/(b+1)=a/b$, for $b>0$ and $a\ge0$, holds exactly when $a=b$. What does this say about adding one to both entries?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Both denominators are nonzero.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Expand the cross products and cancel $ab$.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Equality is equivalent to $b(a+1)=a(b+1)$, hence $ab+b=ab+a$ and $b=a$. Conversely $a=b$ makes both fractions one. Thus adding one preserves these fractions only in that special case; it is not a general equivalence operation. A teaching sequence using only examples equal to one would accidentally conceal the failure. Include both an equal-entry case and a different-entry case, then ask for the condition explaining the contrast.

</details>

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-3}
Explain $3/5+1/10$ using a common unit and give an independent magnitude check.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Rename fifths as tenths.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Adding a positive tenth must increase $3/5$.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Each fifth is two tenths, so $3/5=6/10$ and the sum is $7/10$. The result exceeds $6/10$ and is less than one, agreeing with addition of a small positive quantity. The false componentwise answer $4/15$ is below $3/5$ and fails that check. A complete teaching explanation names tenths as the counted unit rather than saying only that the denominators must match.

</details>

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-4}
Design a task distinguishing “larger denominator means larger fraction” from correct unit-fraction reasoning. Give the mathematical answer and one follow-up.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Keep the numerator and reference unit fixed.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compare equal intervals partitioned into three and six pieces.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Ask whether $1/3$ or $1/6$ is larger and require an explanation on one common interval. Since $1/3=2/6>1/6$, the third is larger: fewer equal parts of the same whole means each part is larger. Follow with $2/3$ versus $5/6$ so that a memorised instruction to choose the smaller denominator no longer suffices. This follow-up has $4/6<5/6$. Neither answer alone establishes a general conception; the explanation shows which quantities were compared.

</details>

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-5}
Explain $7/6\div(2/3)$ by a missing-factor equation and common measurement units.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Write $q(2/3)=7/6$.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The divisor is four sixths.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Multiply the equation by $3/2$ to obtain $q=(7/6)(3/2)=7/4$. In sixth-units, seven such units contain seven fourths of a group of four units. One full group uses four sixths, leaving three sixths, which is three quarters of another group. Both interpretations give $1+3/4$. Naming the group size prevents taking the reciprocal of the dividend by an unexamined pattern.

</details>

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-6}
For positive $x,y$, prove precisely when $x/y>x$. Use the result to repair “division makes smaller.”

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Divide the inequality by positive $x$.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compare $1/y$ with one.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Since $x>0$, $x/y>x$ is equivalent to $1/y>1$. Multiplication by positive $y$ makes this $1>y$. Thus the quotient increases exactly for $0<y<1$, is unchanged for $y=1$, and decreases for $y>1$. The repaired statement names the domain and the divisor condition. Dividing by zero is undefined, and signed dividends require a fresh order analysis; neither should be silently included in the positive proof.

</details>

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-7}
A fictional learner scales a $4:3$ mixture to $10:9$. Identify the preserved quantity, explain the intended proportional answer for nine units of the second ingredient, and propose a follow-up.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Compare differences and ratios separately.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The second ingredient has been multiplied by three.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The response preserves a difference of one, but $10/9$ differs from $4/3$. Preserving mixture ratio requires $12:9$, multiplying both original quantities by three. Ask for the amount of the first ingredient when the second is six: proportional reasoning gives eight, whereas the fixed-difference rule gives seven. Require the learner to name what stays unchanged. This is a discriminating fictional task, not evidence that the response is common in a population.

</details>

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-8}
A service charges $y=5+2x$ pounds for $x\ge0$ units. Show that the relationship is not proportional and explain why two successive equal increases can mislead.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Inspect the value at zero.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compare $y(2)$ with $2y(1)$.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Here $y(0)=5$, whereas a proportional rule on this domain has $y(0)=0$. Also $y(2)=9$ but $2y(1)=14$, so doubling input does not double output. Equal input increments produce equal output increments of twice the input change; this expresses constant slope, not direct proportionality. A task containing only differences could therefore support a correct constant-rate observation while failing to reveal the fixed fee.

</details>

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-9}
Adapt a fraction-order task for a learner who cannot use its visual diagram, preserving its mathematical demand. State what would change the goal.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Identify whether the intended reasoning concerns magnitude or visual decoding.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

A tactile interval or explicit equal-part description can preserve one common unit.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

If the goal is to justify $3/4>2/3$, provide a tactile zero-to-one interval partitioned into twelfths, or describe that interval and ask the learner to compare nine and eight twelfth-steps. Retain the need to identify the common whole and explain order. Asking only which numerator is larger changes the mathematical task and can endorse an invalid general rule. Whether a particular format is usable must be checked with the learner; the mathematical analysis alone does not establish access.

</details>

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-teaching-fractions-proportional-reasoning-exercise-10}
Write a three-column record separating a fictional observation, an interpretation and a follow-up for the answer $1/2+1/3=2/5$. Include a possible result that would weaken the interpretation.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Record the written response without a trait label.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

An immediate correct explanation on another sum could point to a slip.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Observation: the fictional script writes the displayed equality. Interpretation: perhaps numerator and denominator were added componentwise. Follow-up: ask for $1/2+1/2$ and why its units can be added. A response of $2/4$ with the same explanation supports the proposed rule on these tasks. A response of one, followed by a correct twelfths or sixths explanation repairing the original sum, weakens the claim of a stable componentwise rule. Neither outcome warrants a population frequency or an intervention effect.

</details>

## Chapter 13: Teaching Algebra: From Arithmetic to Symbols {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols}

[Chapter context](teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-1}
Repair $6+7=13-4=9$ in two ways and explain what the original equality chain falsely asserted.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Evaluate the expression between the equals signs.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Separate calculations, or put the whole calculation in one expression.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The middle expression $13-4$ equals nine, whereas $6+7$ equals thirteen, so the first equality is false. Write $6+7=13$, then $13-4=9$, as two calculations. Alternatively write $(6+7)-4=9$. The repair preserves the intended sequence of operations while removing the false assertion that all displayed expressions name the same number. Asking only for the final value would miss the notational issue.

</details>

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-2}
Explain the roles of the letters in $u+2=7$, $a(b+c)=ab+ac$ and $y=mx+1$ when $m$ is fixed and $x$ varies.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Distinguish solving a condition from stating an identity.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

One letter specifies a member of a function family.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

In the first equation $u$ is an unknown real constrained to five. In the second, $a,b,c$ are arbitrary real inputs in a universal distributive identity; no one value is being sought. In the third, fixed $m$ is a parameter specifying the slope, $x$ is an input variable and $y$ its dependent output. Other interpretations are possible in other contexts, so a teacher should state the role rather than infer it from the printed letter alone.

</details>

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-3}
A fictional solution gives $-2(x-4)=-2x-8$. Supply a revealing counterexample and a general proof of the correct expression.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Try a value where the original inner difference is positive.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Distribute the full factor $-2$ to both terms.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

At $x=5$, the original is $-2$, while the proposed expression is $-18$, disproving equivalence. Distributivity gives $-2(x-4)=(-2)x+(-2)(-4)=-2x+8$ for every real $x$. The example refutes the wrong claim and the law proves the correction. A follow-up should ask what factor multiplies the second term; simply replacing a sign without explanation could hide the same issue on a new problem.

</details>

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-4}
Solve $4x+3=2x+11$ and explain why substitution and reversible steps supply different information.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Subtract $2x$ from both sides.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Reverse every operation to compare solution sets.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Subtracting $2x$ gives $2x+3=11$; subtracting three gives $2x=8$; dividing by two gives $x=4$. Substitution produces nineteen on each side, establishing that four is a solution. Reversibility establishes equivalence of the equations and hence that every original solution must be four. A substitution check alone would not rule out additional solutions to a different equation with several roots.

</details>

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-5}
Determine all real solutions of $2(x+1)=2x+2$ and $2(x+1)=2x+3$. Explain their value in a teaching sequence.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Expand the left sides in both equations.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The resulting statements no longer depend on $x$.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The first reduces to $2x+2=2x+2$, true for all real $x$. The second reduces, after subtracting $2x+2$, to $0=1$, false for every real $x$. Thus their solution sets are $\mathbb R$ and the empty set. Together with a unique-solution example, they expose the assumption that solving must always produce one number. A learner should explain the remaining truth condition, rather than treat cancellation of the variable as a procedural failure.

</details>

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-6}
Find the lost solution when $x(x+3)=0$ is divided by $x$. Give a complete solution with the necessary cases.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Division requires a nonzero divisor.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Inspect zero separately before dividing.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

At $x=0$ the original equation is true. If $x\ne0$, dividing is legitimate and gives $x+3=0$, hence $x=-3$. Both values satisfy the original, so the complete solution set is $\{0,-3\}$. The division-only method loses zero because its extra hypothesis excludes it. The zero-product property gives the same two cases directly. The teaching issue is tracking the condition on an operation, not banning cancellation in every context.

</details>

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-7}
Squaring $x=-2$ gives $x^2=4$. Compare the solution sets and formulate a precise instruction for handling the squared equation.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Equal squares can have opposite roots.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Return the proposed values to the original condition.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The original has solution set $\{-2\}$; the squared equation has $\{-2,2\}$. Squaring preserves the implication from old solutions to new ones but is not reversible on all real values. Solve the squared condition to generate candidates and then check each in the original: two fails and negative two succeeds. The return check is essential because the operation enlarged the set, rather than merely providing optional arithmetic reassurance.

</details>

### Exercise 8 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-8}
Give two polynomial rules agreeing with $2,4,6$ at inputs $1,2,3$ but disagreeing at four. Explain what additional information would warrant choosing the simpler rule.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Start with $2n$.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Add a polynomial vanishing at the first three inputs.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Use $f(n)=2n$ and $g(n)=2n+(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)$. The added product vanishes at one, two and three, but equals six at four; the outputs there are eight and fourteen. A supplied construction adding two units at every stage, or an explicit assumption of an affine rule, would warrant $2n$ from these initial values. A preference for simplicity is a modelling choice rather than a proof that finite data determine the continuation.

</details>

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-9}
Design three questions using $3(t-1)+2t$ that respectively ask for an expression, a solution set and an identity judgement. Supply full mathematical answers.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Change the requested object while holding the expression fixed.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Simplification gives $5t-3$.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Ask “simplify”: distributivity gives $3t-3+2t=5t-3$. Ask “solve $3(t-1)+2t=12$ over the reals”: equivalence gives $5t=15$, so the solution set is $\{3\}$. Ask “is $3(t-1)+2t=5t-3$ an identity?”: yes, because the distributive calculation holds for every real input. Asking learners to name their answer object distinguishes the three demands; identical arithmetic does not make them the same question.

</details>

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-teaching-algebra-arithmetic-to-symbols-exercise-10}
Prepare a fictional record for a learner who writes $x=8$ when asked to simplify $2(x+4)-x$. Separate the response, two rival interpretations and a follow-up that could distinguish them.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Avoid assigning a permanent trait to the learner.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

One interpretation concerns the task; another concerns combining terms.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Record exactly that the fictional script gives $x=8$. Perhaps the learner believes every letter task asks for a numerical solution; alternatively they have distributed correctly but incorrectly cancelled $2x-x$ to zero. Ask first what simplification requests, then ask them to expand the expression and retain a line for combining its variable terms. A correct description of the task with $2x-x=0$ supports the second interpretation locally. Correct combination followed by insisting on one fixed $x$ supports the first. ## Chapter 14: Read educational evidence with mathematical care {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence}

[Chapter context](reading-educational-evidence.md)

### Exercise 1 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-1}
A headline says, "Study finds that homework improves maths scores by 20%." Apply the checklist to identify at least three questions the headline does not answer.

**Hint 1.** Ask what the percentage uses as its baseline.

**Hint 2.** A causal headline needs a comparison and a design account.

**Solution.**

1. **Comparison:** Improved compared to what? No homework at all, or a different use of the same time?
2. **Outcome:** What was measured? Procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, or test scores? When was it measured?
3. **Participants:** Who were the learners? Age, prior achievement and home support are possible sources of variation to investigate; the headline supplies no estimates of moderation.
4. **Implementation:** How much homework, of what kind, with what feedback? "Homework" is not a single intervention.
5. **Causality:** Was this an experiment or an observation? Higher-achieving schools may assign more homework for reasons unrelated to causation.

### Exercise 2 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-2}
Explain why a $p$-value below $0.05$ does not mean that an educational intervention is guaranteed to work in your classroom.

**Hint 1.** A null-model tail probability is not a classroom guarantee.

**Hint 2.** List effect size, transport and implementation questions separately.

**Solution.** Under the study's specified null model and analysis assumptions, $p<0.05$ means the probability of a prespecified test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one is below $0.05$. It is not the probability that the intervention has no effect, nor that this exact effect arose “by chance.” The $p$-value does not report effect size, practical importance, transportability, or implementation fit. Different learners, teachers, materials, and outcomes can therefore produce a different result in your classroom.

### Exercise 3 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-3}
A meta-analysis reports an average effect size of $0.2$ standard deviations for formative assessment, with high heterogeneity ($I^{2}=80\%$). Interpret this for a teacher who is deciding whether to invest in formative assessment training.

**Hint 1.** Heterogeneity is a model-based variation summary.

**Hint 2.** It does not alone reveal the signs of individual study effects.

**Solution.** The fitted average is $0.2$ standard deviations. An $I^2$ estimate of $80\%$ describes a large estimated between-study contribution to total observed variation under the meta-analysis model, but it does not by itself show that the study estimates include positive, zero, and negative values. Inspect the forest plot, uncertainty intervals, between-study variance estimate, model, and contexts. The average alone is a weak predictor for one teacher; the decision also depends on resemblance to the studied settings, implementation demands, costs, and plausible benefits.

### Exercise 4 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-4}
A researcher claims that "direct instruction is superior to inquiry learning for mathematics" based on a single randomized trial. Critique this claim using the principles in this chapter.

**Hint 1.** Name the concrete interventions rather than broad teaching labels.

**Hint 2.** Keep unmeasured outcomes unknown rather than supplying a preferred direction.

**Solution.** A single trial, however well designed, is provisional. The claim ignores replication, context, and the possibility that the comparison condition was poorly implemented inquiry learning rather than well-designed inquiry learning. The outcome measure may cover only procedural fluency, leaving conceptual understanding and problem-solving transfer unmeasured. No direction of advantage on those unmeasured outcomes follows from the trial. The claim also treats "direct instruction" and "inquiry learning" as monolithic categories, when both encompass wide variation in implementation. A more defensible claim would be: "In this study, under these conditions, with these outcomes, direct instruction produced higher average scores than the inquiry approach used." The teacher should seek replications and consider whether their goals align with the study's outcomes.

### Exercise 5 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-5}
A constructed comparison has mean scores sixty and fifty-four. Compute the raw difference and standardised differences using reference spreads twelve and twenty. Explain why the standardised values differ.

**Hint 1.** Keep the numerator fixed.

**Hint 2.** A standard deviation is a measuring unit, not a universal conversion.

**Solution.** The raw difference is six points. Relative to twelve points it is one half of a standard deviation; relative to twenty it is three tenths. The different denominators change the unit, not the raw contrast. A report must identify the reference spread and any estimator adjustments. The calculation describes supplied hypothetical numbers and establishes no instructional effect or practical value without information about the instrument, design and uncertainty.

### Exercise 6 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-6}
A proportion increases from twenty percent to twenty-five percent. State the percentage-point change and the relative percentage change. Explain the ambiguity of “improved by five percent.”

**Hint 1.** Absolute and baseline-relative changes use different denominators.

**Hint 2.** Divide the difference by the original proportion for relative change.

**Solution.** The absolute change is five percentage points. Relative to twenty percent, the increase is five divided by twenty, or twenty-five percent. “Improved by five percent” could mean a relative increase to twenty-one percent, or be an imprecise reference to five percentage points. The baseline and intended measure must be supplied. Neither interpretation by itself establishes causality or a universal benefit; those require design and outcome information separate from arithmetic.

### Exercise 7 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-7}
Under the equal-size covariance model in the worked case, find the design effect for clusters of twenty with within-cluster correlation one fifth. State one limit of using an independent-record equivalent.

**Hint 1.** Use one plus cluster size minus one times the correlation.

**Hint 2.** Assignment units remain clusters.

**Solution.** The multiplier is $1+19/5=24/5$, or four and eight tenths. Dividing a total record count by this number compares mean variances under the simplified equal-size model. ### Exercise 8 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-8}
A constructed group of fifty has forty observed outcomes totalling two hundred eighty on a zero-to-ten scale. Bound its full-group mean without a missingness assumption.

**Hint 1.** Ten missing outcomes contribute between zero and one hundred.

**Hint 2.** Divide full totals by fifty, not forty.

**Solution.** The total lies between two hundred eighty and three hundred eighty. The full-group mean therefore lies between five and six tenths and seven and six tenths. The observed mean is seven, but it need not equal the assigned group's mean. Narrowing the range requires additional information or assumptions. Deleting the missing records does not prove that their outcomes resemble the observed records. The bound is an exact consequence of the constructed range and counts, not a report of study attrition.

### Exercise 9 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-9}
An estimated effect has a wide uncertainty interval containing both zero and an educationally important positive value. Explain why “no significant effect” does not establish practical equivalence.

**Hint 1.** Failure to reject a null is not proof of that null.

**Hint 2.** Equivalence needs a justified margin and corresponding evidence.

**Solution.** The interval is compatible, under the analysis assumptions, with zero and with a meaningful benefit, so the data may be too imprecise to distinguish them. Practical equivalence asks whether effects lie within a specified range considered negligible for the decision. That margin must be justified and addressed by an appropriate analysis. A broad interval crossing zero does not establish it. It also does not prove benefit; the correct statement preserves the uncertainty and the outcome scale rather than treating a significance label as a decision rule.

### Exercise 10 {#solutions-reading-educational-evidence-exercise-10}
A teacher's local pilot shows higher scores after a new lesson sequence than before. Name three questions remaining before claiming the sequence caused the gain, and identify one useful noncausal result the pilot might supply.

**Hint 1.** Consider changes that could occur without the new sequence.

**Hint 2.** Feasibility and causal effectiveness are different targets.

**Solution.** Ask what comparable learners would have done without the sequence, whether repeated assessment or other concurrent instruction contributed, and whether missing records or changed outcome scoring altered the comparison. Also inspect whether the same mathematical construct was measured at both times. The pilot can still document feasibility, access barriers, workload, learner responses and implementation details. Those observations can improve design and motivate a stronger comparison, but they do not alone identify a causal effect. Keep the proposed mechanism and empirical finding separate in the claim ledger.
