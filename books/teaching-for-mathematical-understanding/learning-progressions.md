
# Learning progressions are hypotheses

## What a learning progression is

A learning progression describes a sequence of increasingly sophisticated ways of thinking about a domain. It is not a curriculum pacing guide, nor is it a rigid developmental stage theory that claims every learner must pass through every step in fixed order. Rather, it is a hypothesised pathway that summarises what research and practice suggest about how understanding deepens over time. The hypothesis is testable: it can be confirmed, revised, or rejected by examining learner work.

This chapter examines what makes a progression useful, how to use it without treating it as a straitjacket, and how to diagnose when a learner's reasoning is at a different point than expected. The central claim is that progressions are tools for anticipating and interpreting student thinking, not prescriptions for lockstep instruction.

## A progression for place value

Consider the development of place-value understanding. A plausible progression might be:

1. **Count individual objects:** The learner sees $43$ as forty-three separate items, with no grouping structure.
2. **Make groups of ten while retaining the total:** The learner can bundle forty-three objects into four groups of ten and three ones, understanding that the total is unchanged.
3. **Coordinate tens and ones verbally:** The learner can say "four tens and three ones" and relate this to the quantity.
4. **Connect groups to written positions:** The learner understands that the digit $4$ in $43$ refers to four tens and the digit $3$ refers to three ones.
5. **Regroup flexibly:** The learner can decompose and recompose, for example $43=4\times 10+3=3\times 10+13$. This is the foundation for subtraction with regrouping.
6. **Generalise to powers of ten and decimals:** The learner extends the pattern to hundreds, thousands, and to tenths and hundredths, understanding that each position is ten times the position to its right.

These connections make the proposed sequence mathematically intelligible, but they do not establish a compulsory developmental order. Step 2 requires conservation of quantity; step 4 requires connecting the physical grouping to the symbolic notation; step 5 requires understanding that the same quantity can be named in multiple ways.

## Using the progression to diagnose and instruct

Suppose a learner is asked to compute $43-18$ using the standard algorithm. They write:

$$
\begin{array}{r}
  43 \\
- 18 \\
\hline
  25
\end{array}
$$

The answer is correct, but when asked why the $4$ "turns into a $3$," the learner says, "You just do that." This establishes a successful calculation in this case, not a fixed position on the progression. The learner might be following a remembered procedure, might understand regrouping but find the verbal prompt unclear, or might need another representation to explain it. The answer alone establishes neither understanding of written place value at step 4 nor inability to regroup at step 5.

Use a follow-up to distinguish those hypotheses. Ask the learner to show $43$ as tens and ones, exchange one ten for ten ones, and check why both $4\times10+3$ and $3\times10+13$ name the same amount. Offer a drawing or objects as well as speech, then ask for a changed example such as $52-27=25$. If the learner preserves the quantity in the exchange but cannot connect it to the written algorithm, work on that connection. If the amount changes during the exchange, investigate conservation of quantity. Record the task, representation and help used; treat the response as evidence for the next teaching decision, not as a stage certificate or a reason to withhold later mathematics.

## Progressions are not laws

A learner who has not mastered step 4 may still reason about decimals in a limited context. A learner who appears to be at step 5 on Monday may revert to step 3 on Tuesday when tired or in an unfamiliar context. Progressions describe tendencies, not deterministic trajectories.

Moreover, cultural and linguistic factors influence how learners express place value. In some languages, number words are more transparent about structure: "four-ten-three" rather than "forty-three." Learners from such backgrounds may move through the progression differently. The progression must be adapted, not imposed.

## Progressions in other domains

**Fractions:** One proposed route connects part-whole (a fraction is a piece of a pie) to quotient (a fraction is a division) to ratio (a fraction compares two quantities) to operator (a fraction transforms a quantity) to measure (a fraction is a number on the number line). Learners who understand fractions only as parts of wholes may struggle with fractions greater than $1$ or with fraction multiplication.

**Algebra:** A progression might move from arithmetic (computing with known numbers) to pre-algebra (recognising patterns and using variables informally) to syntactic algebra (manipulating equations by formal rules) to semantic algebra (interpreting equations as relationships and functions) to structural algebra (recognising equivalent forms and choosing among them).

**Proof:** A planning hypothesis can contrast empirical checking, generic arguments, symbolic transformations and explicit axiomatic derivation. These are not a compulsory sequence or an automatic ranking: a generic diagram may already be deductive if its reasoning covers arbitrary cases, whereas a symbolic calculation at one numerical input remains only an example. The warrant and generality, rather than the format, determine whether an argument proves its claim.

## Common errors in using progressions

**The checkpoint trap.** A teacher uses the progression to deny a learner access to advanced material until every preceding step is certified mastered. This can hold learners back unnecessarily. A learner who reasons structurally about algebra may not need exhaustive drill on arithmetic.

**The label trap.** A teacher assigns a learner a "level" and treats it as a fixed trait. "You are a stage-three thinker" can become a self-fulfilling prophecy. Progressions describe reasoning, not identity.

**The single-pathway trap.** A teacher assumes there is only one valid progression. Treat a progression as a testable teaching-and-learning hypothesis: learners may take different routes, and evidence about their prior knowledge, instructional history, and response to the current task should guide the next step. This is an instructional caution, not a claim that research has identified a uniquely optimal path for every learner.

## Worked route comparison: regrouping in any base

The classroom cases here are fictional. They offer mathematically analysed alternatives, not empirical findings about how learners must develop. Let a positional system have integer base $b\geq2$. A two-place recording with tens-like count $t$ and unit count $u$ represents $bt+u$. Exchanging one base-group for $b$ units gives $b(t-1)+(u+b)$, which expands to the same value. The invariant is the represented quantity; the displayed digit counts change.

In base ten, forty-three becomes three tens and thirteen ones. In base six, the numeral with digits four and three has decimal value twenty-seven. Exchanging one six-group gives three groups of six and nine ones, still twenty-seven. This regrouped description is useful even though nine is not a legal single base-six digit in standard notation. An intermediate count representation need not itself be the final normalised numeral. Confusing these two roles can make a valid exchange look like an illegal numeral.

Consider base-six subtraction of the numeral one-four from four-three. The values in decimal are ten and twenty-seven. Regrouping gives three six-groups and nine ones; remove one six-group and four ones to leave two six-groups and five ones. The base-six result two-five has decimal value seventeen, agreeing with the difference of the original values. This calculation separates the invariant from a memorised rule that always “borrows ten.” The number of replacement units is determined by the base.

A learner who handles the base-six case can be asked to derive the general exchange equation. A learner who handles the equation but hesitates with unfamiliar digits may need notation clarification, not earlier whole-number work. Conversely, a successful base-ten algorithm does not establish transfer to a changed base. The proposed progression can locate a connection to investigate, but it cannot infer the reason for hesitation without further evidence.

This route also has limits. Physical bundles model nonnegative counts conveniently; a regrouped term can be negative in an algebraic identity even when it is not a count of available objects. If a subtraction would require a negative final quantity, an integer interpretation or directed number line is needed. State when a material model has reached that boundary rather than treating all later arithmetic as more of the same bundling.

## Worked route comparison: fractions need connected meanings

Start with three quarters as three parts of a unit partitioned into four equal parts. On a number line, those parts are three successive intervals of length one quarter from zero. As a quotient, three quarters is the result of sharing three units equally among four recipients. Partition each unit into fourths: the three units contain twelve fourths, so each recipient gets three fourths. These constructions reach the same rational measure through different situations.

Now use three quarters as an operator on a length of eight metres. One quarter of the length is two metres, so three quarters is six metres. This does not mean that the number three quarters has become six. The operator multiplies the chosen quantity, and the output depends on that input. A learner can understand this operator in a familiar recipe or length context before fluently placing arbitrary fractions on a number line. Another may understand the number-line measure first. Neither route is ruled out by the mathematical connections.

To connect the meanings, keep a single unit explicit. Applying three quarters to a two-metre length gives one and a half metres; measuring that output in metres gives a number greater than one even though the operator coefficient is less than one. Applying five quarters to one metre gives one and a quarter metres, so a part-whole story restricted to a single partitioned object needs extension to repeated unit lengths. These are changes of interpretation and referent, not contradictory rules.

A fictional assessment supplies three tasks: represent five quarters on a number line, share five identical unit loaves equally among four people, and find five quarters of an eight-metre rope. The numerical measures are one and a quarter units, one and a quarter loaves each, and ten metres respectively. Asking how the shared coefficient enters each result investigates connections without assuming a fixed sequence among them. Record which whole, input, and output the learner names.

The teaching conjecture is to coordinate these meanings through invariant rational relationships. It does not establish that every learner must master all measure tasks before operator tasks, nor that a correct rope answer implies every fraction meaning is secure. Local success and conceptual connections are useful evidence precisely when they are described with their limits.

## Worked route comparison: a generic argument can precede formal notation

Consider the claim that the sum of two odd integers is even. One fictional learner gives several calculations. Another describes two arbitrary odd collections as pairs with one object left over in each. Combining them combines all existing pairs and pairs the two leftovers, leaving none. If the account explicitly covers any odd integer represented this way, it is a generic proof for nonnegative odd counts, not merely a preliminary empirical stage. Extending to all odd integers requires a definition that includes negative values rather than literal object counts.

An algebraic argument writes the integers as $2m+1$ and $2n+1$, with arbitrary integers $m,n$. Their sum is $2(m+n+1)$, so it is an integer multiple of two and therefore even. The integer closure of the multiplier is part of the warrant. This proof covers negative odd integers as well as positive ones. The paired-object account and the algebra share a grouping structure, but their stated domains may initially differ.

A third learner writes exactly the same algebra with $m=2$ and $n=5$ supplied numerically. That calculation verifies one example unless the learner explains how the letters can be arbitrary. Formal symbols alone do not guarantee generality. Conversely, an oral account can have full generality if it uses the definition and covers all allowed cases. A progression that ranks these responses only by representational sophistication would obscure the mathematical issue.

For a discriminating follow-up, ask what changes for two odd negative integers, then ask why one numerical counterexample could refute a universal assertion but many successful cases cannot establish it on an infinite domain. This can identify whether the learner is tracking domain, arbitrary choice, and quantifier direction. It does not locate a single global “proof stage.” A learner might give a strong parity argument while needing support with a geometric diagram or induction hypothesis.

The useful next teaching goal is therefore specific: extend the arbitrary-integer representation, connect it to the pairing explanation, or distinguish examples from coverage. Progression language remains a way to anticipate these connections, not a certificate authorising or withholding later mathematics.

## What would revise a proposed progression?

A mathematical dependency is not automatically an instructional prerequisite. A proof may use a concept explicitly even though a learner first encounters the result through a special case, an intuitive representation, or a different proof. Similarly, two ideas can be connected without every learner acquiring them in the same order. Treat a proposed order as a claim about learning under specified conditions, requiring evidence beyond the logical relations among the ideas.

Useful observations include tasks, representations, supports, explanations and later transfer. If a learner repeatedly solves operator fraction problems while needing help with the supposed earlier part-whole tasks, that observation challenges a strict ordering for those tasks. It does not alone refute every weaker claim about typical routes. If a learner succeeds only after an explicit prompt, record that support rather than treating prompted and independent responses as interchangeable.

A classroom teacher need not build a formal latent-state model to use this caution. A small record can distinguish the proposed connection, the evidence currently available, and the next uncertainty. The record should not invent a numerical confidence or claim a validated stage boundary. The goal is a revisable account of reasoning that supports access to the next mathematical question.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise 1

A learner computes $100-37$ by counting up from $37$ to $100$: $37$ to $40$ is $3$, $40$ to $100$ is $60$, so the answer is $63$. Describe the reasoning visible here, state what the response does not establish, and suggest a follow-up that tests a place-value hypothesis.

**Hint 1.** Counting up measures a difference without requiring the written subtraction algorithm.

**Hint 2.** Keep the visible strategy separate from a hypothesised stable stage.

**Solution.** Counting up computes the difference as the distance from $37$ to $100$: $3+60=63$. This is valid and efficient. It is consistent with coordinating tens and ones, but one successful calculation does not establish a stable progression stage or show how the learner would explain another decomposition. Ask the learner to show the two jumps on a number line and explain $100-37=(60+40)-(30+7)=63$. Then try $83-46$: the jumps $46\to50\to80\to83$ total $4+30+3=37$. Compare that explanation with regrouping, recording any prompts needed. The aim is to test and connect the learner's reasoning rather than force one written method or assign a fixed label.

### Exercise 2

Explain why a learner who understands fractions only as "parts of a whole" might struggle with $\frac{5}{4}$.

**Hint 1.** A single bounded whole does not contain five of its own four parts.

**Hint 2.** Repeated unit intervals or equal sharing can extend the interpretation.

**Solution.** If a fraction is defined as a part of a single whole, then $\frac{5}{4}$ is impossible: you cannot take five parts from a whole divided into four. The learner needs to expand their conception to include fractions as measures on the number line (where $\frac{5}{4}$ is simply a point to the right of $1$) or as quotients (where $\frac{5}{4}$ is the result of dividing $5$ by $4$). Without this expansion, the learner may write $\frac{5}{4}=1\frac{1}{4}$ mechanically without understanding that it represents a quantity greater than one.

### Exercise 3

Design a brief assessment that tests whether a learner can explain and transfer a quantity-preserving regrouping, while keeping a progression-stage judgment provisional.

**Hint 1.** An exchange preserves a quantity; compensation preserves a difference.

**Hint 2.** Use a changed example and record the supports supplied.

**Solution.** Begin with regrouping itself: ask the learner to show $43$ with tens and ones, exchange one ten for ten ones without changing the total, and explain $43=4\times10+3=3\times10+13$. Then subtract $18$ using that representation:

$$43-18=(30+13)-(10+8)=20+5=25.$$

Invite a drawing or objects as well as speech. Change the case to $52-27$: $52=40+12$ gives $(40+12)-(20+7)=20+5=25$. Record what the learner preserves and which prompts or materials are needed.

A separate comparison can investigate compensation: "Why do $43-18$ and $33-8$ have the same difference?" Subtracting $10$ from both numbers gives

$$43-18=(43-10)-(18-10)=33-8=25.$$

That explanation preserves a difference; the tens/ones exchange preserves the minuend's value. Both are valid, but a compensation explanation alone does not answer the regrouping question. Keep their evidence separate. A single correct or unsuccessful explanation does not establish a fixed progression stage.

### Exercise 4

A teacher says, "My year-seven class is still at the part-whole stage for fractions, so I cannot teach them fraction multiplication yet." Critique this reasoning.

**Hint 1.** A proposed route is not an admission requirement.

**Hint 2.** Half of a half provides a meaningful multiplication case within a part-whole model.

**Solution.** The teacher is committing the checkpoint trap. While part-whole understanding is limited, learners at that stage can still explore fraction multiplication in concrete contexts. For example, "half of a half" is accessible through paper folding and connects to the part-whole model. The teacher might introduce multiplication as an extension of "finding a part of a part" before learners have fully developed the measure or operator conceptions. Progressions are hypotheses about sophistication, not barriers to exposure. The teacher should ask what fraction multiplication means in a part-whole context rather than assuming it is impossible.

### Exercise 5 {#learning-progressions-exercise-5}

Prove the one-group exchange identity in base $b\geq2$ and use it to subtract the base-five numeral one-three from three-one.

**Hint 1.** Replace one group by five units in the example.

**Hint 2.** Check the result by converting both original numerals to decimal values.

**Solution.** Distributivity gives $b(t-1)+(u+b)=bt-b+u+b=bt+u$. In base five, three-one is three groups and one unit. Exchange to two groups and six units; removing one group and three units leaves one group and three units, the numeral one-three. The original decimal values are sixteen and eight, and the result has value eight, confirming the difference. Six is an intermediate unit count, not a legal final base-five digit. This distinguishes the value-preserving representation from standard numeral syntax.

### Exercise 6 {#learning-progressions-exercise-6}

Distinguish regrouping forty-three as thirty plus thirteen from changing forty-three minus eighteen to thirty-three minus eight. State the invariant in each.

**Hint 1.** Ask whether one number changes or both arguments change.

**Hint 2.** Expand each expression algebraically.

**Solution.** Regrouping uses $43=30+13$, preserving the minuend's value while changing its decomposition. Compensation uses $(43-10)-(18-10)=43-18$, preserving the difference while changing both numbers. Both lead to twenty-five but provide different evidence about reasoning. A learner who explains compensation has not thereby explained the exchange of one ten for ten ones. A useful follow-up asks for both invariants and their connection rather than requiring one method as the sole sign of understanding.

### Exercise 7 {#learning-progressions-exercise-7}

Explain five quarters as a measure, a quotient, and an operator on twelve litres. State why these are connected without being identical tasks.

**Hint 1.** Keep the relevant unit or input explicit.

**Hint 2.** For equal sharing, distribute five units among four recipients.

**Solution.** As measure it is five quarter-unit intervals from zero, located at one and a quarter units. As quotient, sharing five unit quantities among four people gives each five quarters of a unit. As an operator on twelve litres, it gives fifteen litres: one quarter is three litres and five quarters are fifteen. The same rational coefficient occurs, but the operator's output depends on the input quantity. Success in one task supplies evidence about that interpretation, not an automatic certificate for all others or a required order of acquisition.

### Exercise 8 {#learning-progressions-exercise-8}

A learner proves that two positive odd counts sum to an even count by pairing objects. What must be added to cover all odd integers, including negative ones?

**Hint 1.** Literal object counts do not represent negative cardinalities.

**Hint 2.** Use arbitrary integer parameters in the odd-number definition.

**Solution.** Write arbitrary odd integers as $2m+1$ and $2n+1$ with integer parameters, which may be negative. Their sum is twice $m+n+1$, an integer, and is therefore even. This extends the domain without inventing negative collections of objects. The original pairing argument remains valid for its count domain. The next step is a mathematical domain extension, not evidence that the learner must abandon visual reasoning or pass through a fixed global stage.

### Exercise 9 {#learning-progressions-exercise-9}

A proposed progression says every learner must master number-line fractions before operator fractions. A learner repeatedly solves operator tasks but needs help plotting fractions. What can and cannot be concluded?

**Hint 1.** Distinguish a strict prerequisite claim from a typical-route claim.

**Hint 2.** Examine whether the tasks and support conditions are comparable.

**Solution.** If the observations are secure and the tasks represent the intended meanings, they challenge the strict universal prerequisite claim for those conditions. They do not show that number-line work is useless, that the learner understands every operator task, or that typical-route hypotheses are all false. Record the actual tasks, explanations, and supports; investigate whether plotting access or scale interpretation contributes to the difficulty. A revised route can connect the successful operator meaning to measure without withholding further fraction work until a supposed checkpoint is passed.

### Exercise 10 {#learning-progressions-exercise-10}

Design a record for a learner who solves a regrouping problem only after a teacher supplies the exchange. Explain why the record should not simply say “regrouping mastered.”

**Hint 1.** Separate selecting the exchange from executing it.

**Hint 2.** Record both the prompt and the mathematical explanation given.

**Solution.** Record the original numbers, the teacher's supplied decomposition, the learner's subsequent subtraction, and whether the learner explained why the decomposition preserves value. State that the learner completed the calculation with an exchange prompt. Selecting an exchange independently, executing a supplied exchange, and justifying it are distinct pieces of evidence. A changed problem without the same prompt could investigate independence, with accessible representation options maintained. The initial supported success is useful, but it does not establish a stable stage, general transfer, or a causal effect of the prompt.
