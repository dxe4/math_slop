
# The Twentieth Century: Computation and Bourbaki

Two twentieth-century questions make a useful contrast. What can an exact mechanical procedure calculate or decide? How can a large body of mathematics be organised by shared structures rather than by a sequence of unrelated techniques? Turing's paper on computable numbers gave a precise model for one kind of procedure and a limit on decision methods. The collective publishing as Nicolas Bourbaki sought a structural architecture for mathematics. Neither arose from nothing. Babbage's earlier difference-table problem provides an elementary computational bridge; the earlier algebra chapters supply examples of structure. This chapter keeps machines, proofs and editorial programmes distinct.

For the older computational witness we use [Menabrea's memoir translated by Ada Lovelace in 1843](https://www.fourmilab.ch/babbage/sketch.html) and [Babbage's 1846 fourth edition, Chapter XX](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Economy_of_Machinery_and_Manufactures/Chapter_20), as identified in the existing [Historical Mathematics core source register](../../course-texts/atlas-historical-mathematics-core.md). For Turing we use a [university-hosted scan of his 1936 paper](https://theory.stanford.edu/~trevisan/cs172-07/turing36.pdf) and the [Turing archival bibliography](https://www.turing.org.uk/sources/biblio4.html). For Bourbaki we use the [CNRS historical account](https://news.cnrs.fr/articles/bourbaki-and-the-foundations-of-modern-mathematics), the [Bourbaki association's publication site](https://www.bourbaki.fr/) and the [University of Michigan library finding aid](https://guides.lib.umich.edu/c.php?g=282871&p=1888124). These named sources carry different kinds of claim.

## A table can be generated by a rule

A difference table for square numbers is a calculation with a finite description and a fixed update rule. If the current square is $n^2$, the next square is $(n+1)^2=n^2+(2n+1)$. The next increment rises by 2. In one register convention, the stored difference is the increment that *produced* the current square; in another it is the increment that will produce the *next* square. The two conventions lead to different update orders. The [core course's source comparison](../../course-texts/atlas-historical-mathematics-core.md) identifies the selected Menabrea/Lovelace and Babbage passages and warns against mixing their registers.

## Worked example 1: the difference-engine square table

Following the backward-difference interpretation of the [1843 memoir's square example](https://www.fourmilab.ch/babbage/sketch.html), start with current square $S=9$ and stored previous increment $D=5$. First update $D\leftarrow D+2=7$, then $S\leftarrow S+D=16$. Repeating gives $(S,D)=(25,9),(36,11),(49,13)$. The invariant after stage $n$ is $S=n^2$ and $D=2n-1$: updating $D$ makes it $2n+1$, then adding yields $(n+1)^2$. This proves every step, not just the displayed table.

[Babbage's 1846 Chapter XX](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Economy_of_Machinery_and_Manufactures/Chapter_20) uses a forward-difference convention in its selected example: if the current square is 1 and the stored next increment is 3, add first to get 4, then increase the increment to 5. Applying that instruction order to the first state $9,5$ would give 14, a wrong square. The registers must have meanings, not only values. This historical comparison is about mathematical specification, not evidence that a fully proposed Analytical Engine was built or that a later computer ran this code.

## A model of an effective procedure

Turing's [1936 *On Computable Numbers*](https://theory.stanford.edu/~trevisan/cs172-07/turing36.pdf) describes an idealised machine with a finite set of instructions acting on a tape. The [Turing archive bibliography](https://www.turing.org.uk/sources/biblio4.html) distinguishes paper publication and surviving manuscript material. A machine model lets a question about “a method” become mathematically exact. It also creates a distinction between a problem for which a procedure eventually returns an answer on every input and a process that may continue forever on some inputs. Running a few examples cannot prove termination on all inputs.

The famous modern **halting problem** asks whether one general procedure could determine, for every encoded program and input, whether that program eventually stops. The result is negative. The argument below is a school-level diagonal reconstruction of the impossibility idea related to Turing's decision work. The exact 1936 source proof has its own machine coding and terminological choices; this compressed program description is not quoted from it.

## Worked example 2: a halting decider contradiction

Assume a total program $H(P,x)$ returns `yes` exactly when program $P$ halts on input $x$, and `no` otherwise. Construct a new program $D(y)$ that asks $H(y,y)$; if the answer is `yes`, $D$ loops forever, and if `no`, $D$ halts immediately. Now run $D$ on its own description. If $H(D,D)$ says `yes`, $D(D)$ loops, so the answer is wrong. If it says `no`, $D(D)$ halts, so that answer is wrong. Both possibilities contradict the assumed correctness of $H$. Therefore no such total correct halting decider exists for the chosen general program model.

The proof depends on programs having descriptions that can be supplied as inputs and on the construction of $D$ being an effective program in the same model. It does not say that no particular program's behaviour can ever be determined. A simple loop that visibly stops after ten steps is decidable by inspection. The impossibility concerns one algorithm covering every possible encoded case. This is stronger than observing that a computation is slow and different from a practical resource limit.

## A collective architecture of structures

The [CNRS account of Bourbaki](https://news.cnrs.fr/articles/bourbaki-and-the-foundations-of-modern-mathematics) describes a group writing under one pseudonym and an aim to organise mathematical subjects around abstract structures. The [Michigan finding aid](https://guides.lib.umich.edu/c.php?g=282871&p=1888124) identifies the pseudonym and the publication project, while the [association's site](https://www.bourbaki.fr/) names the continuing *Éléments de Mathématique*. The date of an early summary fascicle is not the date every later chapter or edition appeared. A collective name also should not be treated as a single person's biography.

A **structure** is a set of objects together with specified operations or relations satisfying stated laws. An isomorphism between two instances preserves the relevant operations while changing labels. This viewpoint can connect apparently different calculations. It also risks losing the purpose, setting or constructive method of a particular source if applied carelessly. Bourbaki's programme is a historical editorial choice, not a theorem that every reader must prefer one style.

## Worked example 3: four equally likely symbols

In [Claude Shannon's 1948 *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf), a source with four symbols $A,B,C,D$ each having probability $1/4$ appears in the discussion of maximum entropy. Shannon's stated maximum for this four-symbol source is $\log_2 4=2$ bits per symbol. We can reconstruct the calculation with his general expression $H=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i$:

$$H=-4\left(\frac14\log_2\frac14\right)=-4\left(\frac14\cdot(-2)\right)=2.$$

The unit “bit” depends on base-two logarithms. Four fixed-length binary labels—00, 01, 10 and 11—also use two digits per symbol, agreeing with the entropy in this equal-probability case. That agreement does not make every code optimal for every source. If one symbol occurs more frequently, the entropy changes, and questions about coding efficiency require the actual distribution and coding constraints. The specific four-symbol equal-probability case is in Shannon's paper; the displayed labels and arithmetic are our transparent modern presentation of it. This third problem connects exact computation to the mathematical specification of information while keeping it distinct from Turing's question about universal decision procedures.

## A modern structural illustration: a group-structure comparison

As a modern elementary instance of the structural question associated with [Bourbaki's algebra route](https://news.cnrs.fr/articles/bourbaki-and-the-foundations-of-modern-mathematics), compare addition modulo four, $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, with pairs of bits under coordinatewise addition modulo two, $(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$. Each has four elements, an identity and inverses; both are groups. But in the first, $1+1=2$ and $1+1+1+1=0$, so element 1 has order four. In the bit-pair group, adding any nonzero pair to itself gives $(0,0)$, so every nonidentity element has order two. An isomorphism preserves element orders; therefore no isomorphism exists.

The example answers a genuine structural classification question: equal size alone does not imply equal operation structure. It is our numerical teaching case, not a group table claimed to be printed on a Bourbaki page. The proof identifies exactly which law is preserved, rather than relying on different-looking labels. A simple relabelling cannot turn an order-four element into an order-two element. The same discipline appeared in the previous chapter's matrix and ideal examples.

## Machines, people and print

The [Menabrea/Lovelace memoir](https://www.fourmilab.ch/babbage/sketch.html) distinguishes memoir and separately headed translator's Notes; [Babbage's 1864 autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/57532/pg57532-images.html) is a later participant narrative. Turing's abstract machine is a proof model, not a hardware specification for a built device. Bourbaki's collective books are a publication and research programme, not a mechanical procedure. The words “computer” and “machine” can cover human labour, proposed mechanisms, actual apparatus and formal models in different sources. A history of computation must say which is meant.

The two main twentieth-century developments here are also connected by limits. A structural description can state a problem precisely; it does not guarantee an algorithm that solves it. A machine can compute a long table; it does not by itself decide every theorem about the structure behind that table. The halting proof shows one principled obstruction. The group comparison shows a positive finite classification with a short invariant. Neither warrants a claim that all mathematics is computation or that formal structure makes source context irrelevant.

The final chapter looks beyond a familiar sequence of canonical books. It asks whose mathematical practices are visible, how archives and institutions affect that visibility, and how to compare problems without inventing routes of influence. The methods learned here—state the object, operation, witness and limit—will remain the same.

## A finite specification versus an infinite promise

The square-table procedure has a finite description and produces any requested next square after finitely many repetitions. Its invariant proves that each produced entry is correct. But asking the procedure to print *all* squares and then stop has no final step: there are always more inputs. This distinction helps prepare the halting question. A program may compute every individual requested value correctly while an unrestricted run never terminates. Conversely, a program that halts quickly can give a wrong value if its invariant fails. “Computable,” “correct” and “terminating on every input” are related but separate properties. Turing's formal model made those separations available for proof; a physical machine's speed and memory raise additional questions that are not settled by the abstract impossibility theorem.

## What a structural comparison forgets

The two four-element groups demonstrate that an invariant can rule out an isomorphism without enumerating every possible relabelling. Their element orders are mathematical facts independent of the printed names of the elements. Yet the calculation says nothing about why a particular historical author chose one group for a geometric symmetry or arithmetic problem. A structural account deliberately discards some context to compare laws; a historical account restores the witness, use and audience. The CNRS account of Bourbaki describes an editorial ambition to organise knowledge, while the association and library records show a continuing publication under a collective name. The group example illustrates that ambition mathematically but does not stand in for an inspected Bourbaki proof page. Naming that boundary is part of a faithful worked example.

## Dates and media

Turing's paper has receipt, reading and issue dates in its printed heading and archive description. The difference-table passages belong to two nineteenth-century printed books with different register conventions. The Bourbaki project has early publications and later editions rather than one all-at-once release. Treating each as simply “the twentieth century” would erase their different material forms. The mathematical comparison is still valuable: it asks how a finite rule, a proof of impossibility and an axiomatic organisation each make a claim reproducible. Reproducibility here means that another reader can follow the stated steps and sources; it does not imply that any historical proposed engine or present software was executed during this authoring.

### Exercise 8

### Exercise 9

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

## Summary

Babbage-era difference registers, Turing’s machine model and Bourbaki’s structural programme answer different questions about procedures and mathematical organisation. The first yields an invariant for a table, the second a universal decision limit, and the third a way to compare operations across labels. The final chapter uses the book’s source discipline to examine mathematical practices beyond the usual canon.
