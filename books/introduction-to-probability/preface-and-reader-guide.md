
# Preface and Reader Guide

This book provides an L2 introduction to probability with algebra, elementary calculus and an explicit distinction between a model and the data it is intended to describe. It starts with finite outcomes, not sigma-algebras. Continuous chapters use familiar densities and ordinary integrals; the general measure-theoretic framework is a later continuation.

## Preparation and a short diagnostic

The required algebra is supplied by [Algebra and Functions](#/books/algebra-and-functions). Basic integral and derivative preparation for Chapters 9–11 is supplied by [Calculus as Change and Accumulation](#/courses/atlas-calculus-change).

Before beginning, calculate a fraction of a finite set, expand a squared difference and interpret a function's domain. For example:

- Three of eight equally likely tickets win. Their total probability is $3/8$.
- The mean of the three numbers $1,4,7$ is four.
- Expanding $(x-a)^2$ gives $x^2-2ax+a^2$.
- A rule involving division by $b$ needs $b\ne0$.

Before the density chapters, check that $\int_0^1 2x\,dx=1$ and that differentiating $x^2$ gives $2x$. If these steps are unfamiliar, review the linked preparation while studying the earlier discrete chapters.

## What each part develops

Part I turns experiments into sample spaces, events, counts and conditional laws. Part II develops independence, numerical random variables, moments and discrete families. Part III introduces densities, joint laws and transformed quantities, then proves a finite-variance weak law. Part IV applies the central limit theorem, introduces finite Markov models and Monte Carlo, and closes with cumulative problems and projects.

The sequence supplies exact chapter links in the [book introduction](#/books/introduction-to-probability). Chapters contain worked examples and ten exercises with two stages of help and a full solution. Use the reveals after making a serious attempt; comparing just final numbers misses the model obligations and proof steps.

## Proofs, citations and approximations

Definitions name the objects being studied. Proved propositions and theorems include their hypotheses and arguments. Some broader results, notably the classical iid central limit theorem and a general finite-chain convergence theorem, are explicitly cited rather than presented with an incomplete proof. Their applications retain all stated independence, moment and nondegeneracy assumptions.

An exact probability, an upper bound, an approximation and an observed relative frequency are different mathematical claims. Moment inequalities give finite guarantees under the model. Limit theorems concern specified sequences as sample size grows. A normal approximation at one sample size is not automatically a finite error guarantee.

The notation and source guide in [the back matter](references-and-notation.md) collects the main conventions. New text and exercises use British spelling and CC BY-SA 4.0. External sources retain their own rights; source links do not imply that their text or exercises have been copied.
