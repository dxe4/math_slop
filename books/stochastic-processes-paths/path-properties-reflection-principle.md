
# Path Properties and the Reflection Principle

Brownian motion has continuous paths, yet its maximum over an interval is not determined by the endpoint. A path can cross a barrier and return below it before the final observation. Reflection turns this hidden crossing event into an ordinary Gaussian tail, providing exact first-arrival and barrier probabilities.

This L4 chapter assumes [Brownian Motion](brownian-motion.md), Gaussian densities, conditional expectation, stopping times and dominated convergence. We work with standard Brownian motion and its usual completed right-continuous natural filtration. Independence statements refer to this Brownian filtration. Enlarging the filtration by future information can destroy them.

## Stopping times and continuous barriers

For $a>0$, define
$$
\tau_a=\inf\{t\ge0:B_t=a\},\qquad
M_T=\sup_{0\le s\le T}B_s.
$$
Continuity makes $\tau_a$ a stopping time: the event $\{\tau_a\le t\}$ is $\{M_t\ge a\}$, and the supremum can be computed from values at rational times together with the endpoint. The event is thus measurable from observations up to $t$.

Before proving arrival is almost sure, allow $\tau_a=\infty$. On $\{\tau_a<\infty\}$ continuity gives $B_{\tau_a}=a$ exactly; there is no overshoot. This exact contact is essential in reflection. A jump process crossing above a threshold need not land on it and has a different argument.

## Why Brownian motion restarts at a stopping time

**Lemma 14.1 (bounded stopping-time restart).** If $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, the process $W_u=B_{\tau+u}-B_\tau$, $u\ge0$, is Brownian motion independent of $\mathcal F_\tau$.

**Proof.** First let $\tau$ take values in a fixed discrete time grid. On the event $\{\tau=t_k\}$, any event belonging to $\mathcal F_\tau$ intersects that event in $\mathcal F_{t_k}$. Deterministic-time independent increments therefore give, for any bounded continuous function $g$ of finitely many future increments and any $A\in\mathcal F_\tau$,
$$
E[\mathbf1_Ag(W_{u_1},\ldots,W_{u_r})]
=P(A)E[g(B_{u_1},\ldots,B_{u_r})].
$$
Summing the disjoint possible values of $\tau$ proves the grid case.

For a bounded general $\tau$, use its upper dyadic approximations $\tau_n=2^{-n}\lceil2^n\tau\rceil$. They are grid-valued stopping times, $\tau_n\downarrow\tau$, and $\mathcal F_\tau\subseteq\mathcal F_{\tau_n}$. Brownian continuity gives convergence of every increment in the displayed identity. Bounded convergence passes to the limit. The identity for bounded continuous cylinder functions extends to bounded measurable functions by a monotone-class argument, proving the finite-dimensional law and independence. Continuity of $W$ supplies the Brownian path condition, and its path sigma-algebra is generated by rational-time coordinates. ∎

An almost surely finite unbounded stopping time can be treated by localisation. For reflection on $[0,T]$ it is enough to use $\tau_a\wedge T$, already bounded. This avoids assuming finite expected hitting time, a hypothesis that will in fact fail.

## Reflection preserves the Brownian path law

At $\tau=\tau_a\wedge T$, leave the path before $\tau$ unchanged and negate all increments after it:
$$
\widetilde B_s=
\begin{cases}
B_s,&s\le\tau,\\
2B_\tau-B_s,&s>\tau.
\end{cases}
$$
Conditional on $\mathcal F_\tau$, the future increments are an independent Brownian motion. Their negation has the same law because centred Gaussian increments are symmetric. Hence the reflected whole process is Brownian as well. The transformation is its own inverse: before the first crossing the paths agree, and reflection does not change the crossing time.

If the barrier has not been reached by $T$, the transformation does not alter any observation before $T$. If it has been reached, the final value becomes $2a-B_T$.

**Theorem 14.2 (reflection principle).** For $a>0$, $T>0$ and $b\le a$,
$$
P(M_T\ge a,\ B_T\le b)=P(B_T\ge2a-b).
$$
In particular,
$$
P(M_T\ge a)=2\{1-\Phi(a/\sqrt T)\},
$$
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function.

**Proof.** Reflection maps paths reaching $a$ and ending at or below $b$ bijectively to paths ending at or above $2a-b$. Every path in the latter class has crossed $a$, by continuity and $2a-b\ge a$. The map preserves Brownian law and is invertible, proving the first identity.

Split the crossing event according to whether $B_T\ge a$ or $B_T<a$. The first part is simply the Gaussian endpoint event, because any such endpoint forces a crossing. The reflected second part has the same probability as $B_T>a$. Gaussian endpoint atoms are zero, so the sum is twice that tail. ∎

The maximum consequently has the same one-time distribution as $|B_T|$. This is a distributional identity, not the pathwise assertion that $M_T=|B_T|$. A path that rises and returns to zero shows why the latter would be wrong.

**Worked example 14.3 (crossing between observations).** For a standard Brownian motion observed for four time units, the probability of reaching level two is $2(1-\Phi(1))$. The probability that the endpoint alone is at least two is only $1-\Phi(1)$. Exactly half the crossing probability comes from paths ending below the barrier. Sampling only the final value misses those crossings.

## First-arrival distribution and its infinite mean

Since $\{\tau_a\le t\}=\{M_t\ge a\}$,
$$
P(\tau_a\le t)=2\{1-\Phi(a/\sqrt t)\},\qquad t>0.
$$
As $t\to\infty$, the right side tends to one, so $\tau_a<\infty$ almost surely. Differentiating the smooth distribution function on positive times gives density
$$
f_{\tau_a}(t)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,t^{3/2}}
\exp\left(-\frac{a^2}{2t}\right).
$$
The chain rule contributes both the sign and the factor $t^{-3/2}$; omitting the derivative of $a/\sqrt t$ would give an incorrect density.

The survival function is $2\Phi(a/\sqrt t)-1$. The normal density is continuous and positive at zero, giving
$$
P(\tau_a>t)\sim\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac a{\sqrt t}.
$$
Its integral over large $t$ diverges. The tail-integral identity therefore gives $E\tau_a=\infty$. Almost-sure arrival does not license expectation-preserving optional stopping for $B_t$ at this time: such an argument would incorrectly give $EB_{\tau_a}=0$ although the terminal value is $a$.

**Worked example 14.4 (scaling arrival times).** The distribution formula shows $\tau_a$ has the same law as $a^2\tau_1$. Multiplying the barrier height by three multiplies every quantile of arrival time by nine. It does not produce a finite mean by multiplying an infinite one. A median is finite because the distribution increases continuously from zero to one, even though its expectation diverges.

## Endpoints conditioned to stay below a barrier

Write $p_T(y)=(2\pi T)^{-1/2}e^{-y^2/(2T)}$. For $y<a$, reflection gives
$$
P(M_T<a,\ B_T\in dy)
=\{p_T(y)-p_T(2a-y)\}\,dy.
$$
This is the transition density killed on reaching the upper barrier. It is nonnegative because $(2a-y)^2-y^2=4a(a-y)>0$. Its integral over $y<a$ equals the survival probability, so it is not a full probability density unless divided by that probability.

Dividing by the positive endpoint density gives a version of the conditional barrier probability:
$$
P(M_T<a\mid B_T=y)
=1-\exp\left(-\frac{2a(a-y)}T\right),\qquad y<a.
$$
Conditioning on an exact continuous endpoint is interpreted through a regular conditional density, not division by a positive point probability. The result describes a Brownian bridge from zero to $y$ over this interval.

**Worked example 14.5 (a pinned bridge).** Conditional on $B_1=0$, the probability that the path stays below one is $1-e^{-2}$. Both endpoints are zero, yet the conditional probability of crossing one is $e^{-2}>0$. The path still fluctuates between its endpoints. This also explains why linearly joining sparse observations can underestimate barrier crossings.

## Continuity, regularity and the scope of path claims

Brownian increments satisfy $E|B_t-B_s|^4=3|t-s|^2$. The Kolmogorov continuity theorem therefore supplies continuous versions of the Gaussian process constructed from finite-dimensional laws. Higher even Gaussian moments give local Hölder continuity for every exponent below one-half, using the full stated theorem. Continuity and these Hölder results do not imply differentiability.

Nowhere differentiability is a separate classical theorem; a proof is not being inferred from reflection or from a single quadratic-variation limit. The present book proves quadratic variation directly in the Brownian chapter. That result rules out finite total variation on a nontrivial interval, because a continuous bounded-variation path has squared-increment sums bounded by its maximum increment times its total variation, which tends to zero with mesh.

The reflection theorem is exact for continuous Brownian paths. A finite numerical grid sees only a subset of times. Its sampled maximum is at most the true maximum, and no number of endpoint histograms alone establishes an exact crossing probability. Bridge calculations can quantify what remains between observations under the model; they do not remove uncertainty about whether a real system follows the Brownian assumptions.

## A finite interval gives finite mean exit

The infinite mean for a single upper barrier contrasts with exit from a bounded interval. Fix $a,b>0$ and let $\eta=\inf\{t\ge0:B_t\notin(-a,b)\}$. Continuity makes the terminal point either $-a$ or $b$, once finiteness is known. Apply bounded optional sampling first to $\eta\wedge n$ for the martingales $B_t$ and $B_t^2-t$.

These continuous-time martingale sampling identities follow by approximating bounded stopping times on grids and using continuity and finite-horizon L2 maximal control. Until exit, $|B_{\eta\wedge n}|\le\max(a,b)$. Therefore
$$
E(\eta\wedge n)=E B_{\eta\wedge n}^2\le\max(a,b)^2.
$$
Monotone convergence gives $E\eta<\infty$, so exit is almost surely finite. Bounded convergence in the first martingale gives $E B_\eta=0$. If $p=P(B_\eta=b)$, then $bp-a(1-p)=0$, giving $p=a/(a+b)$. Bounded convergence in the second-moment identity now sharpens the duration to
$$
E\eta=E B_\eta^2
=b^2\frac a{a+b}+a^2\frac b{a+b}=ab.
$$
The proof establishes integrability before taking the unbounded-time limit. It does not simply write an optional-stopping identity at $\eta$ and presume its hypotheses.

**Worked example 14.6 (two barriers).** Between levels minus two and three, starting at zero, upper exit probability is $2/5$ and expected exit time is six. Removing the lower barrier changes both the event and the duration distribution: the time to reach three alone is finite almost surely but has infinite mean. A bounded spatial interval supplies control absent from the single-barrier problem.

For a start $x$ between lower level $\ell$ and upper level $u$, translating Brownian motion gives upper exit probability $(x-\ell)/(u-\ell)$ and mean duration $(x-\ell)(u-x)$. Both vanish or become certain as appropriate at the endpoints. These functions solve the harmonic and Poisson equations for the Brownian generator, which is one-half the second derivative.

## What cannot be reflected without revision

The proof uses symmetry of future Brownian increments. A process $B_t+vt$ with nonzero deterministic drift does not have that symmetry after negating its increments: the drift changes sign. Its maximum therefore cannot be obtained by blindly doubling its original endpoint tail. A change-of-measure or differential-equation argument can treat drift, but it needs additional justification.

Similarly, a two-sided crossing event is not the sum of independent upper and lower crossings. One path can visit both barriers. Adding the two one-sided reflection probabilities counts that overlap twice and may even exceed one. Exit questions are instead naturally posed through the first reached boundary, while maximum-and-minimum questions need their joint structure. The interval calculation above answers one such first-exit question without claiming independence of the two crossing events.

## Local Hölder regularity from Gaussian moments

**Theorem 14.4.** On every bounded interval, almost every Brownian path is Hölder continuous of each order $\alpha<1/2$. The random Hölder constant may depend on the interval and on the chosen exponent.

Here is a direct dyadic estimate using the already chosen continuous version. First take an interval of length one; scaling handles other lengths. Fix $0<\alpha<1/2$ and an even integer $p$ with $p(1/2-\alpha)>1$. At level $n$, each adjacent dyadic increment has $p$th absolute moment $c_p2^{-np/2}$. Markov's inequality and a union bound over $2^n$ increments bound the probability of any increment exceeding $2^{-n\alpha}$ by
\[
 c_p2^{-n[p(1/2-\alpha)-1]}.
\]
The exponent is positive, so these probabilities are summable. Outside one null event, every sufficiently fine dyadic increment obeys the bound. Absorb the finitely many coarser levels into a finite random multiplicative constant.

To pass from adjacent dyadic points to arbitrary $s<t$, choose $n$ so $2^{-n-1}<t-s\le2^{-n}$. Approximate both endpoints by dyadic points at levels $k\ge n$. Each refinement changes an endpoint by at most one adjacent increment at the finer level; the coarse endpoints are at most two adjacent level-$n$ intervals apart. The total change is bounded by a constant times
\[
 \sum_{k\ge n}2^{-k\alpha}
 =\frac{2^{-n\alpha}}{1-2^{-\alpha}}
 \le C_\alpha|t-s|^\alpha .
\]
Continuity passes the dyadic approximation to its limit. This proves the stated Hölder estimate. A countable family of exponents tending up to $1/2$, followed by a countable family of integer horizons, supplies the simultaneous local statement. Lower exponents follow on bounded intervals by comparison of powers.

The proof supplies no exponent $1/2$: the geometric probability estimate loses its positive exponent there. Brownian failure of local $1/2$-Hölder regularity, the law of the iterated logarithm, and nowhere differentiability require further arguments and remain advanced imported path theorems here. An unsuccessful bound at an endpoint is not itself proof that the endpoint statement is false.

## Immediate oscillation and the limits of stopping slogans

Reflection gives $\mathbb P(M_t\le0)=0$ for each $t>0$, by letting a positive barrier decrease to zero. Symmetry gives a negative minimum in the same interval almost surely. Intersecting over $t=1/n$ shows positive and negative values occur arbitrarily close to zero on one probability-one event. By the strong Markov theorem this also describes the fresh increments immediately after an almost surely finite stopping time. In particular, after a first hit, Brownian motion is not trapped on one side merely because the first crossing position was exactly the boundary.

This observation does not say that every zero of every path can be selected as a stopping time. Nor does it establish differentiability or a rate of oscillation. It illustrates the difference between qualitative crossing behaviour, quantitative moment regularity and exact distributional identities. Each has its own proof and quantifiers.

A useful audit has four parts. Specify the information filtration, verify that the chosen random time is a stopping time, state the theorem that supplies its future law, and check the integrability needed for any expectation identity. The one-barrier time shows why these checks are separate: it is an almost surely finite stopping time with a fresh Brownian future, but its mean is infinite. The two-barrier exit time from the preceding chapter has finite mean because stopping keeps the process spatially bounded. These facts coexist without contradiction.



## Development: arrival-time quantiles and fractional moments

A finite median is often more informative than the nonexistent mean in this one-barrier problem. At barrier $a>0$, solving $P(\tau_a\le m)=1/2$ gives $\Phi(a/\sqrt m)=3/4$, hence
\[
 m=\frac{a^2}{[\Phi^{-1}(3/4)]^2}.
\]
More generally, the $q$-quantile is $a^2/[\Phi^{-1}(1-q/2)]^2$ for $0<q<1$. The denominator is positive and finite; at the endpoints the limiting time approaches zero or infinity respectively. Each fixed quantile scales quadratically in the barrier, while the infinite mean remains infinite. These are different summaries of the same law rather than contradictory estimates.

The hitting density also identifies precisely which positive moments exist. For $r>0$, its $r$th moment integrates a constant times $t^{r-3/2}\exp[-a^2/(2t)]$. Near infinity the exponential is bounded above by one and below by a positive constant, so integrability is equivalent to $r-3/2<-1$, or $r<1/2$. Near zero, the substitution $u=a^2/(2t)$ turns the integral into an exponentially decaying tail times a power of $u$, finite for every fixed $r$. Thus positive moments exist exactly for $0<r<1/2$. At the boundary $r=1/2$ the divergent tail is logarithmic. This proves a sharper statement than simply noting the infinite first moment.

The distributional identity $M_T\stackrel d=|B_T|$ likewise supplies an expectation without identifying paths:
\[
 EM_T=2\int_0^\infty x(2\pi T)^{-1/2}
             e^{-x^2/(2T)}\,dx=\sqrt{2T/\pi}.
\]
The substitution $u=x^2/(2T)$ evaluates the integral. A finite mean maximum on a fixed horizon and an infinite mean time to reach a fixed level coexist because one holds the time fixed and varies the level, whereas the other holds the level fixed and varies time. Interchanging those questions without transforming their tail integrals is a common source of false intuition.

## Summary

Continuity turns barrier crossings into stopping times with exact contact. Approximation proves the bounded stopping-time restart property, and symmetry then preserves Brownian law under reflection. This yields maximum probabilities, first-arrival densities and killed endpoint densities. The first arrival is finite almost surely with infinite mean. Endpoint conditioning describes bridges and exposes crossings missed by a finite observation grid.
