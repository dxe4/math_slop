
# Abelian Categories

In **Modules: linear algebra over a ring**, every construction---kernels, images, quotients, direct sums---was carried out inside the category of modules over a fixed ring. The elements of a module played an essential role: we spoke of $rm$, of $f(m)$, of the subgroup generated by a set. This chapter asks whether the same structure can be described without mentioning elements at all. The answer is yes, and the resulting abstraction is called an **abelian category**.

Abelian categories were introduced to unify modules over rings, sheaves of abelian groups, and representations of groups and algebras under one roof. The payoff is enormous: theorems proved in the abstract setting apply simultaneously to all concrete examples. The price is pedagogical care: without elements, one must learn to chase arrows instead of elements.

## 2.1 Categories revisited

The categorical definition is developed in [Introduction to Category Theory](#/books/introduction-to-category-theory/categories-and-examples); we recall it here before adding exactness structure.

A **category** $\mathcal{C}$ consists of:

1. a class $\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal{C})$ of **objects**;
2. for each pair $A, B \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal{C})$, a set $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,B)$ of **morphisms**;
3. for each $A \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal{C})$, an **identity morphism** $\operatorname{id}_{A} \in \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,A)$;
4. a **composition** operation $\circ: \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(B,C) \times \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,B) \to \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,C)$, written $g \circ f$,

such that composition is associative and identities act as units:

$$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f, \qquad \operatorname{id}_{B} \circ f = f = f \circ \operatorname{id}_{A}.$$

**Example 2.1.** The category $\mathbf{Set}$ has sets as objects and functions as morphisms. The category $\mathbf{Grp}$ has groups and group homomorphisms. The category $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ has left $R$-modules and $R$-linear maps.

**Example 2.2.** A partially ordered set $(P, \leq)$ is a category with objects the elements of $P$ and a unique morphism $p \to q$ whenever $p \leq q$. Composition is forced by transitivity.

**Example 2.3.** A monoid $M$ is a category with one object; morphisms are elements of $M$ and composition is monoid multiplication. A group is a category with one object in which every morphism is invertible.

A morphism $f: A \to B$ is an **isomorphism** if there exists $g: B \to A$ with $gf = \operatorname{id}_{A}$ and $fg = \operatorname{id}_{B}$. It is a **monomorphism** if $fh = fk$ implies $h = k$ for all $h,k$ with codomain $A$. It is an **epimorphism** if $hf = kf$ implies $h = k$ for all $h,k$ with domain $B$.

In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, monomorphisms are exactly injective linear maps and epimorphisms are surjective linear maps. In $\mathbf{Ring}$, the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ is an epimorphism that is not surjective: any two ring homomorphisms out of $\mathbb{Q}$ agreeing on $\mathbb{Z}$ are equal.

## 2.2 Initial, terminal, and zero objects

An object $0$ is **initial** if for every object $X$ there is exactly one morphism $0 \to X$. An object $1$ is **terminal** if for every $X$ there is exactly one morphism $X \to 1$.

**Proposition 2.4.** Initial objects are unique up to unique isomorphism. The same holds for terminal objects.

*Proof.* If $0$ and $0'$ are initial, there are unique morphisms $f: 0 \to 0'$ and $g: 0' \to 0$. The composite $gf: 0 \to 0$ is a morphism from $0$ to $0$; by uniqueness it must be $\operatorname{id}_{0}$. Similarly $fg = \operatorname{id}_{0'}$. $\square$

In $\mathbf{Set}$, the empty set is initial and every singleton is terminal. In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the zero module is both initial and terminal. A category in which the initial and terminal objects coincide is called a **pointed category**.

A **zero object** is an object that is both initial and terminal. In a pointed category, every hom-set $\operatorname{Hom}(A,B)$ has a distinguished **zero morphism**, namely the unique composite $A \to 0 \to B$.

## 2.3 Products and coproducts

A **product** of objects $A$ and $B$ is an object $A \times B$ together with projection morphisms $\pi_{A}: A \times B \to A$ and $\pi_{B}: A \times B \to B$ such that for every object $X$ and pair of morphisms $f: X \to A$, $g: X \to B$, there exists a unique morphism $(f,g): X \to A \times B$ satisfying

$$\pi_A\circ(f,g)=f,\qquad \pi_B\circ(f,g)=g.$$

A **coproduct** $A \sqcup B$ (or $A \oplus B$ in additive notation) is the dual notion: it comes with injections $\iota_{A}: A \to A \sqcup B$ and $\iota_{B}: B \to A \sqcup B$, and any pair $f: A \to X$, $g: B \to X$ factors uniquely through $A \sqcup B$.

In $\mathbf{Set}$, the product is the Cartesian product and the coproduct is the disjoint union, which generally differ. In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, finite products and coproducts coincide and are given by the direct sum $A \oplus B$ with its standard projections and injections. Such finite biproducts occur in additive categories, including abelian categories; they do not require the additional abelian-category axioms.

## 2.4 Kernels and cokernels

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a category with a zero object. The **kernel** of a morphism $f: A \to B$ is a morphism $k: K \to A$ such that $fk = 0$ and for every $h: X \to A$ with $fh = 0$, there is a unique $u: X \to K$ with $ku = h$.

The **cokernel** of $f$ is the dual notion: a morphism $c: B \to C$ such that $cf = 0$ and every $h: B \to X$ with $hf = 0$ factors uniquely through $C$.

In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the categorical kernel is the inclusion $\operatorname{ker} f \hookrightarrow A$, and the categorical cokernel is the quotient map $B \to B/\operatorname{im} f$.

**Definition 2.5.** A category is **preadditive** if every hom-set is an abelian group and composition is bilinear. It is **additive** if it is preadditive, has a zero object, and finite products and coproducts exist and coincide.

## 2.5 Abelian categories

For any map f in an additive category with kernels and cokernels, define its coimage as coker(ker f) and its image as ker(coker f). These include a quotient q from the source and an inclusion i into the target. There is a canonical factorisation f=i h q: first f factors through q because it kills its kernel; then coker(f) kills that factor because q is epic, so it factors through i.

An **abelian category** is an additive category with all kernels and cokernels in which this canonical comparison h from coimage to image is an isomorphism for every map. Thus the categorical first isomorphism theorem is built into the definition, with its specified comparison rather than an arbitrary abstract isomorphism between two objects.

This implies the normality properties used below: every monomorphism is the kernel of its cokernel, and every epimorphism is the cokernel of its kernel. For a mono, its kernel is zero by cancellation. Its coimage quotient is therefore an identity up to the unique compatible isomorphism. The canonical comparison then identifies the mono with the image kernel inclusion. The dual argument applies to an epi. These are the usual normal-mono and normal-epi axioms, here recovered with their actual universal maps.

The object on the left of the first-isomorphism comparison is called **coimage**, and the one on the right **image**. Their canonical isomorphism allows them to describe the same factor, while the image still carries its specified monomorphism into the target.

**Example 2.6.** The category $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ is abelian. Kernels and cokernels are the usual ones, and the second axiom follows from the first isomorphism theorem for modules.

**Example 2.7.** The category of finitely generated abelian groups is abelian. Kernels of maps between finitely generated groups are finitely generated, and cokernels are obviously finitely generated.

**Example 2.8 (Non-example).** The category of free abelian groups is additive but not abelian. In it, multiplication by $2$ on $\mathbb Z$ is both monic and epic: any map from $\mathbb Z$ that kills $2\mathbb Z$ is zero because its free codomain is torsion-free. It is not an isomorphism. (Its categorical cokernel in this subcategory is the zero map $\mathbb Z\to0$, even though its cokernel in $\mathbf{Ab}$ is $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$.) This violates the balanced property proved in Exercise 2.2 for abelian categories.

**Example 2.9.** The category of sheaves of abelian groups on a topological space is abelian. This was one of Grothendieck's motivating examples: one wants to do homological algebra with sheaves without reducing to stalks.

## 2.6 Exactness in abelian categories

A sequence $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ in an abelian category is **exact at $B$** if $\operatorname{im} f = \operatorname{ker} g$ as subobjects of $B$. A short exact sequence is a five-term exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$.

Because every monomorphism is a kernel and every epimorphism is a cokernel, the notion of subobject is well behaved: subobjects of $B$ correspond to equivalence classes of monomorphisms into $B$.

**Proposition 2.10 (Snake lemma).** Given a commutative diagram in an abelian category with exact rows

$$
\begin{array}{ccccccccc}
0 & \to & A & \xrightarrow{f} & B & \xrightarrow{g} & C & \to & 0 \\
  &     & \downarrow^{a} & & \downarrow^{b} & & \downarrow^{c} & & \\
0 & \to & A' & \xrightarrow{f'} & B' & \xrightarrow{g'} & C' & \to & 0
\end{array}
$$

there is a natural exact sequence

$$\operatorname{ker} a \to \operatorname{ker} b \to \operatorname{ker} c \xrightarrow{\partial} \operatorname{coker} a \to \operatorname{coker} b \to \operatorname{coker} c.$$

*Proof for modules.* For x in ker c, lift it to y in B. Since g′b(y)=cg(y)=0, there is a unique z in A′ with b(y)=f′(z). Set ∂x to the class of z modulo im a. Another lift differs by f(u), so its z differs by a(u), proving independence. Additivity and scalar compatibility follow by choosing sums and multiples of lifts.

At ker b, an element mapping to zero in ker c is f(u); its zero b-image and injectivity of f′ imply a(u)=0. At ker c, a zero connecting class means z=a(u), so y−f(u) lies in ker b and still maps to x. These give exactness at the two kernel positions, including the evident injectivity of ker a into ker b.

At coker a, a class z maps to zero exactly when f′(z)=b(y) for some y. Then x=g(y) lies in ker c and has ∂x=[z]. At coker b, a class y′ maps to zero exactly when g′(y′)=c(x) for some x in C. Lift x to y in B; then y′−b(y) lies in ker g′ and hence in im f′, showing that its cokernel class comes from coker a. Finally surjectivity of g′ gives surjectivity onto coker c. The composites are zero directly from the diagram, so these reverse containments prove exactness everywhere, with zero at both ends if included.

A map between such diagrams sends the chosen lifts to valid lifts and preserves the constructed z, proving naturality. The reduction to modules for an arbitrary abelian category, using only this finite diagram and the imported embedding theorem, is justified in the next section. ∎

## 2.7 The Freyd-Mitchell embedding theorem

The following theorem justifies the use of element-wise arguments in abelian categories:

**Theorem 2.11 (Freyd--Mitchell; imported).** Every small abelian category admits a fully faithful exact embedding into $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ for some ring $R$.

The construction of the ring and embedding is a substantial theorem outside this chapter; the references give complete proofs. Its exact, fully faithful embedding justifies element chases for finite diagrams while preserving their categorical content.

This means that any statement about finite limits and colimits in a small abelian category can be proved by diagram chase in a module category. The theorem is powerful but non-constructive: it does not tell us what $R$ is.

### Small subcategories for a finite diagram

The smallness hypothesis in the imported theorem can be supplied for a fixed finite diagram in a locally small abelian category. Start with its finite set of objects and a zero object. At each stage include finite biproducts and a chosen kernel and cokernel for every map between the current objects. Local smallness makes the maps a set, so each stage and the countable union of stages remain sets of objects. Take the full subcategory on this union. Every finite collection of objects appears at a common stage, so all their required kernels, cokernels and finite biproducts appear later. The inherited canonical coimage-image isomorphisms are arrows there by fullness. Thus it is a small abelian subcategory containing the diagram, and its inclusion preserves those exact constructions.

Applying Freyd–Mitchell to that subcategory gives a fully faithful exact module embedding. Exactness reflects the finite kernel/image equations used in the snake proof and five lemma. This does not construct one embedding of the entire large category, nor does it authorise a proper-class diagram chase. The embedding theorem itself remains the explicitly imported step, with its source retained in the bibliography.

### Functor categories give more examples

For small J and an abelian category A, the functor category [J,A] is abelian, with kernels and cokernels constructed objectwise. For a natural transformation f:F⇒G, naturality makes each diagram arrow send a component kernel into the next component kernel; its universal property gives the restricted arrow uniquely. Identity and composition follow from that uniqueness, giving a kernel functor. The same construction with quotients gives a cokernel functor. Their naturality and universal factorisations are checked at every object. Finite biproducts and addition are also pointwise. The coimage-image comparison is an isomorphism at each object because A is abelian, hence is a natural isomorphism. This proves the abelian axioms and shows exactness is objectwise in this functor category.

## 2.8 Common errors

- **Assuming all categories are concrete.** The morphisms in an abelian category need not be functions on underlying sets. Arguments must use universal properties.
- **Confusing monic with injective.** In a general category, monomorphisms need not be injective functions; but in an abelian category, monomorphisms are exactly the kernels of some morphism, which is the categorical analogue of injectivity.
- **Forgetting that images are subobjects, not objects.** The image of $f: A \to B$ is a monomorphism into $B$, not simply an object. Different monomorphisms with isomorphic domains may represent different subobjects.
- **Applying set-theoretic arguments without checking they are finite.** The Freyd-Mitchell theorem applies to small categories. Large categories such as the category of all modules require more care.

## 2.9 Exercises

### Exercise 2.1

Show that in any category, the product of two objects, if it exists, is unique up to unique isomorphism.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Use each product property to build a compatible arrow.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Compare both projections to identify each composite.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Suppose $P$ and $P'$ are both products of $A$ and $B$ with projections $\pi_{A}, \pi_{B}$ and $\pi'_{A}, \pi'_{B}$. By the universal property of $P$, there is a unique $u: P' \to P$ with $\pi_{A} u = \pi'_{A}$ and $\pi_{B} u = \pi'_{B}$. Similarly there is $v: P \to P'$ with $\pi'_{A} v = \pi_{A}$ and $\pi'_{B} v = \pi_{B}$. Then $\pi_{A} uv = \pi_{A}$ and $\pi_{B} uv = \pi_{B}$, so $uv = \operatorname{id}_{P}$ by uniqueness. Likewise $vu = \operatorname{id}_{P'}$. $\square$
</details>

### Exercise 2.2

Prove that in an abelian category, a morphism that is both monic and epic is an isomorphism.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Normality identifies a mono with the kernel of its cokernel.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Epicity makes that cokernel map zero.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Let $f: A \to B$ be monic and epic. Since $f$ is monic, it is the kernel of its cokernel $c: B \to C$. Since $f$ is epic, $c = 0$. The kernel of the zero map $B \to C$ is $\operatorname{id}_{B}: B \to B$. By uniqueness of kernels up to isomorphism, $A \cong B$ and $f$ is an isomorphism. $\square$
</details>

### Exercise 2.3

In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, show that the direct sum $A \oplus B$ satisfies both the product and coproduct universal properties.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Construct pairing and copairing using coordinates.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Uniqueness follows by projecting or by the two summand inclusions.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Projections $\pi_{A}(a,b) = a$ and $\pi_{B}(a,b) = b$ give the product structure: given $f: X \to A$ and $g: X \to B$, define $(f,g)(x) = (f(x), g(x))$. Injections $\iota_{A}(a) = (a,0)$ and $\iota_{B}(b) = (0,b)$ give the coproduct structure: given $f: A \to X$ and $g: B \to X$, define $[f,g](a,b) = f(a) + g(b)$. Both maps are unique by construction. $\square$
</details>

### Exercise 2.4

Show that the category of torsion-free abelian groups is not abelian.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Test multiplication by two on the integers.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
All permitted target groups have no nonzero two-torsion.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Multiplication by $2$ on $\mathbb Z$ is monic. It is also epic among torsion-free abelian groups: if $u,v:\mathbb Z\to T$ agree after multiplication by $2$, then $2(u(1)-v(1))=0$, and torsion-freeness gives $u=v$. It is not an isomorphism. Thus this category contains a morphism that is monic and epic but not invertible, contradicting Exercise 2.2 for an abelian category. Its categorical cokernel inside the subcategory is $\mathbb Z\to0$; the fact that the ambient cokernel $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ leaves the subcategory is not by itself a proof that no categorical cokernel exists. $\square$
</details>

### Exercise 2.5

State and prove the five lemma in an arbitrary abelian category.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Prove the middle map monic and epic separately.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Use the small-diagram reduction above, then lift and correct the relevant elements.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Given a commutative diagram with exact rows

$$
\begin{array}{ccccccccc}
A_{1} & \to & A_{2} & \to & A_{3} & \to & A_{4} & \to & A_{5} \\
\downarrow & & \downarrow & & \downarrow & & \downarrow & & \downarrow \\
B_{1} & \to & B_{2} & \to & B_{3} & \to & B_{4} & \to & B_{5}
\end{array}
$$

if $f_{1}$ is epic, $f_{2}$ and $f_{4}$ are isomorphisms, and $f_{5}$ is monic, then $f_{3}$ is an isomorphism.

By Freyd--Mitchell, the finite diagram may be chased in a module category. For injectivity, suppose $f_3(x)=0$. The image of $x$ in $A_4$ maps to zero in $B_4$, so it is zero because $f_4$ is monic. Exactness writes $x$ as the image of some $y\in A_2$. The image $f_2(y)$ lies in the kernel of $B_2\to B_3$, so it comes from some $z\in B_1$. Choose $w\in A_1$ with $f_1(w)=z$. Commutativity makes $f_2(y-\operatorname{im}w)=0$; since $f_2$ is monic, $y=\operatorname{im}w$, and consequently $x=0$.

For surjectivity, take $b\in B_3$. Its image in $B_4$ lifts uniquely along $f_4$ to $a\in A_4$. The image of $a$ in $A_5$ maps to zero in $B_5$, so it is zero because $f_5$ is monic. Thus $a$ comes from some $x\in A_3$. Now $b-f_3(x)$ maps to zero in $B_4$, hence comes from $y\in B_2$. Choose the inverse image of $y$ under the isomorphism $f_2$ and add its image to $x$; the resulting element maps to $b$. Therefore $f_3$ is monic and epic, hence an isomorphism by Exercise 2.2. $\square$
</details>


### HA-05-06 — Coimage and image with their maps

For multiplication by six from Z to Z, identify coimage, image and the canonical comparison. Explain why invertibility of the comparison does not make the original map invertible.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
The kernel is zero and the cokernel is Z/6.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The image is the subgroup 6Z with its inclusion.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The coimage is Z and the image object is 6Z, included in the target Z. The canonical comparison sends k to 6k as an element of 6Z, and has inverse 6k↦k. Thus it is an isomorphism. The original map additionally composes with the inclusion 6Z→Z, which is not onto and is not invertible. Keeping the inclusion in the image factorisation prevents confusing an isomorphism onto an image with an isomorphism onto the original target.
</details>

### HA-05-07 — Zero kernel criterion

In an additive category with kernels, prove a map is monic exactly when its kernel object is zero.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
For the converse, subtract two arrows with equal composites.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
For the forward direction, use that a kernel inclusion is monic.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
If f is monic, its kernel arrow k satisfies fk=0=f0, so k=0. Since a kernel arrow is monic, cancellation of this zero arrow forces id_K=0, making K a zero object. Conversely if K is zero and fa=fb, the difference a−b has zero composite with f and factors through K. Thus a−b=0 and a=b. Addition of parallel arrows is the hypothesis enabling this latter subtraction. The dual argument gives the zero-cokernel criterion for epicity.
</details>

### HA-05-08 — Kernel of a transformation

In the arrow-diagram category of abelian groups, let F be Z→Z by multiplication by two and G be C₂→C₂ by zero. Reduce modulo two at each object. Compute the kernel diagram of this natural transformation, including its arrow.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Both component kernels are 2Z.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Restrict F's arrow; it sends 2k to 4k.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The square commutes because reduction after multiplication by two is zero, as is G's arrow after reduction. Its kernel diagram has 2Z at both objects. The connecting arrow is the restriction of multiplication by two, so in the bases given by the generator two of each copy it is again multiplication by two. It is not the zero arrow merely because the target quotient arrow was zero. The kernel's inclusion transformation and its componentwise universal properties determine this action exactly.
</details>

### HA-05-09 — A failed canonical comparison

In free abelian groups, compute the canonical coimage-image comparison for multiplication by two on Z.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Its kernel is zero and its categorical cokernel in this subcategory is zero.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The comparison is still the original multiplication map between the resulting objects.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The coimage is Z because the kernel is zero. The image is also Z because it is the kernel of the zero cokernel map. Under those canonical identifications the comparison is multiplication by two, which is not invertible. Thus this additive category fails the abelian comparison axiom even though the objects needed in this particular kernel/cokernel calculation exist. The actual comparison arrow, rather than an abstract isomorphism between the two copies of Z, is essential.
</details>

### HA-05-10 — Splitting without elements

In a short exact sequence A→B→C in an abelian category, suppose the inclusion i has retraction r with ri=id. Construct a section of the quotient q using only arrows.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
The map id_B−ir kills i and factors through its cokernel q.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Cancel the epimorphism q to prove the section equation.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Factor id_B−ir as sq for s:C→B, since its composite with i is zero. Then qsq=q because qi=0. Epicity of q gives qs=id_C. Also rsq=r−rir=0, so rs=0. The maps (r,q):B→A⊕C and the copairing [i,s]:A⊕C→B are inverse: their composite on B is ir+sq=id, and the four biproduct-coordinate equations give identity on A⊕C. This proves splitting through universal maps without assigning elements to an abstract abelian category.
</details>
