
# Modules: linear algebra over a ring

Vector spaces are the backbone of undergraduate linear algebra, but their good behavior depends on the field hypothesis: every nonzero scalar is invertible, every subspace has a complement, and every linear map has a matrix representation after choosing bases. When the scalars come from a general ring, many of these privileges disappear. The resulting theory---modules over a ring---is richer, more subtle, and essential for modern algebra, algebraic geometry, and homological methods.

This chapter defines modules and module homomorphisms, studies free and finitely presented modules, introduces Noetherian finiteness, and uses exact sequences to organize the relationship between submodules, quotients, and extensions. The examples stay concrete: abelian groups as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, matrices over principal ideal domains, and explicit short exact sequences that fail to split.

## 1.1 Definition and first examples

Let $R$ be a ring with identity $1 \neq 0$. A **left $R$-module** $M$ is an abelian group $(M,+)$ together with a scalar multiplication $R \times M \to M$, written $(r,m) \mapsto rm$, satisfying the following axioms for all $r,s \in R$ and $m,n \in M$:

1. $r(m+n) = rm + rn$,
2. $(r+s)m = rm + sm$,
3. $(rs)m = r(sm)$,
4. $1m = m$.

A **right $R$-module** is defined analogously with multiplication $M \times R \to M$ written $mr$ and associativity $(mr)s = m(rs)$. When $R$ is commutative, the two notions coincide up to notation; we work with left modules unless stated otherwise.

**Example 1.1 (Vector spaces).** If $R = k$ is a field, a $k$-module is exactly a vector space over $k$. All four axioms are the familiar vector space laws.

**Example 1.2 (Abelian groups).** If $R = \mathbb{Z}$, a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is precisely an abelian group. The scalar multiplication is forced by the axioms:

$$nm = \underbrace{m + m + \cdots + m}_{n\text{ times}}, \qquad (-n)m = -(nm).$$

Conversely, every abelian group admits this unique $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure. Thus the theory of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is the theory of abelian groups.

**Example 1.3 (Ideals).** If $R$ is commutative, every ideal $I \subseteq R$ is an $R$-module under the ring multiplication. In particular, $R$ itself is an $R$-module.

**Example 1.4 (Matrices).** The set $M_{m \times n}(R)$ of $m \times n$ matrices over $R$ is an $R$-module under entrywise addition and scalar multiplication. When $m=n$, it is also a ring, but the module structure does not require matrix multiplication.

**Example 1.5 (The zero module).** The trivial group $\{0\}$ with $r \cdot 0 = 0$ is the **zero module**, denoted $0$. It plays the role of the zero vector space.

### Submodules and quotient modules

A **submodule** $N \subseteq M$ is a subgroup closed under scalar multiplication: if $n \in N$ and $r \in R$, then $rn \in N$. The quotient $M/N$ is the quotient abelian group with scalar multiplication $r(m+N) = rm + N$. One checks this is well defined exactly when $N$ is a submodule.

**Definition 1.6.** An $R$-module $M$ is **cyclic** if it is generated by a single element: $M = Rm$ for some $m \in M$. It is **finitely generated** if there exist $m_{1}, \dots, m_{n} \in M$ such that every element of $M$ is an $R$-linear combination $r_{1}m_{1} + \cdots + r_{n}m_{n}$.

The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic for every $n$. The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}^{2}$ is finitely generated but not cyclic.

## 1.2 Module homomorphisms

An **$R$-linear map** (or **module homomorphism**) $f: M \to N$ is a homomorphism of abelian groups satisfying $f(rm) = rf(m)$ for all $r \in R$ and $m \in M$. The set of all such maps is denoted $\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,N)$; it is an abelian group under pointwise addition and, when $R$ is commutative, an $R$-module via $(rf)(m) = r \cdot f(m)$.

**Proposition 1.7 (First isomorphism theorem).** Let $f: M \to N$ be an $R$-linear map. Then

$$M/\operatorname{ker} f \cong \operatorname{im} f$$

via the map $m + \operatorname{ker} f \mapsto f(m)$.

*Proof.* The map is well defined because $f(m) = f(m')$ whenever $m - m' \in \operatorname{ker} f$. It is $R$-linear because $f$ is, and it is bijective by construction. $\square$

The kernel and image of an $R$-linear map are submodules of $M$ and $N$ respectively. The cokernel is $\operatorname{coker} f = N/\operatorname{im} f$.

**Definition 1.8.** A map $f: M \to N$ is **injective** (a **monomorphism**) if $\operatorname{ker} f = 0$. It is **surjective** (an **epimorphism**) if $\operatorname{im} f = N$. It is an **isomorphism** if there exists $g: N \to M$ with $gf = \operatorname{id}_{M}$ and $fg = \operatorname{id}_{N}$.

## 1.3 Free modules and the universal property

A **free $R$-module** on a set $S$ is an $R$-module $F(S)$ together with a map $i: S \to F(S)$ such that for every $R$-module $M$ and every set map $\varphi: S \to M$, there exists a unique $R$-linear map $\tilde{\varphi}: F(S) \to M$ with $\tilde{\varphi} \circ i = \varphi$.

Concretely, $F(S)$ is the direct sum of copies of $R$ indexed by $S$:

$$F(S) = \bigoplus_{s \in S} R = \{ (r_{s})_{s \in S} : r_{s} = 0 \text{ for all but finitely many } s \}.$$

The map $i$ sends $s$ to the basis element $e_{s}$ which is $1$ in coordinate $s$ and $0$ elsewhere. When $S = \{1, \dots, n\}$, we recover $R^{n}$ with its standard basis.

**Proposition 1.9.** The free module $F(S)$ is unique up to unique isomorphism.

*Proof.* If $(F', i')$ is another free module on $S$, the universal property of $F$ gives a unique linear map $F \to F'$ extending $i'$, and vice versa. The composites in either direction are linear maps extending the identity on $S$, hence equal to the identity by uniqueness. $\square$

**Worked example 1.10.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and $S = \{a,b\}$. Then $F(S) = \mathbb{Z}^{2}$. Given the set map $\varphi(a) = 3$ and $\varphi(b) = 5$ into $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, the unique extension is

$$\tilde{\varphi}(x,y) = 3x + 5y \pmod{12}.$$

We verify: $\tilde{\varphi}(1,0) = 3 = \varphi(a)$ and $\tilde{\varphi}(0,1) = 5 = \varphi(b)$. Linearity follows from the ring structure of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.

### Presentations

A finitely presented $R$-module is one that can be described by generators and relations. For the matrix convention below, assume $R$ is commutative. Explicitly, there exists an exact sequence

$$R^{m} \xrightarrow{A} R^{n} \to M \to 0,$$

where $A$ is an $n \times m$ matrix over $R$. With column vectors, the columns of $A$ are relations among $n$ generators. For a noncommutative ring, left-module presentations require a compatible row-vector convention or matrices over the opposite ring. Over a principal ideal domain, the Smith normal form diagonalizes $A$ and yields the structure theorem for finitely generated modules.

**Worked example 1.11.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$$

gives the presentation $\mathbb{Z}^{2} \xrightarrow{A} \mathbb{Z}^{2} \to M \to 0$. The cokernel is

$$M \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}.$$

The order of $M$ is $60$, and by the Chinese remainder theorem,

$$M \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/30\mathbb{Z}.$$

**Retained symbolic illustration (not executed).**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, ZZ
from sympy.matrices.normalforms import smith_normal_form

A = Matrix([[6, 0], [0, 10]])
print(smith_normal_form(A, domain=ZZ))
```

The invariant factors also follow directly from the entry gcd two and determinant sixty: they are two and thirty. The retained illustration is not an execution claim.

## 1.4 Noetherian modules and finite control

An $R$-module $M$ is **Noetherian** if every ascending chain of submodules stabilizes: given $N_{1} \subseteq N_{2} \subseteq \cdots$, there exists $k$ such that $N_{k} = N_{k+1} = \cdots$.

**Proposition 1.12.** An $R$-module $M$ is Noetherian if and only if every submodule of $M$ is finitely generated.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $N \subseteq M$ is not finitely generated. Choose $n_{1} \in N$. Since $N \neq Rn_{1}$, choose $n_{2} \in N \setminus Rn_{1}$. Inductively choose $n_{k+1} \in N \setminus (Rn_{1} + \cdots + Rn_{k})$. The chain

$$Rn_{1} \subsetneq Rn_{1} + Rn_{2} \subsetneq \cdots$$

never stabilizes, contradicting the Noetherian hypothesis.

($\Leftarrow$) Given an ascending chain $N_{1} \subseteq N_{2} \subseteq \cdots$, the union $N = \bigcup_{i} N_{i}$ is a submodule. By hypothesis, $N$ is finitely generated, say by $x_{1}, \dots, x_{r}$. Each $x_{j}$ lies in some $N_{i_{j}}$; let $k = \max\{i_{1}, \dots, i_{r}\}$. Then all $x_{j} \in N_{k}$, so $N = N_{k} = N_{k+1} = \cdots$. $\square$

A commutative ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if it is Noetherian as a module over itself, equivalently if every ideal is finitely generated.

**Examples 1.13.** Fields, principal ideal domains, and $\mathbb{Z}$ are Noetherian. The polynomial ring $k[x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots]$ in infinitely many variables is not: the ideal $(x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots)$ is not finitely generated, and the chain

$$(x_{1}) \subsetneq (x_{1}, x_{2}) \subsetneq (x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}) \subsetneq \cdots$$

never stabilizes.

**Theorem 1.14 (Hilbert basis theorem).** If $R$ is a commutative Noetherian ring, then $R[x]$ is Noetherian.

*Proof.* The zero ideal is immediate. For a nonzero ideal I, include zero among its leading coefficients. These coefficients form an ideal J: align degrees by multiplying two chosen polynomials by powers of x to check addition, and use scalar multiplication to check absorption; a cancelled leading coefficient contributes zero. Since R is Noetherian, finitely many coefficients a_i generate J. Choose f_i in I with those leading coefficients and let d be their maximum degree.

The submodule L of polynomials in I of degree below d lies in the finite free module of coefficient vectors R^d. Finite free modules are Noetherian by Proposition 1.15 and induction, so choose finitely many generators of L. For any f in I of degree N at least d, express its leading coefficient as a linear combination of the a_i. Subtract that combination of the polynomials x raised to N minus deg f_i times f_i. Its leading coefficient cancels, so the degree decreases. Repeating reaches L after finitely many steps. Thus the f_i and the chosen low-degree generators generate I as an ideal, proving the theorem. ∎

Iterating, $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ is Noetherian for every field $k$ and every finite $n$. The infinite-variable counterexample shows the finiteness hypothesis is essential.

**Proposition 1.15.** In a short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$, the module $B$ is Noetherian if and only if both $A$ and $C$ are Noetherian.

*Proof.* Chains in A are chains in B and chains in C lift to inverse-image chains in B, proving necessity. Conversely take a chain B_j of submodules. Both its intersections with A and its images in C stabilise, so choose a common stage k. For j at least k and x in B_j, choose y in B_k with the same image in C. Then x minus y lies in the intersection of B_j with A, which equals the intersection of B_k with A. Hence x lies in B_k. Every later chain member equals B_k, proving sufficiency. ∎

## 1.5 Exact sequences

A sequence of $R$-module maps

$$\cdots \to A_{i-1} \xrightarrow{f_{i-1}} A_{i} \xrightarrow{f_{i}} A_{i+1} \to \cdots$$

is **exact at $A_{i}$** if $\operatorname{im} f_{i-1} = \operatorname{ker} f_{i}$. It is **exact** if it is exact at every internal position.

A **short exact sequence** is a five-term exact sequence

$$0 \to A \xrightarrow{i} B \xrightarrow{p} C \to 0.$$

Exactness encodes three facts simultaneously: $i$ is injective, $p$ is surjective, and $\operatorname{im} i = \operatorname{ker} p$. Thus $A$ is identified with a submodule of $B$, and $C \cong B/A$.

**Worked example 1.16.** For any positive integer $n$,

$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\times n\,} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\bmod n\,} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to 0$$

is exact. Multiplication by $n$ is injective; reduction modulo $n$ is surjective; and $\operatorname{ker}(\bmod n) = n\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{im}(\times n)$.

**Worked example 1.17 (Nonsplitting).** The sequence

$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\times 2\,} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\bmod 2\,} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$$

does **not** split as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules. A splitting would require a section $s: \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ with $(\bmod 2) \circ s = \operatorname{id}$. The image of $1 \in \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ would be an integer $z$ with $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, so $z$ is odd. But $2z = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ only when $z = 0$, a contradiction. Hence $\mathbb{Z} \not\cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

This failure is the germ of extension theory: short exact sequences classify the ways $C$ can be built from $A$ with a given quotient, and the nontrivial extensions are measured by $\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{R}(C,A)$.

## 1.6 Common errors

- **Treating modules like vector spaces.** Over a general ring, not every submodule has a complement, not every module is free, and division by nonunits is meaningless.
- **Checking exactness at only some positions.** Exactness is a local condition; verify it at every object.
- **Assuming every short exact sequence splits.** This holds for vector spaces but fails already for $\mathbb{Z}$-modules.
- **Confusing "generated by" with "free on."** The cyclic module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by one element but is not free on one generator because the universal property fails: the map sending the generator to $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ does not extend to a $\mathbb{Z}$-linear map $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$.

## 1.7 Exercises

### Exercise 1.1

For an integer $n \ne 0$, show that the only $\mathbb{Z}$-module homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is the zero map.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
A map is determined by the image of the residue generator.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
That integer image is killed by n.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Let $f: \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ be $\mathbb{Z}$-linear. Then $f(\overline{1})$ is an integer $m$ satisfying $nm = f(n \cdot \overline{1}) = f(\overline{0}) = 0$. Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free, $m = 0$. Thus $f(\overline{k}) = km = 0$ for all $k$. $\square$
</details>

### Exercise 1.2

Prove that a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Use the finite free surjection onto the generated module.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Prove finite free Noetherianity by the exact-sequence criterion.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Let $M$ be generated by $m_{1}, \dots, m_{n}$. The map $R^{n} \to M$ sending the standard basis to the generators is surjective. Since $R$ is Noetherian, $R^{n}$ is Noetherian by induction using Proposition 1.15 applied to $0 \to R \to R^{n} \to R^{n-1} \to 0$. The quotient $M$ of a Noetherian module is Noetherian. $\square$
</details>

### Exercise 1.3

For the presentation matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, compute the Smith normal form and identify the cokernel.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
The entry gcd is two and the determinant is twenty-four.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Swap columns, then clear the new first column and row by integer operations.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Using integer row and column operations, we reduce $A$ to $\operatorname{diag}(2,12)$. The cokernel is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. The invariant factors are $2$ and $12$. Explicitly, swap columns to obtain rows (2,4),(6,0), subtract three times the first row from the second, then subtract twice the first column from the second. This gives diagonal entries two and minus twelve; changing the second row sign gives the stated Smith form.
</details>

### Exercise 1.4

Show that $0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$, with first map $\overline{1} \mapsto \overline{2}$ and second map reduction modulo $2$, is exact but does not split.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Find the kernel and image explicitly.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
A split middle group would have every element killed by two.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Exactness: the first map is injective, the second is surjective, and the image of the first is $\{\overline{0}, \overline{2}\}$, which equals the kernel of the second. If the sequence split, $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ would be isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. But $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order $4$, while every element of the direct sum has order dividing $2$. Hence no splitting exists. $\square$
</details>

### Exercise 1.5

Let $R = k[x]$ where $k$ is a field. Show that the ideal $(x^{2}, x^{3})$ is finitely generated and find a minimal generating set.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
One listed generator is already a multiple of the other.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The ideal is nonzero and cannot have an empty generating set.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
We claim $(x^{2}, x^{3}) = (x^{2})$. Indeed $x^{3} = x \cdot x^{2} \in (x^{2})$, so $(x^{2}, x^{3}) \subseteq (x^{2})$. The reverse inclusion is immediate. The generator $x^{2}$ is minimal because $(x^{2})/(x^{3}) \cong k$ as a $k$-vector space, so the ideal cannot be generated by an element of higher degree. $\square$
</details>


### HA-01-06 — A cyclic left module

For a left module M and m∈M, identify Rm as a quotient of the left module R. Must the kernel be a two-sided ideal?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Use the map r↦rm and its annihilator kernel.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Consider matrices acting on a column vector.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The map R→Rm is linear and onto, with kernel the left ideal of r killing m, so the first isomorphism theorem gives the quotient module. It need not be two-sided. For two-by-two matrices acting on the first standard column, the kernel consists of matrices with first column zero. It is a left ideal, but multiplying the matrix unit E₁₂ on the right by E₂₁ gives E₁₁, which no longer kills that column. Thus the module quotient is valid without an accompanying quotient-ring structure.
</details>

### HA-01-07 — Sum versus product

Explain why the standard countable coordinate vectors generate the direct sum of integer copies but not their direct product.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
A linear combination uses finitely many generators.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The constant sequence one has infinitely many nonzero coordinates.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Every finite-support sequence is the corresponding finite combination of standard coordinate vectors, giving the direct sum. Every combination of those vectors still has finite support, so it cannot give the constant sequence one in the product. Thus that list is not a generating set for the product. The free module on a countable set is the direct sum with its specified basis, and replacing it by the product would destroy the stated universal extension formula from arbitrary generator values.
</details>

### HA-01-08 — A relation among two generators

Over k[x,y], find the kernel and cokernel of R²→R sending (a,b) to xa+yb.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
The cokernel is reduction modulo (x,y).
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
From xa=−yb, primality of x forces x to divide b.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The image is (x,y), so the cokernel is k. For a kernel pair, x divides yb, and x does not divide y in the polynomial UFD, so b=xc. Substitution gives x(a+yc)=0, and the domain property yields a=−yc. Thus the kernel is generated by (−y,x). Conversely that vector maps to zero. It is free on this generator because a nonzero scalar cannot kill its nonzero coordinates in a domain. This computes an exact sequence rather than merely counting the original generators.
</details>

### HA-01-09 — A free quotient splits

Prove a short exact sequence with free quotient C has a section, specifying any choice used.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Choose a preimage in B for each chosen basis vector of C.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Extend these choices linearly and compare with the quotient map on the basis.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Choose one lift for each basis vector, using set-indexed choice if the basis is infinite. The free-module property extends these lifts to a linear map s:C→B. Its composite with the quotient fixes the basis and hence is id_C. Thus it is a section. The resulting map A⊕C→B sends (a,c) to i(a)+s(c). Every b has a unique such expression because b−s(p(b)) lies in the included A. This supplies an actual direct-sum isomorphism depending on the chosen section.
</details>

### HA-01-10 — A finite module without a basis

Show the ideal (x,y) of k[x,y] is finitely generated and torsion-free but is not a free module.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
After passing to the fraction field it has dimension one.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
A free module of that rank would make the ideal principal.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The two elements x,y generate it, and as a submodule of a domain it is torsion-free. Its fraction-field span is one-dimensional, so if it were free its basis would have one member and the ideal would be principal. A generator would divide both x and y, whose greatest common divisor is a unit, forcing the ideal to be the whole ring. But evaluating at the origin shows one is not in it. Hence it is not free. Finite generation and torsion-freeness do not recover the vector-space basis property over this domain.
</details>

## 1.8 Looking forward

In **Abelian Categories**, we will reframe these constructions in a setting where kernels, cokernels, and exactness are defined purely by universal properties, without reference to elements.
