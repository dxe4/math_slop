
# Reader Guide and Notation {#section-1-reader-guide-and-notation}

<a id="section-1-why-this-book-exists"></a>
## Why this book exists {#section-3-why-this-book-exists}

Every equation began as a story. A baker wonders how many loaves to prepare; a cyclist estimates arrival time; a student splits a bill. This book teaches algebra by keeping the story in view: we translate a situation into symbols, manipulate those symbols with care, and translate back to see whether the answer makes sense in the original context.

The manuscript is self-contained at the foundation level. No prior algebra is assumed. If you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide whole numbers and simple fractions, you have enough arithmetic to begin. The first chapter introduces variables as placeholders and builds from there.

<a id="section-7-how-the-story-first-method-works"></a>
## How the story-first method works {#section-9-how-the-story-first-method-works}

Each chapter opens with a concrete situation: a café order, a gym membership, a draining tank, a map scale. We ask a question about that situation, introduce the algebraic tool needed to answer it, and practise until the tool feels natural. Then we apply the same tool to a new story so that structure, not surface detail, becomes visible.

The method has three moves:

1. **Translate.** Read the story, identify the quantities that vary or are unknown, and write an expression, equation, or inequality that captures their relationship.
2. **Manipulate.** Use the algebraic rules introduced in the chapter to simplify, solve, or rearrange.
3. **Interpret.** State the answer in the language of the original story and check whether it is plausible.

Skipping the interpretation step is the most common source of error. A solution of $x = -3$ may be mathematically correct but nonsense if $x$ represents the number of sandwiches ordered.

<a id="section-19-three-reading-routes"></a>
## Three reading routes {#section-21-three-reading-routes}

<a id="section-21-the-continuous-route"></a>
### The continuous route {#section-23-the-continuous-route}

Read the chapters in reading order for a sustained build: variables and expressions, patterns and sequences, graphing stories, equations with two unknowns, inequalities and regions, formulas from science, equivalent expressions, simplification strategies, checking and estimation, and finally algebra applied across subjects. This route suits a learner working through the book over a term or semester.

<a id="section-25-the-equations-first-route"></a>
### The equations-first route {#section-27-the-equations-first-route}

If you need to solve linear equations and graph lines quickly, read **A letter can hold a place**, **Graphing Stories**, **Equations with Two Unknowns**, and **Inequalities and Regions**. Return to **Patterns and Sequences** for the function idea, and to **Checking and Estimation** for verification habits. This route suits a learner preparing for a course that assumes basic equation-solving.

<a id="section-29-the-applications-first-route"></a>
### The applications-first route {#section-31-the-applications-first-route}

If you prefer to see why algebra matters before studying its internal structure, read **A letter can hold a place**, **Formulas from Science**, and **Algebra in Other Subjects**. Return to the earlier chapters for any technique that feels unfamiliar. This route suits a learner returning to mathematics after a break.

<a id="section-33-how-to-work-a-chapter"></a>
## How to work a chapter {#section-35-how-to-work-a-chapter}

First read the opening story and try to write an expression or equation before the chapter does. On a second pass, cover each worked example and predict the next step before reading it. Each chapter prints an inline solution after every practice item so that the source remains self-contained; cover that solution until you have made a serious attempt. Work at least four exercises this way: one retrieval or notation check, one routine calculation, one explanation or reasoning exercise, and one transfer problem whose story differs from the worked examples. The back matter then collects the same decisive steps for review.

Compare your solution by structure rather than by wording. If your reasoning differs, ask whether it establishes the same conditions and uses no hidden assumption.

<a id="section-39-exercise-markers"></a>
## Exercise markers {#section-41-exercise-markers}

Exercises are numbered within each chapter. Markers indicate the expected reasoning demand:

- **R** — retrieval or notation check;
- **F** — routine fluency or calculation;
- **P** — proof, justification, or structural explanation;
- **C** — counterexample, repair, or misconception diagnosis;
- **T** — transfer to a new surface form or context;
- **I** — investigation or project requiring extended reasoning across sections.

A single exercise may carry multiple markers. Solutions show the decisive reasoning, not only the final answer. When an exercise tests model choice, interpretation, or error diagnosis, the solution explains why that choice is warranted.

<a id="section-52-how-to-report-a-suspected-error"></a>
## How to report a suspected error {#section-54-how-to-report-a-suspected-error}

Report a suspected error with the chapter identifier, the smallest questionable claim, and, when possible, a counterexample or proposed repair. For an exercise, include the exact step at which the supplied answer and your calculation diverge. This makes mathematical and notation issues reproducible.

<a id="section-56-scope-and-deliberate-boundaries"></a>
## Scope and deliberate boundaries {#section-58-scope-and-deliberate-boundaries}

This book reaches a first treatment of linear expressions, equations, inequalities, graphs, sequences, formula rearrangement, equivalence, simplification, and estimation. It does not attempt quadratic equations, exponential functions beyond geometric sequences, formal proof by induction, or abstract algebraic structures. Those are separate volumes because each needs its own prerequisites, examples, and pace.

The next component is a prerequisite diagnostic. The notation and convention guide after it is designed for repeated reference rather than one-time reading.

<a id="section-62-following-the-extended-teaching"></a>
## Following the extended teaching {#section-64-following-the-extended-teaching}

After its first-pass reading and six numbered questions, each chapter continues with further development and identified practice extensions. The second pass deepens the same quantities and rules through fuller derivations, changed representations and boundary cases.

The longer worked explanations connect quantities, expressions, equations, tables and graphs. When a calculation uses a rule, name the inputs allowed by the story and the condition that permits the step. In particular, the one-variable equation work in **A letter can hold a place** prepares the substitutions in **Equations with Two Unknowns**. **Equivalent Expressions** makes the common-domain and reversible-step reasoning explicit; return to it when a cancellation or rearrangement is unclear. **Simplification Strategies** then compares routes through the same expression rather than changing the equation to be solved.

Each chapter also offers three identified practice extensions, such as AS-01-E07. Their complete keys appear later in that chapter under **Solutions to practice extensions**. Write an attempt before opening the matching key, then keep a separate comparison and correction. These extensions are optional alongside an existing course's named assignments; use the printed task ID when recording which work you chose and what help you opened. A correct public comparison does not establish unseen work or a grade.

Chapter 10 supplies the synthetic AS-10-P01 modeling dossier, with a primary case, a changed case, complete public references and an explicit self-review rubric. Its supplied observations let you practise the mathematics without arranging an experiment. The original **design your own formula** project still asks you to collect data and check a prediction against reality. Supplied synthetic values and expected results cannot establish that you made those observations or performed that check.

The chapter minute labels are earlier planning figures. The full workload of this expanded teaching and the work you select is unknown. Pause at a failed prerequisite, use its worked repair, and return with the original attempt retained.
