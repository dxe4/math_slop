
# Product Measures and Fubini–Tonelli

An iterated integral inspects one coordinate at a time. A product integral measures the joint domain before choosing an order. Their equality is a theorem with hypotheses, not a consequence of writing two integral signs next to each other. For nonnegative functions there is no cancellation to conceal missing mass, and Tonelli's theorem permits infinite values. For signed functions, absolute integrability is the condition that makes both orders reliable.

Throughout the main construction, \((X,\Sigma,\mu)\) and \((Y,\mathcal T,\nu)\) are sigma-finite measure spaces. Neither needs finite total measure or completeness. We build their product on the product sigma algebra, prove both integration theorems, and then discuss the effect of completion. The [extension and uniqueness chapter](abstract-measures-caratheodory.md) provides the pi–lambda argument. The monotone and dominated convergence theorems are those of [chapter 6](measure-convergence-theorems.md). A final counterexample explains why the sigma-finite hypothesis is doing mathematical work.

## Rectangles, sections and measurable information

The product sigma algebra \(\Sigma\otimes\mathcal T\) is generated by rectangles \(A\times B\), with \(A\in\Sigma\) and \(B\in\mathcal T\). It is a sigma algebra on the Cartesian product, not the collection of all subsets whose individual sections happen to be measurable. The latter condition alone does not supply the countable structure needed for joint measurability.

For \(E\subset X\times Y\), its sections are
\[
 E_x=\{y:(x,y)\in E\},\qquad E^y=\{x:(x,y)\in E\}.
\]
For a function \(h\), write \(h_x(y)=h(x,y)\). Every product-measurable set has measurable sections at every input. Indeed, taking a section commutes with complements and countable unions, and rectangle sections are either the relevant factor set or the empty set. The family with measurable sections is therefore a sigma algebra containing all rectangles.

The same reasoning applies to functions: for each fixed input, the section inclusion map is measurable, because its preimage of a rectangle is a measurable factor set or the empty set. Composing with a jointly measurable function gives a measurable section. This establishes section measurability; it does not yet establish measurability of the function obtained by integrating a section. That is the next step.

**Lemma 10.1 (Measurable section masses).** If \(E\in\Sigma\otimes\mathcal T\) and \(\nu\) is sigma finite, then \(x\mapsto\nu(E_x)\) is measurable.

*Proof.* First suppose \(\nu(Y)<\infty\). The sets \(E\) for which the claim holds form a lambda system. The whole product has the constant section mass \(\nu(Y)\). Complementation gives the measurable function \(\nu(Y)-\nu(E_x)\), with no infinite subtraction. For a countable disjoint union, the section masses are the pointwise sum of the section masses, hence a measurable increasing limit of partial sums. Rectangles belong to this lambda system, since their section mass is \(\nu(B)1_A(x)\). Rectangles form a pi system containing the whole product, so the pi–lambda theorem proves the assertion.

For sigma-finite \(\nu\), choose a measurable disjoint partition \(Y=\bigcup_jY_j\) with finite masses. Apply the finite case to the finite measures \(\nu_j(B)=\nu(B\cap Y_j)\). The section mass for \(\nu\) is the nonnegative sum of the resulting measurable functions, and is measurable. ∎

In this proof, sigma finiteness enters before any interchange of integrals. It allows complements to be handled on finite pieces. The formula for an indicator section is elementary, but passing from rectangles to all generated sets requires this closure argument.

## Constructing the product measure from sections

**Theorem 10.2 (Product measure).** There is a unique measure on \(\Sigma\otimes\mathcal T\) satisfying
\[
 (\mu\times\nu)(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B).
\]
The convention is \(0\cdot\infty=0\). This product measure is sigma finite, and for every product-measurable set,
\[
 (\mu\times\nu)(E)=\int_X\nu(E_x)\,d\mu(x)
 =\int_Y\mu(E^y)\,d\nu(y).
\]

*Proof.* The preceding lemma makes the first integral a defined nonnegative integral for every product-measurable set. Define \(\rho(E)\) by that integral. It vanishes on the empty set. For disjoint \(E_n\), their sections are disjoint, so countable additivity of \(\nu\) and monotone convergence give
\[
 \rho\left(\bigcup_nE_n\right)
 =\int_X\sum_n\nu((E_n)_x)\,d\mu
 =\sum_n\rho(E_n).
\]
Thus \(\rho\) is a measure. On a rectangle, direct integration of its section mass gives the stated product, including the zero-times-infinity convention: an infinite value supported on a null set has integral zero.

Repeat the construction in the opposite coordinate order, defining \(\widetilde\rho(E)=\int_Y\mu(E^y)\,d\nu\). It has the same rectangle values. Choose increasing finite-measure exhaustions \(X_j\uparrow X\) and \(Y_j\uparrow Y\). Each rectangle \(X_j\times Y_j\) has finite mass for both constructed measures, and these rectangles cover the product. On each such rectangle, restrict both measures and apply finite-measure pi–lambda uniqueness to their intersections with generating rectangles. They agree there, and continuity from below gives agreement everywhere. The same argument identifies any other measure with these rectangle values. It also proves sigma finiteness. ∎

This construction is an alternative to first proving that rectangle volumes form a premeasure and then extending it. Here the measurable-section lemma supplies existence directly. Uniqueness uses the same finite-localization principle as the abstract extension theorem. The construction concerns the generated product sigma algebra; it has not silently completed that sigma algebra.

**Worked example 10.3 (A triangular region).** Let \(E=\{(x,y):0<y<x<1\}\), with length measure in both factors. Its vertical section has length \(x\) for \(0<x<1\), so its product measure is \(\int_0^1x\,dx=1/2\). Its horizontal section has length \(1-y\) for \(0<y<1\), giving the same answer. Boundaries have measure zero by the section formula. The choice of strict or non-strict inequalities therefore does not affect this area. That boundary assertion is now proved from the product measure rather than assumed from a picture.

## Tonelli for nonnegative functions

**Theorem 10.4 (Tonelli).** If \(h:X\times Y\to[0,\infty]\) is product measurable, its section integrals are measurable and
\[
 \int_{X\times Y}h\,d(\mu\times\nu)
 =\int_X\left(\int_Yh(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)
 =\int_Y\left(\int_Xh(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y).
\]
Every quantity may be infinite.

*Proof.* For indicators this is theorem 10.2, including measurability of the inner integrals. Finite nonnegative linear combinations give the claim for nonnegative simple functions. Choose nonnegative simple functions \(s_n\uparrow h\) pointwise. For each fixed \(x\), monotone convergence in the second variable gives \(\int s_n(x,y)\,d\nu\uparrow\int h(x,y)\,d\nu\). The limit is measurable. Monotone convergence in the outer variable and on the product gives the identity. Repeat in the opposite order. ∎

Because all the approximations are nonnegative, no finiteness check is needed before exchanging the integrals. An answer of infinity is an allowed conclusion, not a failure of the theorem. Conversely, if either iterated integral of a nonnegative function is finite, Tonelli proves joint integrability and finiteness of the other order. This is often the simplest way to establish absolute integrability before applying Fubini to a signed expression.

For counting measures on \(\mathbb N\), the theorem becomes
\[
 \sum_m\sum_na_{mn}=\sum_n\sum_ma_{mn}
 =\sup_{F\subset\mathbb N^2\text{ finite}}\sum_{(m,n)\in F}a_{mn},
 \qquad a_{mn}\ge0.
\]
The last equality follows from the definition of a nonnegative sum: every finite set of pairs is contained in a finite rectangle, while every finite rectangular sum is one of the finite-subset sums. There is no chosen diagonal ordering hidden in the notation.

**Worked example 10.5 (A layer-cake identity).** If \(f:X\to[0,\infty]\) is measurable, take length measure on \((0,\infty)\) and set \(h(x,t)=1_{\{t<f(x)\}}\). The set is product measurable: it is the union, over positive rational \(q\), of \(\{f>q\}\times(0,q)\). Its vertical section has length \(f(x)\), including an infinite length when \(f(x)=\infty\). Tonelli gives
\[
 \int_Xf\,d\mu=\int_0^\infty\mu\{f>t\}\,dt.
\]
For \(p>0\), apply the same theorem to \(p t^{p-1}1_{\{t<f(x)\}}\) to obtain \(\int f^p=p\int_0^\infty t^{p-1}\mu\{f>t\}\,dt\). Thus the distribution of the heights determines every positive moment that exists. No integration-by-parts boundary term is being discarded.

## Fubini and the exceptional sections

**Theorem 10.6 (Fubini).** If a real or complex product-measurable function satisfies \(\int|h|\,d(\mu\times\nu)<\infty\), then its sections are integrable for almost every input in either factor. Their integrals, defined arbitrarily as zero on the exceptional null sets, are integrable measurable functions. Both iterated integrals equal the product integral.

*Proof.* Tonelli applied to \(|h|\) shows that \(H(x)=\int_Y|h(x,y)|\,d\nu\) is measurable and has finite integral. Therefore \(H\) is finite almost everywhere. On this full-measure set both positive and negative section integrals of the real part are finite, and their difference is the section integral. Their measurable nonnegative integral functions give a measurable difference on this set; extend it by zero elsewhere. Its absolute value is at most \(H\), proving outer integrability. Tonelli for the positive and negative parts gives the desired identity by legitimate finite subtraction. Treat the imaginary part the same way and repeat with the variables reversed. ∎

The exceptional set cannot generally be removed from the statement. For example, on the plane the Borel function \(h(x,y)=1_{\{0\}}(x)\) has product integral zero, but its section at \(x=0\) is the constant function one on the whole line and has infinite integral. The section formula for \(|h|\) is still correct: its infinite value is supported on a null set. An integrable product function means almost every section is integrable, rather than every section.

**Worked example 10.7 (A signed integral over the triangle).** For \(h(x,y)=xy1_{\{0<y<x<1\}}\), the absolute value is bounded by one and supported on a set of finite product measure. Thus Fubini applies, though Tonelli also applies because the function is nonnegative. Integrating first in \(y\) gives \(\int_0^1x(x^2/2)\,dx=1/8\). In the other order the integral is \(\int_0^1y(1-y^2)/2\,dy=1/8\). The changing limits describe different sections of the same set. Writing the same limits in both orders would describe different regions, regardless of which theorem had been invoked.

For integrable \(f\) on \(X\) and \(g\) on \(Y\), Tonelli applied to \(|f(x)g(y)|\) gives the norm identity
\[
 \|f\otimes g\|_{L^1(\mu\times\nu)}=\|f\|_1\|g\|_1.
\]
Fubini then gives the product of their signed or complex integrals. This is a theorem about integrable factors. If an integral is only conditionally defined through improper limits, the hypotheses must be revisited before using the identity.

## Cancellation can change the answer

On \((0,1)^2\), consider
\[
 h(x,y)=\frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
\]
For each positive \(x\), differentiation of \(y/(x^2+y^2)\) gives the integrand, so \(\int_0^1h(x,y)\,dy=1/(1+x^2)\). Integrating that result gives \(\pi/4\). In the other order the antiderivative is \(-x/(x^2+y^2)\), giving an iterated value \(-\pi/4\). All these one-dimensional integrals exist.

There is no contradiction with Fubini. In the wedge \(0<y<x/2\), \(0<x<1/2\), the integrand is at least \(12/(25x^2)\). Integrating over the wedge gives a lower bound \(\int_0^{1/2}6/(25x)\,dx=\infty\) for the positive part. Symmetry interchanges positive and negative parts, so both parts have infinite integral. The product signed integral is undefined, rather than equal to either iterated answer. Existence of both iterated integrals is therefore insufficient for Fubini.

The same issue occurs in arrays. Put \(a_{m,m}=1\), \(a_{m,m+1}=-1\) and all other entries zero, for positive integer indices. Each row sums to zero, so the row-first sum is zero. The first column sums to one and every later column sums to zero, so the column-first sum is one. The sum of absolute values is infinite. The two orders package infinitely many cancellations differently; absolute summability is precisely what prevents that instability.

## Completing the product: which statements survive?

Even when both factor spaces are complete, the product sigma algebra need not be complete. For an example take Lebesgue measure on the unit interval in both factors. The diagonal \(D=\{(x,x):0\le x\le1\}\) is Borel and has product measure zero, because every vertical section is a singleton. Choose a non-Lebesgue-measurable subset \(A\subset[0,1]\), and put \(N=\{(x,x):x\in A\}\). If \(N\) were product measurable, its preimage under the diagonal map would be Lebesgue measurable. That map is measurable into the product sigma algebra, since the preimage of a rectangle is the intersection of its factors. Its preimage of \(N\) is \(A\), a contradiction. Thus a subset of the product null set need not belong to the uncompleted product sigma algebra.

On the completion, every measurable set has a product-measurable representative differing from it inside a product-measurable null set \(Z\). Tonelli shows \(\nu(Z_x)=0\) for almost every \(x\). If the factor \(Y\) is complete, any subset of such a section is measurable, and the completed set has measurable sections with the same masses for almost every \(x\). The analogous assertion holds in the other direction. Applying the same argument to simple approximations gives the completed-space forms of Tonelli and Fubini, with section measurability asserted almost everywhere. Alternatively, one can state their identities using a product-measurable representative throughout.

The qualification about almost every section is unavoidable. Let \(A\subset[0,1]\) be non-Lebesgue-measurable. The set \(\{0\}\times A\) is measurable in the completed planar measure, since it lies inside the null rectangle \(\{0\}\times[0,1]\). Its section at zero is \(A\), which is not measurable. Other sections are empty. Completion permits that one section; it does not make the original nonmeasurable subset of the line measurable under length measure.

## Approximation by finite sums of separate variables

Finite sums of functions \(u(x)v(y)\) provide useful approximations to general integrable product functions. Here is a proof that does not assume a topological structure on either space.

First work on a finite-measure rectangle \(U=A\times B\). The algebra generated by its relative measurable rectangles consists of finite unions of such rectangles. Let \(\mathcal C\) be the subsets of \(U\) that can be approximated in symmetric-difference measure, to any positive tolerance, by members of that algebra. This class contains the algebra and is closed under relative complements, since taking complements preserves symmetric-difference measure. It is closed under finite unions by the union bound.

For countable unions of sets in \(\mathcal C\), first replace the union by a finite initial union with small remaining measure, using continuity from below and finiteness of the measure of \(U\). Then approximate the finitely many sets with a shared error budget. Thus \(\mathcal C\) is a sigma algebra containing the rectangle algebra, so it contains every product-measurable subset of \(U\). The indicators of those sets are consequently approximable in \(L^1\) by algebra indicators. Refining an algebra member into a finite disjoint grid shows that each approximating indicator is a finite sum of rectangle indicators, hence a finite sum of separate-variable products.

For a general \(h\in L^1(\mu\times\nu)\), first restrict to a finite-measure rectangle in an increasing exhaustion. The discarded \(L^1\) norm tends to zero by dominated convergence. Next approximate the restricted function by a finite-valued simple function in \(L^1\), as in the integral construction. Finally approximate each of its finitely many indicator sets by the preceding algebra approximation, choosing the errors after accounting for its coefficient. Combining these stages proves density of finite sums of integrable separate-variable functions in product \(L^1\).

This argument explains why rectangle computations can eventually control all integrable functions. It does not identify an arbitrary integrable function with a finite sum. Each tolerance may require more rectangles and different factors, and the approximation is in norm rather than necessarily uniform or pointwise.

## The sigma-finite boundary

Let \(X=Y=[0,1]\), with Lebesgue measure \(\mu\) in the first factor and counting measure \(\nu\) on the Borel sets in the second. Counting measure on this uncountable set is not sigma finite: its finite-measure sets are finite, and countably many finite sets cannot cover the interval. The diagonal is measurable in the product sigma algebra. Its vertical sections have counting mass one, while its horizontal sections have length zero. Therefore
\[
 \int_X\nu(D_x)\,d\mu=1,
 \qquad \int_Y\mu(D^y)\,d\nu=0.
\]
Even for a nonnegative indicator, the two iterated integrals disagree. A theorem asserting both equalities cannot hold for arbitrary factor measures. This example does not undo the extension theorem, which has its own existence statement without sigma finiteness. It shows why the present product-integration theorem includes that additional hypothesis.

## Exercises with hints and complete solutions

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E001 — Measurable sections

Prove that all sections of a product-measurable function are measurable, including extended-real functions.

**Hint 1.** First prove the assertion for sets using closure under complements and countable unions.

**Hint 2.** Apply the set assertion to inverse images of rays.

**Solution.** Rectangle sections are measurable; the family of sets whose sections are all measurable is a sigma algebra and therefore contains the product sigma algebra. For a product-measurable function \(h\), the section of \(\{h>a\}\) at \(x\) is \(\{y:h_x(y)>a\}\). It is measurable for every real \(a\), which proves extended-real measurability of the section. Real and imaginary parts give the complex version.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E002 — A null rectangle with infinite height

If \(\mu(A)=0\) and \(\nu(B)=\infty\), compute the product measure of \(A\times B\) and justify the computation from section integrals.

**Hint 1.** The section mass is infinite on \(A\) and zero outside it.

**Hint 2.** A nonnegative function supported on a null set has integral zero, even if it takes infinite values there.

**Solution.** Every nonnegative simple function bounded by the section mass is supported on \(A\) and has integral zero. The supremum defining the integral is therefore zero. Thus \((\mu\times\nu)(A\times B)=0\), giving the convention \(0\cdot\infty=0\) through the actual definition. This computation involves no cancellation of infinities.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E003 — An integral over a changing domain

Compute \(\int_0^1\int_0^x(x+y)\,dy\,dx\) in both orders, justifying the change.

**Hint 1.** The domain is \(0<y<x<1\).

**Hint 2.** In the reversed order, \(y<x<1\) for fixed \(y\).

**Solution.** The nonnegative bounded function on a finite-measure region satisfies Tonelli and Fubini. The first order gives \(\int_0^1(3x^2/2)\,dx=1/2\). Reversing gives \(\int_0^1\int_y^1(x+y)\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1(1/2+y-3y^2/2)\,dy=1/2\). The revised limits describe the same triangle.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E004 — Tail distributions and moments

For \(f(x)=x^{-\alpha}\) on \((0,1)\), \(\alpha>0\), use the layer-cake formula to determine when \(f\in L^p\) for finite positive \(p\).

**Hint 1.** For \(t\ge1\), the superlevel set has length \(t^{-1/\alpha}\).

**Hint 2.** Compare the resulting power integral at infinity with exponent minus one.

**Solution.** The superlevel measure is one for \(0<t<1\), and \(t^{-1/\alpha}\) for \(t\ge1\). Hence \(\int f^p=1+p\int_1^\infty t^{p-1-1/\alpha}\,dt\). This is finite exactly when \(p<1/\alpha\), or \(\alpha p<1\), and its value then is \(1/(1-\alpha p)\). At equality the tail is logarithmically divergent, and for larger exponents it diverges as a power.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E005 — Integrability of almost all rows

If \(h\in L^1(\mu\times\nu)\), prove that \(x\mapsto\int|h(x,y)|\,d\nu\) is finite almost everywhere. Give an example where it is infinite at one input.

**Hint 1.** Tonelli computes the integral of that nonnegative function.

**Hint 2.** Use a function supported on a vertical line in the plane.

**Solution.** Tonelli makes the outer integral equal to \(\|h\|_1<\infty\); a nonnegative measurable function with finite integral is finite almost everywhere. On the plane, \(h(x,y)=1_{\{0\}}(x)\) has product norm zero but section integral infinity at zero. Thus the almost everywhere qualification is sharp.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E006 — An absolute-integrability audit

For the cancellation example \((x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\), prove divergence of the absolute integral using the specified wedge and explain why both iterated values can still exist.

**Hint 1.** For \(0<y<x/2\), bound the numerator from below and the denominator from above.

**Hint 2.** Integrate a lower bound proportional to \(x^{-2}\) over a wedge of width proportional to \(x\).

**Solution.** The numerator is at least \(3x^2/4\), the denominator at most \(25x^4/16\), so the function is at least \(12/(25x^2)\). Integration in \(y\) over \((0,x/2)\) leaves \(6/(25x)\), whose integral near zero is infinite. Swapping coordinates gives an infinite negative part as well. Fixed-coordinate integration can cancel contributions before the remaining coordinate approaches zero, producing finite iterated values; the joint signed integral is undefined and Fubini is inapplicable.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E007 — Absolute sums

Suppose \(\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|<\infty\). Prove equality of both iterated sums and the sum in any enumeration of the pairs.

**Hint 1.** Apply Tonelli to the absolute values and Fubini to the real and imaginary parts.

**Hint 2.** A finite set of pairs captures all but an arbitrarily small absolute tail.

**Solution.** Counting measures are sigma finite, so Tonelli gives finite absolute row and column totals and Fubini gives equality of the iterated sums. For any tolerance, choose a finite set of pairs whose omitted absolute sum is below that tolerance. Every enumeration eventually includes this finite set, and its remaining partial-sum error is bounded by the same absolute tail. Thus every enumeration converges to the common sum, proving order independence.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E008 — Product completeness

Explain why the completed planar set \(\{0\}\times A\), with non-Lebesgue-measurable \(A\subset[0,1]\), does not contradict the section theorem for product-measurable sets.

**Hint 1.** Distinguish the product sigma algebra from its completion.

**Hint 2.** Its section at zero is not measurable in the factor.

**Solution.** The set belongs to the completion because it is a subset of the null rectangle \(\{0\}\times[0,1]\). If it belonged to the uncompleted product sigma algebra, the section theorem would make its section at zero, namely \(A\), Lebesgue measurable. Hence it is not product measurable. The completed theorem asserts section measurability only almost everywhere, allowing the exceptional input zero.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E009 — Tensor norms

For \(f\in L^p(\mu)\), \(g\in L^p(\nu)\), with \(1\le p<\infty\), prove \(\|f\otimes g\|_p=\|f\|_p\|g\|_p\).

**Hint 1.** Apply Tonelli to \(|f(x)|^p|g(y)|^p\).

**Hint 2.** Integrate one factor while treating the other value as a nonnegative constant.

**Solution.** Tonelli gives the integral of the product as \((\int|f|^p\,d\mu)(\int|g|^p\,d\nu)\), with both factors finite. Taking the positive \(p\)-th root gives the identity. If either norm is zero, the product integral is zero by the same argument; no division by a zero norm is necessary.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E010 — Rectangle approximation

On a finite-measure rectangle \(U\), prove that approximability in symmetric-difference measure by the rectangle algebra is closed under countable unions.

**Hint 1.** Approximate the countable union first by a finite initial union.

**Hint 2.** The symmetric difference of two finite unions is contained in the union of the individual symmetric differences.

**Solution.** For \(E=\bigcup_nE_n\), continuity from below and finiteness of \(\mu\times\nu(U)\) allow a finite initial union \(F\) with measure of \(E\setminus F\) below half the desired error. Approximate each of the finitely many \(E_n\) used in \(F\) by an algebra set with error below the remaining budget divided by the number of terms. Their union is an algebra member, and the union bound controls its symmetric difference from \(F\). Adding the first truncation error proves the claim.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E011 — A sigma-finiteness failure

Verify the two diagonal iterated integrals for length and counting measure on \([0,1]\), and prove that the counting factor is not sigma finite.

**Hint 1.** A singleton has counting mass one and length zero.

**Hint 2.** A set of finite counting mass must be finite.

**Solution.** Each vertical diagonal section has counting mass one, so its outer length integral is one. Each horizontal section has length zero, so its outer counting integral is zero. A countable cover by finite-counting-mass sets would be a countable union of finite sets, hence countable, and cannot cover the uncountable interval. Thus the sigma-finite hypothesis fails exactly where the theorem requires it.

### MEASURE-PRODUCT-E012 — Integrating a nonnegative series

For nonnegative product-measurable \(h_n\), prove that the integral of \(\sum_nh_n\) equals either order of integration with the sum interchanged with both integrals.

**Hint 1.** Use finite partial sums and monotone convergence.

**Hint 2.** Apply Tonelli to each partial sum, or add counting measure as a third sigma-finite factor.

**Solution.** The partial sums increase to the series pointwise. Monotone convergence on the product makes its integral the sum of the individual product integrals. Tonelli identifies each individual integral with either iterated integral. Monotone convergence in each variable then moves the nonnegative sum through the inner and outer integrals. All identities hold with infinite values permitted; no uniform bound or absolute-value estimate is needed because every term is nonnegative.

## Summary

Product measurability guarantees measurable sections, and sigma finiteness lets their masses be assembled into measurable functions and a unique product measure. Tonelli permits nonnegative integrands with infinite answers. Fubini uses absolute integrability to control cancellation and gives integrable sections almost everywhere. Completion preserves the integral formulas through representatives but can introduce individual nonmeasurable sections. Rectangle approximations explain density of separate-variable sums in product \(L^1\). The signed cancellation example and the non-sigma-finite diagonal demonstrate different failures: the first loses absolute integrability, while the second falls outside the hypotheses needed even for the nonnegative theorem.
