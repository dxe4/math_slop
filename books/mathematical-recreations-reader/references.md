
# References and Further Reading {#section-1-references-and-further-reading}

This bibliography identifies useful comparison texts, classical sources, and professional guidance. The book is written under CC BY-SA 4.0; standard definitions and recreational conventions belong to shared practice. References are included for further reading, historical context, alternative treatments, and broader coverage.

A named result or study is a retrieval convention, not a complete history. Recreational ideas have circulated across many cultures and centuries; a modern statement may differ sharply from an early source.

<a id="section-5-invariants-parity-and-combinatorial-games"></a>
## Invariants, parity, and combinatorial games {#section-7-invariants-parity-and-combinatorial-games}

- Berlekamp, E. R., Conway, J. H., & Guy, R. K. *Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays* (2nd ed., 4 vols.). A K Peters: [vol. 1 (2001)](https://www.routledge.com/Winning-Ways-for-Your-Mathematical-Plays-Volume-1/author/p/book/9781568811307), [vol. 2 (2003)](https://www.routledge.com/Winning-Ways-for-Your-Mathematical-Plays-Volume-2/Berlekamp-Conway-Guy/p/book/9780429487323), [vol. 3 (2003)](https://www.routledge.com/Winning-Ways-for-Your-Mathematical-Plays-Volume-3/Berlekamp-Conway-Guy/p/book/9781568811437), and [vol. 4 (2004)](https://www.routledge.com/Winning-Ways-for-Your-Mathematical-Plays-Volume-4/Berlekamp-Conway-Guy/p/book/9781568811444). Comprehensive treatment of impartial and partizan games, including Nim, Sprague–Grundy theory, and misère analysis.
- Conway, J. H. (1976). *On Numbers and Games*. Academic Press. The foundational text on surreal numbers and combinatorial game theory; denser than *Winning Ways* but essential for deeper study.
- Bouton, C. L. (1901). "Nim, a game with a complete mathematical theory." *Annals of Mathematics*, 3(1/4), 35–39. The original proof of the nim-sum theorem.

<a id="section-11-classical-recreational-mathematics"></a>
## Classical recreational mathematics {#section-13-classical-recreational-mathematics}

- Ball, W. W. R., & Coxeter, H. S. M. (1987). *Mathematical Recreations and Essays* (13th ed.). Dover. A classic survey of recreational problems from antiquity to the twentieth century.
- Loyd, S. (1914). [*Sam Loyd's Cyclopedia of 5000 Puzzles, Tricks, and Conundrums (with Answers)*](https://archive.org/details/samloydscycloped00loyd). Lamb Publishing. The primary title page attributes the volume to Sam Loyd; this bibliography does not duplicate the author as an unsupported editor credit.

<a id="section-16-graph-theory-and-networks"></a>
## Graph theory and networks {#section-18-graph-theory-and-networks}

- Euler, L. (presented/paper dated 1736; journal volume published 1741). [“Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis.”](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/euler-works/53/) *Commentarii Academiae Scientiarum Imperialis Petropolitanae*, 8, 128–140. Euler's bridge argument is an early foundational work in graph theory; this description is a historical characterization, not a sole-priority claim.
- Harary, F. (1969). *Graph Theory*. Addison-Wesley. A standard reference for graph-theoretic definitions and theorems used in the puzzles chapters.
- West, D. B. (2001). *Introduction to Graph Theory* (2nd ed.). Prentice Hall. Modern textbook treatment of Euler trails, planarity, and coloring.
- Diestel, R. (2017). [*Graph Theory*](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-53622-3) (5th ed., Graduate Texts in Mathematics 173). Springer. A deeper route from the chapters' elementary network questions into modern graph theory.

<a id="section-23-tiling-dissections-and-geometric-constructions"></a>
## Tiling, dissections, and geometric constructions {#section-25-tiling-dissections-and-geometric-constructions}

- Grünbaum, B., & Shephard, G. C. (1987). *Tilings and Patterns*. W. H. Freeman. The definitive reference on periodic and aperiodic tilings, including Penrose tilings and substitution systems.
- Berger, R. (1966). [*The Undecidability of the Domino Problem*](https://doi.org/10.1090/memo/0066). *Memoirs of the American Mathematical Society*, no. 66, 72 pp. Primary source for the existence of aperiodic tile sets in the domino-problem setting.
- Frederickson, G. N. (1997). *Dissections: Plane and Fancy*. Cambridge University Press. Systematic treatment of geometric dissections with explicit piece inventories.
- Huzita, H., & Scimemi, B. (1989). "The algebra of paper-folding (origami)." *Proceedings of the First International Meeting of Origami Science and Technology*, 215–222. Origami axioms and their algebraic consequences.
- Alperin, R. C. (2000). "A mathematical theory of origami constructions and numbers." *New York Journal of Mathematics*, 6, 119–133. A precise algebraic treatment of origami operations, classical constructibility, and the cubic-enabling simultaneous fold.
- Lang, R. J. [“Huzita–Justin Axioms.”](https://langorigami.com/article/huzita-justin-axioms/) Accessed 2026-09-06. Secondary historical note on naming and early enumerations of the single-fold axioms; used to avoid assigning the seven-operation list to an unsupported single priority claim.
- Haga, K. (2008). *Origamics: Mathematical Explorations Through Paper Folding*. Edited and translated by J. C. Fonacier and M. Isoda. World Scientific. ISBN 978-981-283-489-8. Source for Haga's named fold theorems and their mathematical exploration.
- Hull, T. C. (2020). [“Flat Vertex Folds: Local Properties.”](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108778633.008) In *Origametry: Mathematical Methods in Paper Folding*, Chapter 5, 75–106. Cambridge University Press. Proofs and historical notes for the Maekawa–Justin and Kawasaki–Justin theorems.

<a id="section-34-probability-paradoxes-and-coding"></a>
## Probability, paradoxes, and coding {#section-36-probability-paradoxes-and-coding}

- Feller, W. (1968). *An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications* (3rd ed., Vol. 1). Wiley. Standard reference for the birthday problem, conditional probability, and combinatorial probability.
- Diaconis, P., & Graham, R. (2012). *Magical Mathematics: The Mathematical Ideas That Animate Great Magic Tricks*. Princeton University Press. Connections between card shuffling, permutations, and mathematical magic.
- Bayer, D., & Diaconis, P. (1992). "Trailing the dovetail shuffle to its lair." *Annals of Applied Probability*, 2(2), 294–313. The original analysis of riffle-shuffle randomisation.
- Hamming, R. W. (1950). "Error detecting and error correcting codes." *Bell System Technical Journal*, 29(2), 147–160. Foundational paper on Hamming distance and error-correcting codes.
- MacWilliams, F. J., & Sloane, N. J. A. (1977). *The Theory of Error-Correcting Codes*. North-Holland. The 1977 date identifies the original title-page edition; later successor listings may describe a reprint date.

<a id="section-42-knot-theory-and-topology"></a>
## Knot theory and topology {#section-44-knot-theory-and-topology}

- Adams, C. C. (2004). *The Knot Book: An Elementary Introduction to the Mathematical Theory of Knots*. American Mathematical Society. Accessible introduction to Reidemeister moves, knot invariants, and the Jones polynomial.
- Reidemeister, K. (1927). "Elementare Begründung der Knotentheorie." *Abhandlungen aus dem Mathematischen Seminar der Universität Hamburg*, 5(1), 24–32. The original proof that Reidemeister moves characterise knot equivalence.
- Jones, V. F. R. (1985). "A polynomial invariant for knots via von Neumann algebras." *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society*, 12(1), 103–111. Discovery of the Jones polynomial.

<a id="section-48-magic-squares-and-latin-squares"></a>
## Magic squares and Latin squares {#section-50-magic-squares-and-latin-squares}

- Andrews, W. S. (1917). *Magic Squares and Cubes*. Open Court Publishing. Historical compendium of magic-square constructions.
- Ollerenshaw, K., & Bondi, H. (1982). "Magic squares of order four." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London A*, 306(1495), 443–532. Independent analytical enumeration of the 880 order-four classes up to square symmetries.
- Tarry, G. (1901). [*Le problème des 36 officiers*](https://mediatheques.agglo-larochelle.fr/doc/SYRACUSE/1639383/le-probleme-des-36-officiers-m-g-tarry). Secrétariat de l'Association, Paris, 36 pp.; extract from the Association française pour l'avancement des sciences Paris Congress 1900 proceedings. Primary source for the order-six nonexistence result.
- Dénes, J., & Keedwell, A. D. (1974). *Latin Squares and Their Applications*. Academic Press. Standard reference for Latin squares and orthogonality.
- Felgenhauer, B., & Jarvis, F. (2005). [“Enumerating possible Sudoku grids.”](https://www.afjarvis.org.uk/sudoku/sudoku.pdf) 20 June 2005; supporting [enumeration and verification page](https://www.afjarvis.org.uk/sudoku/bertram.html). Source for the exact completed-Sudoku and order-nine Latin-square counts and their computational verification.
- Bose, R. C., Shrikhande, S. S., & Parker, E. T. (1960). "Further results on the construction of mutually orthogonal Latin squares and the falsity of Euler's conjecture." *Canadian Journal of Mathematics*, 12, 189–203. Disproves Euler's broader conjecture about orders $4k+2>6$, not the order-six thirty-six officers nonexistence result. [Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-1960-016-5).

<a id="section-57-fractals-and-iterative-geometry"></a>
## Fractals and iterative geometry {#section-59-fractals-and-iterative-geometry}

- Falconer, K. (2003). *Fractal Geometry: Mathematical Foundations and
  Applications* (2nd ed.). Wiley. The separated self-similar-set dimension
  theorem justifies the Hausdorff dimension values stated in the fractals
  chapter; its elementary box-counting argument is supplied in the chapter.
- Mandelbrot, B. B. (1982). *The Fractal Geometry of Nature*. W. H. Freeman. The foundational exposition of fractal dimension and self-similarity.
- Peitgen, H.-O., Jürgens, H., & Saupe, D. (1992). *Chaos and Fractals: New Frontiers of Science*. Springer. Comprehensive treatment of the Mandelbrot set, Julia sets, and iterated function systems.
- Penrose, R. (1979). [“Pentaplexity: A Class of Non-Periodic Tilings of the Plane.”](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03024384) *The Mathematical Intelligencer*, 2(1), 32–37. Primary exposition of Penrose's nonperiodic tilings; this entry does not assert a precise discovery date.

<a id="section-72-historical-attribution-checklist"></a>
## Historical attribution checklist {#section-74-historical-attribution-checklist}

Every historical or priority claim should record:

1. a classification as contemporary exposition, secondary history, translated primary source, or primary source;
2. verified author, title, edition, publisher or archive, and stable locator;
3. a note explaining whether the citation supports provenance, formulation, evidence, example, or broader context;
4. a check that modern terminology is not projected unchanged backward;
5. careful comparison of primary sources for culturally broad claims and disputed priority.

<a id="section-82-reading-route-recommendations"></a>
## Reading-route recommendations {#section-84-reading-route-recommendations}

For a deeper treatment of combinatorial games, proceed to *Winning Ways* or *On Numbers and Games*. For graph theory, continue into West or Diestel. For probability and coding, study Feller and MacWilliams–Sloane. For fractals and dynamics, explore Peitgen–Jürgens–Saupe. For geometric constructions, consult Frederickson on dissections and the Huzita–Scimemi, Alperin, and Lang sources on origami.

<a id="section-86-citation-policy-for-contributors"></a>
## Citation policy for contributors {#section-88-citation-policy-for-contributors}

Paraphrase sources and cite the job they perform. Do not copy exercise sequences, distinctive examples, diagrams, or prose merely because a concept is standard. Direct quotations require exact locators and license or fair-use review. When sources disagree, record the disagreement. When provenance is unknown, label it unknown rather than inventing a clean lineage.
