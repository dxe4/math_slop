
# References, Reading Routes, and Attribution Guide {#section-1-references-reading-routes-and-attribution-guide}

<a id="section-1-status-and-purpose"></a>
## Status and purpose {#section-3-status-and-purpose}

This is a responsible reading map for the first edition. A bounded metadata audit resolved the named works to the edition-specific candidate records below, except that the manuscript does not preserve which version-sensitive copies were actually consulted. Page locators, translation history, and historical-priority claims therefore remain limited to the explicit statements in this guide. A name below does not imply sole discovery, and no author or publisher named here has reviewed or endorsed this book.

The chapters are designed to be self-contained. References therefore serve four narrower purposes: to offer another treatment, to continue beyond the chapter, to help verify a conventional theorem name, and to locate the historical record. Standard definitions and mathematical statements are part of a shared literature. The manuscript's particular exposition, examples, dependency routes, exercises, and solution narratives are newly composed unless an item is later marked as adapted.

<a id="section-7-what-a-finished-citation-should-establish"></a>
## What a finished citation should establish {#section-9-what-a-finished-citation-should-establish}

A citation should identify the exact item actually consulted. For a book, that means author or editor, title, edition if relevant, publisher, year, and a stable catalog identifier when useful. For an article, it means author, title, journal, volume, year, page range, and a verified DOI or other stable identifier. For a web resource, it means an accountable author or organization, title, version or date, stable URL, and access date. Page or theorem locators should accompany a close paraphrase, an adapted proof, an exercise derived from a distinctive source, or a historical claim.

Four kinds of note must not be collapsed into one:

- A **mathematical source** helps verify a definition, theorem, or proof technique.
- A **historical source** supplies evidence about chronology, priority, terminology, or transmission. A modern textbook is rarely sufficient evidence for a contested priority claim.
- A **reading recommendation** is chosen for pedagogical value; it need not be the source of this manuscript's wording.
- An **attribution note** discloses a quotation, close paraphrase, adapted example, dataset, diagram, exercise, or software contribution.

Primary sources are important evidence, but age does not make them automatically decisive. A historical claim should compare the relevant primary publication with reputable historical scholarship and should distinguish first publication, independent discovery, earlier special cases, and later generalization. Secondary sources are usually better starting points for learners and can reveal which primary documents need inspection. This guide intentionally makes no new priority claims.

<a id="section-20-foundation-proof-and-counting-routes"></a>
## Foundation, proof, and counting routes {#section-22-foundation-proof-and-counting-routes}

<a id="section-22-chapter-1-sets-functions-relations-orders"></a>
### Chapter 1: `sets-functions-relations-orders` {#section-24-chapter-1-sets-functions-relations-orders}

Kenneth Rosen's *Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications* and Ralph Grimaldi's *Discrete and Combinatorial Mathematics* provide broad comparison routes for sets, functions, relations, and elementary orders. B. A. Davey and H. A. Priestley's *Introduction to Lattices and Order* continues from partial orders into lattice structure. A reader wanting a proof-centered bridge can pair these with Daniel Velleman's *How to Prove It*.

Terms such as equivalence relation, quotient set, partial order, closure, and lattice are generic mathematical vocabulary, not ownership claims. If a later edition adds a historical statement about the development of set theory or order theory, that statement needs specialist historical evidence rather than an unsupported textbook anecdote.

<a id="section-28-chapter-2-proof-induction-invariants"></a>
### Chapter 2: `proof-induction-invariants` {#section-30-chapter-2-proof-induction-invariants}

Velleman's *How to Prove It* and Richard Hammack's *Book of Proof* are useful alternate routes through quantifiers, direct proof, contradiction, induction, and recursive definitions. Ronald Graham, Donald Knuth, and Oren Patashnik's *Concrete Mathematics* gives a more advanced route through sums, recurrences, and discrete reasoning.

Induction, minimal-counterexample reasoning, invariants, and extremal choice describe proof patterns. They should not be presented as inventions attributable to a single modern author. A distinctive borrowed proof organization or exercise still requires a local citation even when the underlying theorem is standard.

<a id="section-34-chapter-3-counting-bijections-inclusion-exclusion"></a>
### Chapter 3: `counting-bijections-inclusion-exclusion` {#section-36-chapter-3-counting-bijections-inclusion-exclusion}

Miklós Bóna's *A Walk Through Combinatorics*, Richard Stanley's *Enumerative Combinatorics, Volume 1*, and Graham, Knuth, and Patashnik's *Concrete Mathematics* offer increasingly deep treatments of bijections, binomial identities, pigeonhole arguments, and inclusion–exclusion. Herbert Wilf's *generatingfunctionology* is a useful transition from elementary counting to generating functions.

“Pigeonhole principle” and “inclusion–exclusion” are conventional retrieval labels. If the final manuscript discusses who first stated a particular form, the claim must cite verified historical research. A bijective proof copied in its distinctive construction or wording is not made attribution-free merely because the identity itself is classical.

<a id="section-40-chapter-4-recurrences-generating-functions"></a>
### Chapter 4: `recurrences-generating-functions` {#section-42-chapter-4-recurrences-generating-functions}

Wilf's *generatingfunctionology*, Stanley's two volumes of *Enumerative Combinatorics*, and Philippe Flajolet and Robert Sedgewick's *Analytic Combinatorics* form a route from formal power series to advanced coefficient analysis. The present chapter primarily uses algebraic and formal methods; analytic asymptotics should be read with the convergence hypotheses in its specialist literature.

The final citation pass must distinguish an ordinary generating function used purely formally from a power series used analytically. Software-produced coefficient tables require a computation record as well as a mathematical reference.

<a id="section-46-graphs-probability-and-games"></a>
## Graphs, probability, and games {#section-48-graphs-probability-and-games}

<a id="section-48-chapter-5-graphs-trees-connectivity"></a>
### Chapter 5: `graphs-trees-connectivity` {#section-50-chapter-5-graphs-trees-connectivity}

Douglas West's *Introduction to Graph Theory*, Reinhard Diestel's *Graph Theory*, and J. A. Bondy and U. S. R. Murty's *Graph Theory* provide broad treatments of paths, trees, connectivity, cuts, and degree arguments. These are reading routes, not evidence that any displayed proof was adapted from a specific edition.

The familiar degree-sum identity is often called the handshaking lemma. That label is conventional terminology, not a historical priority statement. Any historical narrative about the origins of graph theory needs direct documentation and careful scope.

<a id="section-54-chapter-6-planarity-coloring-matchings"></a>
### Chapter 6: `planarity-coloring-matchings` {#section-56-chapter-6-planarity-coloring-matchings}

West, Diestel, and Bondy–Murty cover planar graphs, coloring, and matchings. László Lovász and Michael Plummer's *Matching Theory* is a specialist continuation. The chapter's use of “Euler's formula” and “Hall's condition” follows established retrieval practice. The names alone do not assert sole discovery, first formulation, or the exact historical form of the result.

Before release, every named result should be checked for the hypotheses used here: connectedness in the displayed planar identity, finiteness and bipartite structure in the matching criterion, and simplicity where a coloring bound assumes it. Historical attribution and mathematical scope are separate checks.

<a id="section-60-chapter-7-probabilistic-extremal-methods"></a>
### Chapter 7: `probabilistic-extremal-methods` {#section-62-chapter-7-probabilistic-extremal-methods}

Noga Alon and Joel Spencer's *The Probabilistic Method* is the main continuation for expectation, alteration, concentration, and dependency methods. Béla Bollobás's *Extremal Graph Theory* and Stasys Jukna's *Extremal Combinatorics* provide routes beyond the chapter's elementary extremal arguments. Diestel and West give graph-theoretic context.

“Ramsey-style” and “Turán-style” in this manuscript classify forms of argument; they are not historical claims. Use exact theorem citations alongside named results; descriptive labels identify forms of argument. Claims about discovery, influence, or precedence require primary publications plus qualified historical interpretation.

<a id="section-66-chapter-8-games-strategy-capstone"></a>
### Chapter 8: `games-strategy-capstone` {#section-68-chapter-8-games-strategy-capstone}

Elwyn Berlekamp, John Conway, and Richard Guy's *Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays* is a major route into combinatorial game theory. Aaron Siegel's *Combinatorial Game Theory* and Michael Albert, Richard Nowakowski, and David Wolfe's *Lessons in Play* continue the subject systematically. The chapter restricts itself to finite games and explicitly stated conventions; readers should not transfer claims without checking whether play is normal or misère, impartial or partizan, finite or potentially infinite.

Game positions, named commercial games, and puzzle text can raise intellectual-property questions independent of theorem attribution. A final audit should verify permissions for any non-original board, diagram, rule text, or substantial position sequence.

<a id="section-72-structural-continuations"></a>
## Structural continuations {#section-74-structural-continuations}

<a id="section-74-chapter-9-quotients-and-canonical-representatives"></a>
### Chapter 9: `quotients-and-canonical-representatives` {#section-76-chapter-9-quotients-and-canonical-representatives}

Velleman and Rosen supply accessible background on equivalence relations. Davey–Priestley provides order-theoretic examples, while standard abstract algebra texts provide further examples of operations descending to quotient structures. The mathematical obligation is always local: prove that a proposed operation is independent of representative choice.

“Canonical” should mean selected by a specified rule or natural universal property, not merely the representative the author happens to prefer. If a canonical-form algorithm is implemented, cite the algorithmic source and document tie-breaking and normalization choices.

<a id="section-80-chapter-10-finite-orders-lattices-mobius"></a>
### Chapter 10: `finite-orders-lattices-mobius` {#section-82-chapter-10-finite-orders-lattices-mobius}

Davey and Priestley's *Introduction to Lattices and Order* is the main lattice route. Stanley's *Enumerative Combinatorics, Volume 1* treats partially ordered sets and Möbius inversion in enumerative context. For a bounded primary route to Möbius-function theory, use Gian-Carlo Rota, “On the Foundations of Combinatorial Theory I. Theory of Möbius Functions” (1964), 340–368, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02199936). This identifies a work; it does not by itself establish a complete historical-priority account.

The spelling “Möbius” and the conventional term “Möbius inversion” aid retrieval but do not settle the lineage of each finite-poset formulation. The release audit must verify exact sources, accents, and bibliographic metadata.

<a id="section-86-chapter-11-advanced-enumeration-partitions"></a>
### Chapter 11: `advanced-enumeration-partitions` {#section-88-chapter-11-advanced-enumeration-partitions}

George Andrews's *The Theory of Partitions* is a specialist route for integer partitions. Stanley's *Enumerative Combinatorics* and Wilf's *generatingfunctionology* connect partitions to coefficient methods. Flajolet–Sedgewick becomes relevant when asymptotic analysis is introduced beyond the present chapter.

Partition diagrams and generating-function manipulations are standard, but a distinctive diagram, table, or exercise construction can still require attribution. Identities should be cited at the most precise form actually used; a nearby but different analytic identity is not an adequate source.

<a id="section-92-chapter-12-graph-decompositions-orientations"></a>
### Chapter 12: `graph-decompositions-orientations` {#section-94-chapter-12-graph-decompositions-orientations}

Diestel, West, and Bondy–Murty are useful general references for orientations, connectivity, trees, and decomposition methods. Specialist results should be sourced individually once the final theorem list freezes. Words such as decomposition, orientation, certificate, and cut have several technical variants, so citations must match the chapter's exact definitions.

A diagram reconstructed from an external source needs attribution even when redrawn. A generated witness should include the input, software version, exact output or digest, and an independently checkable mathematical certificate.

<a id="section-98-chapter-13-designs-and-set-systems"></a>
### Chapter 13: `designs-and-set-systems` {#section-100-chapter-13-designs-and-set-systems}

Douglas Stinson's *Combinatorial Designs: Constructions and Analysis* and Thomas Beth, Dieter Jungnickel, and Hanfried Lenz's *Design Theory* are established routes into block designs and incidence methods. Jukna's *Extremal Combinatorics* and Bóna's *A Walk Through Combinatorics* provide broader set-system context.

Parameter notation varies substantially across the literature. A citation must be checked against this chapter's meanings of points, blocks, replication number, block size, and pair concurrence. Feasibility conditions proved by double counting are necessary conditions unless sufficiency is explicitly established.

<a id="section-104-chapter-14-matroid-independence-bridge"></a>
### Chapter 14: `matroid-independence-bridge` {#section-106-chapter-14-matroid-independence-bridge}

James Oxley's *Matroid Theory* is a standard specialist reference. Dominic Welsh's *Matroid Theory* and Alexander Schrijver's *Combinatorial Optimization* provide further routes through independence, bases, circuits, rank, and greedy optimization. Readers should verify which axiom system a source adopts before comparing proofs.

The term “matroid” and named representations are conventional subject vocabulary. Historical claims about axiom systems, independent discoveries, or the development of greedy characterizations need exact primary and historical sources. An exchange argument is not valid merely because its conclusion resembles a greedy theorem; its hypotheses must be cited and proved.

<a id="section-110-computation-and-synthesis"></a>
## Computation and synthesis {#section-112-computation-and-synthesis}

<a id="section-112-chapter-15-exact-computation-experiments"></a>
### Chapter 15: `exact-computation-experiments` {#section-114-chapter-15-exact-computation-experiments}

Donald Knuth's *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4A: Combinatorial Algorithms, Part 1* is a broad algorithmic route. Frank Ruskey's *Combinatorial Generation* is a working manuscript dated 1 October 2003, not a conventionally published book. For graph calculations, Brendan McKay and Adolfo Piperno's published work on practical graph isomorphism is relevant when canonical labeling or isomorph rejection is actually used; the exact article and software version must then be cited.

“Computed exhaustively” is a reproducibility claim. It should be accompanied by the finite search space, representation, generation rule, rejection rule, arithmetic model, software and version, source or archived artifact, input parameters, output digest, and an independently checkable summary. Random searches also need seeds and generator details. A computation over tested instances is evidence about those instances, not by itself a proof of an unrestricted theorem.

<a id="section-118-chapter-16-cumulative-projects-solutions"></a>
### Chapter 16: `cumulative-projects-solutions` {#section-120-chapter-16-cumulative-projects-solutions}

This chapter synthesizes the preceding routes rather than introducing a single external literature. Each project should cite the chapter IDs on which it depends and any external theorem, dataset, algorithm, or adapted problem. Solutions must distinguish a proof from a certificate checked by code and both from empirical exploration.

If a project is inspired by a recognizable published problem, changing names or numbers does not remove the attribution obligation. The final editorial pass should record provenance alongside the problem during revision, not attempt to reconstruct it after layout.

<a id="section-124-software-data-figures-and-licenses"></a>
## Software, data, figures, and licenses {#section-126-software-data-figures-and-licenses}

A mathematical citation does not substitute for a software citation. When code materially produces a table, counterexample, diagram, or certificate, record the software name, authors or project, exact version or commit, project URL or archive identifier, license, invocation, and machine-relevant settings. Cite the underlying algorithm separately when appropriate. Generated output should be checked for whether its license or terms impose attribution requirements.

External datasets need creator, title, version, repository, stable identifier, license, and any transformations. Figures need provenance at the figure level: original, redrawn from, adapted from, or generated from data. “Redrawn” does not erase copyright. Alt text and numerical alternatives are part of accessibility, while permission and license are separate legal checks.

This manuscript states CC BY-SA 4.0 in its front matter. That declaration covers project-owned text and other material the project has authority to license; it cannot relicense incompatible third-party content. Quotations, externally sourced images, code, fonts, and datasets require their own compatibility review. Bibliographic facts may be recorded, but expressive annotations copied from a catalog should not be assumed free of restrictions.
