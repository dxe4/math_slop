
# Induction, Recursion, and Loop Reasoning

Induction, recursion, and invariants are three views of the same structure: establish a base state, justify a step, and conclude a property for everything generated by repeated steps.

**Learning purpose.** You will learn to select an induction domain, strengthen
a hypothesis when composition demands it, derive a loop invariant from a
postcondition, and prove termination with a well-founded ranking function. The
chapter uses the finite encodings and function language of Chapter 2.

## 1. Ordinary induction

To prove $P(n)$ for every integer $n\ge 0$:

1. prove $P(0)$;
2. assume $P(k)$ for an arbitrary $k\ge 0$;
3. prove $P(k+1)$ from that assumption.

For the geometric sum,

$1+r+\ldots+r^n = (r^{n+1}-1)/(r-1)$ for $r\ne 1$.

The base $n=0$ gives one on both sides. Assuming the formula through $k$, add $r^{k+1}$ and combine over $r-1$, obtaining $(r^{k+2}-1)/(r-1)$. The excluded case $r=1$ has sum $n+1$; division by zero is not a harmless notation issue.

## 2. Strong and well-founded induction

Strong induction assumes all smaller cases. To prove every integer $n\ge 2$ factors into primes, either $n$ is prime or $n=ab$ with $2\le a,b<n$; factor each by the strong hypothesis.

Well-founded induction generalizes “smaller integer” to any relation with no infinite descending chain. Termination proofs use the same principle. A recursive function on a tree terminates when every call receives a proper subtree because subtree size decreases.

## 3. Recursive definitions require decreasing structure

Factorial is defined by $0! = 1$ and $(n+1)! = (n+1)n!$. The recursive program mirrors the definition. A malformed recursion that calls itself on $n+1$ has no descent and the equations do not define a finite computation.

For Euclid’s algorithm, recursion on $(a,b)$ calls $(b,a \bmod b)$. The second component decreases when $b>0$, even though the first may change irregularly. The measure need not be the entire argument.

## 4. Solving recurrences by expansion

For binary search,

$T(n)\le T(\left\lceil n/2 \right\rceil)+c$.

After $k$ expansions the size is at most $\left\lceil n/2^k \right\rceil$. When $k\approx \log_{2}n$, it reaches one, so $T(n)=\mathcal{O}(\log n)$. A lower bound follows because each comparison has at most a fixed number of outcomes and must distinguish $n+1$ possible insertion positions.

For merge sort, $T(n)=2T(n/2)+cn$. At each of $\log_{2}n$ levels, total merge cost is $cn$, so $\Theta(n \log n)$ for powers of two; rounding changes constants, not the order.

## 5. Loop invariants as time-indexed induction

If a loop state after $k$ iterations is $s_{k}$, an invariant is a property $I(s_{k})$ proved by induction on $k$. Initialization proves the base; preservation proves the step.

To sum an array, maintain

$total = \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} a[j]$, with $0\le i\le n$.

Initially $i=0,total=0$, matching the empty sum. One iteration adds $a[i]$ and increments $i$, preserving the equation. At $i=n$, it is the desired full sum.

## 6. Strengthening an induction hypothesis

Sometimes the desired theorem is too weak to preserve. To prove a tree-evaluation algorithm uses a stack safely, “it returns the right value” may not say enough about stack shape. Strengthen the theorem to include the exact stack effect: evaluating an expression from stack $S$ returns its value and restores $S$ unchanged.

Strengthening is not cheating. It reveals the compositional fact needed by the recursive step.

## 7. Mutual induction and state machines

A grammar may define expressions and terms mutually:

- an expression is a term or expression plus term;
- a term is a number or parenthesized expression.

Proving properties of both requires mutual induction: assume the property for every immediately used expression and term constructor, then prove both families together.

Transition systems also invite invariant induction. If $Init(s)$ describes initial states and $Step(s,s')$ transitions, prove safety property $Safe$ by:

- $Init(s)\implies Safe(s)$;
- $Safe(s)\land Step(s,s')\implies Safe(s')$.

Then every reachable state is safe. This proof may fail because $Safe$ is not inductive even though all reachable states are safe; adding auxiliary facts can strengthen it into an inductive invariant.

## 8. Termination and ranking functions

A ranking function maps program states to a well-founded set and strictly decreases on every loop step. For nested loops, a lexicographic pair can work: $(outer_{remaining}, inner_{remaining})$, ordered so any decrease in the first dominates resetting the second.

For recursive graph traversal, “number of unvisited vertices” decreases only if vertices are marked before recursive calls. Marking afterward can recurse around a cycle forever. The proof diagnoses the implementation order.

## 9. Structural recursion and folds

A fold consumes an inductive structure by replacing each constructor with an operation. List length replaces empty with zero and cons with $1+$. Sum replaces empty with zero and cons with addition. Many proofs about folds follow from one structural-induction pattern.

For a binary syntax tree, an evaluator is a fold: literals map to values and operator nodes combine evaluated children. If operations are partial, the fold’s codomain should record failure rather than pretending every syntax tree has a value.

## 10. Deriving a loop from an invariant

Invariants can guide construction, not only verify finished code. To compute
$x^n$ for integer $n\ge 0$, choose state $r,b,e$ and invariant

$r\cdot b^e=x^n$.

Initialization $r=1,b=x,e=n$ satisfies it. We want $e=0$, when the invariant
gives $r=x^n$. If $e$ is odd, transform

$r←r\cdot b$, $e←e-1$;

the product becomes $(r b)b^{e-1}=rb^e$. Once $e$ is even, transform

$b←b^{2}$, $e←e/2$;

because $(b^{2})^{e/2}=b^e$. Positive $e$ decreases, so the algorithm terminates.

This derivation explains exponentiation by squaring. The proof counts
$\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ multiplications, not constant-time bit operations on arbitrarily
large numbers.

## 11. Complete induction proof for Euclid

For integers $a\ge 0,b>0$, division gives $a=qb+r$ with $0\le r<b$. A common divisor
of $a,b$ divides $r=a-qb$; a common divisor of $b,r$ divides $a=qb+r$.
Therefore

$\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)$.

The recursive call has second argument $r<b$. Strong induction on the positive
second argument proves correctness: the base reaches remainder zero, where
$\gcd(a,0)=a$; the step preserves the gcd and invokes the result on a smaller
second argument. The same descent proves termination.

The theorem assumes Euclidean remainder. A language whose remainder can be
negative for negative operands needs normalization or a different domain
statement.

## 12. Strengthening a theorem: tree flattening

Define `flatten(t)` to list leaves left-to-right. We want to prove appending the
result to tail list $z$ behaves predictably. The simple theorem “flatten lists
the leaves” can be awkward inside a recursive implementation using an
accumulator. Strengthen it:

$\mathit{flattenInto}(t,z)=\mathit{flatten}(t)\mathbin{+\!+}z$.

For a leaf $a$, implementation prepends $a$ to $z$, matching the equation. For
node $(l,r)$, suppose the strengthened theorem holds for both subtrees. If the
implementation calls `flattenInto(r,z)` then `flattenInto(l, result)`, the
induction hypotheses give

`flatten(l) ++ (flatten(r) ++ z)`.

Associativity of concatenation yields $flatten(node(l,r))++z$. Setting $z$ to
empty recovers the original theorem. The stronger statement matches the
program’s compositional interface.

## 13. A recurrence proof with upper and lower bounds

Let merge sort on powers of two satisfy

$T(1)=d$, $T(n)=2T(n/2)+cn$.

Expanding $j$ levels gives

$T(n)=2^j T(n/2^j)+jcn$.

At $j=\log_{2}n$, subproblem size is one, so

$T(n)=nd+cn \log_{2}n$.

Thus $T(n)=\Theta(n \log n)$ when $c,d$ are positive constants. The explicit
solution supplies both bounds. Writing only a recursion-tree picture suggests
the result; the expansion and base substitution prove it for the stated
powers-of-two recurrence.

For nonpowers, balanced floors and ceilings change lower-order terms. A proof
can sandwich between neighboring powers of two if cost is suitably monotone,
or work directly with rounded recurrence.

## 14. Lexicographic variants for nested work

Consider a parser that processes a list of tokens and, for each token, scans a
finite rule list. The inner scan resets when the outer token advances. The
single quantity “inner rules remaining” can increase at reset.

Use pair

\[(\text{tokens remaining},\ \text{rules remaining for current token})\]

ordered lexicographically. An inner step decreases the second coordinate while
holding the first. Moving to the next token decreases the first, so resetting
the second is allowed. Lexicographic order on pairs of nonnegative integers is
well-founded.

Not every pair works. If a transition can increase the first coordinate while
decreasing the second, lexicographic descent may fail. Check every branch.

## 15. Inductive invariants versus reachable truth

A property can hold on all reachable states without being preserved from every
state satisfying it. Let a system have reachable states $(phase=0,x=0)$ and
$(phase=1,x=1)$. Property $x\le 1$ is true on both. But if the transition rule on
an unreachable state $(phase=0,x=1)$ moves to $x=2$, then $x\le 1$ is not
inductive.

Strengthen it with phase relation, for example $x=phase$ on the reachable
fragment. An inductive invariant may be stronger than the desired safety
property so that local preservation becomes true.

This explains why a failed invariant proof does not necessarily refute safety.
It may expose a weak invariant rather than a real counterexample.

## 16. Full loop proof: lower-bound binary search

Let $a[0..n)$ be nondecreasing and let $x$ be a target. The lower-bound
algorithm maintains half-open interval $[lo,hi)$:

```text
lo := 0; hi := n
while lo < hi:
    mid := floor((lo + hi)/2)
    if a[mid] < x: lo := mid + 1
    else:          hi := mid
return lo
```

Its postcondition is not merely “return some plausible position.” It returns
the least index $i$ such that $a[i]\ge x$, or $n$ if no such index exists. Use
invariant

1. $0\le lo\le hi\le n$;
2. every $j<lo$ satisfies $a[j]<x$;
3. every $j\ge hi$ satisfies $a[j]\ge x$.

At initialization, the bounds hold and the two quantified regions are empty.
For preservation, $lo\le mid<hi$ whenever $lo<hi$. If $a[mid]<x$, sortedness gives
$a[j]\le a[mid]<x$ for every $j\le mid$, so assigning $lo=mid+1$ extends the proven
lower region. Otherwise $a[mid]\ge x$, and sortedness gives $a[j]\ge a[mid]\ge x$ for
every $j\ge mid$, so assigning $hi=mid$ extends the upper region. The unchanged
side of the invariant remains true in each branch.

The nonnegative integer variant $hi-lo$ strictly decreases. On the first
branch the new length is $hi-(mid+1)$; on the second it is $mid-lo$; both are
smaller than the old positive length. Thus the loop terminates. At exit,
$lo=hi$. Every earlier index is below $x$, and every index at or after $lo$ is
at least $x$, exactly the lower-bound postcondition.

If the caller wants membership, it additionally tests $lo<n$ and $a[lo]=x$.
This separation avoids an out-of-bounds read when $lo=n$ and handles duplicate
targets by returning the first occurrence.

After one iteration the interval length is at most $\left\lceil L/2 \right\rceil$ when its old
length is $L$; induction gives at most $\left\lceil n/2^k \right\rceil$ after $k$ iterations.
Hence the comparison count is logarithmic. This cost proof uses constant-time
indexing and comparison; it is not automatically a bit-complexity theorem for
arbitrarily large keys.

## 17. Structural induction for a typed evaluator

Consider expression trees generated by integer literal $Lit(z)$, addition
$Add(e1,e2)$, and conditional $IfZero(test,yes,no)$. Define mathematical
meaning recursively:

- $meaning(Lit(z))=z$;
- `meaning(Add(e1,e2))=meaning(e1)+meaning(e2)`;
- `meaning(IfZero(t,y,n))` is `meaning(y)` when $meaning(t)=0$, otherwise
  `meaning(n)`.

An evaluator recursively follows the same cases. Prove theorem: for every
finite expression $e$, evaluation terminates and returns `meaning(e)`.

For a literal, it returns $z$ immediately. For addition, structural induction
gives termination and correct results $v1=meaning(e1)$ and
$v2=meaning(e2)$ for both proper subtrees. The evaluator adds them, returning
the definition’s value. For a conditional, the induction hypothesis first
justifies evaluating $test$. Depending on its result, the evaluator invokes
exactly one proper branch subtree; the relevant hypothesis supplies that
branch’s result and termination. The implementation and meaning choose the
same branch because they inspect the same integer.

The theorem deliberately says nothing about expressions with variables,
overflowing fixed-width addition, or effects in the unchosen branch. Adding
any of those features changes the semantic domain or proof. In particular, a
language with effects cannot replace the conditional by eager evaluation of
both branches and keep the same observational meaning.

Termination could alternatively use tree size as a ranking function: every
recursive call receives a proper subtree. Structural induction packages that
descent into the definition of the data.

## 18. Dynamic programming as induction over dependencies

Let a finite directed acyclic graph describe subproblems, with edge $u\to v$
meaning the value at $u$ depends on value at $v$. A memoized solver is correct
when it computes dependencies before their consumers and combines them with the
declared recurrence.

Under the standard convention that an edge precedes its head in a topological
order, $u\to v$ puts the consumer before its dependency. Evaluate in the
reverse of such a topological order, so every dependency precedes its consumer.
Induct over that evaluation order. Base vertices have no unresolved dependencies, so the
algorithm stores their declared base values. At vertex $u$, every dependency
lies earlier; the induction hypothesis says its table value is correct. Applying
the recurrence therefore stores the mathematical value for $u$. The proof
covers every vertex after finitely many steps.

This argument exposes two independent obligations. A cycle invalidates the
topological induction unless a different fixed-point semantics is intended.
And a correct dependency order does not rescue a wrong recurrence. A robust
implementation validates acyclicity or supplies a cycle contract instead of
silently recursing forever.

Memoization changes cost, not the recurrence’s denotation. Without caching, a
shared subproblem may be recomputed along exponentially many paths. With one
table entry per vertex and one scan per dependency edge, the evaluation takes
$\mathcal{O}(\lvert V\rvert+\lvert E\rvert)$ recurrence operations after the graph is represented explicitly.

## Worked audit: fast Fibonacci pair

Naive recursion repeats work exponentially. A linear iterative algorithm maintains pair $(\mathbb{F}_{i},F_{i+1})$. Initialize $(0,1)$ at $i=0$; update $(a,b)$ to $(b,a+b)$. Induction proves the pair invariant. After $n$ steps, return $a=\mathbb{F}_{n}$.

This uses $\Theta(n)$ additions and constant count of integer variables, but bit complexity grows because Fibonacci numbers have $\Theta(n)$ bits. Calling it constant-space assumes machine words large enough or counts words rather than bits.

## Common errors

- Proving a few base cases but no arbitrary step.
- Assuming $P(k+1)$ while trying to prove it.
- Using strong induction without checking factors are strictly smaller.
- Writing a recurrence that does not match the implementation’s work.
- Maintaining an invariant only on the “usual” branch.
- Giving a decreasing real-valued measure that can descend forever, such as $1,1/2,1/3,\ldots$, without stronger well-founded structure.


## Worked example 1: a complete fast-power trace

For $3^5$, initialise $(r,b,e)=(1,3,5)$. The invariant is $rb^e=243$. The odd step gives $(3,3,4)$; squaring and halving gives $(3,9,2)$; another such step gives $(3,81,1)$; and the final odd step gives $(243,81,0)$. The corresponding products are $1\cdot3^5$, $3\cdot3^4$, $3\cdot9^2$, $3\cdot81$ and $243\cdot81^0$, all equal to 243.

Each nonterminal step strictly decreases the nonnegative exponent. When it reaches zero the invariant gives the returned accumulator exactly, rather than merely suggesting an approximation. The trace also separates the two roles of the proof: the product equation establishes the value, while descent establishes termination. The general invariant proof in Section 10 covers all nonnegative exponents; this trace illustrates its individual branches and does not replace that arbitrary-input argument.

## Worked example 2: an accumulator theorem for reversal

Define $go([],a)=a$ and $go(x::xs,a)=go(xs,x::a)$ on finite lists. Prove the stronger statement $go(xs,a)=reverse(xs)++a$ for every accumulator $a$. For an empty input, both sides equal $a$. For $x::xs$, the induction hypothesis applied to the smaller list with accumulator $x::a$ gives $reverse(xs)++(x::a)$. Since $x::a=[x]++a$, associativity gives $(reverse(xs)++[x])++a=reverse(x::xs)++a$.

Setting $a=[]$ proves ordinary reversal. For the concrete call $go([1,2,3],[])$, the accumulators become $[1]$, $[2,1]$ and $[3,2,1]$ as the unread list shrinks. Termination follows from the decreasing unread length. Proving only the empty-accumulator case would not supply the induction hypothesis needed by the recursive call, whose accumulator is generally nonempty.

## Worked example 3: a reset that defeats a simple counter

A process scans three rules for each of two tokens. Its remaining-work pairs can be $(2,3),(2,2),(2,1),(2,0),(1,3),(1,2),(1,1),(1,0),(0,3)$, at which point there are no tokens left. At a reset, rules remaining increases from zero to three. Even the sum of coordinates increases from two to four, so neither quantity is a valid strictly decreasing natural variant for every transition.

Lexicographic order works: each rule scan decreases the second coordinate with the first fixed, while a token advance decreases the first coordinate regardless of the reset. There can be only finitely many decreases of the first coordinate; between them there can be only finitely many decreases of the second. This proves no infinite execution has these transitions. For this fixed rule bound, $4\cdot tokens+rules$ is also a decreasing integer variant: a reset drops it by one. If reset sizes are not uniformly bounded, the lexicographic argument still applies while that particular scalar formula need not.

### Exercise 7
<a id="induction-recursion-loop-reasoning-ex-7"></a>

Why can marking vertices only after recursive neighbor visits make depth-first search fail to terminate, and how should discovery be repaired?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Follow a two-vertex directed cycle.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A recursive call must see that its ancestor has already been discovered.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

7. **Termination bug.** DFS marks a vertex after recursively visiting
   neighbors. **Solution:** on a cycle, calls can revisit active vertices
   before either is marked. Mark on discovery so the unvisited-vertex measure
   decreases for every recursive call.

On edges $a\to b$ and $b\to a$, an unmarked call at $a$ calls $b$, which sees $a$ still unmarked and calls $a$ again, repeating indefinitely. Marking on entry makes $a$ discovered before visiting $b$, so $b$ does not recurse into it. In a finite graph there are at most $|V|$ newly discovered calls, each scanning finitely many neighbors. The unvisited count decreases for calls that discover a new vertex, not for a helper call that immediately returns on an already marked vertex.

</details>

10. **Mutual recursion.** Even calls Odd on $n-1$; Odd calls Even on $n-1$.

</details>

### Exercise 11
<a id="induction-recursion-loop-reasoning-ex-11"></a>

Trace the fast-power method for $3^{13}$, retaining the invariant at every state.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Record triples $(r,b,e)$, not just a final number.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Odd subtraction and even squaring are separate permitted transitions.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

11. **Fast power trace.** Trace $3^{13}$. **Solution:** start $(r,b,e)=(1,3,13)$;
    odd gives $(3,3,12)$; halve stages produce squared bases while reducing
    exponent, ultimately $r=1594323,e=0$. At every row $r b^e=3^{13}$.

The complete sequence is $(1,3,13)$, $(3,3,12)$, $(3,9,6)$, $(3,81,3)$, $(243,81,2)$, $(243,6561,1)$, $(1594323,6561,0)$. Each odd transition preserves $rb^e$ by moving one base factor into $r$; each even transition preserves it by replacing $b^e$ with $(b^2)^{e/2}$. The final exponent is zero, so the invariant identifies the returned value as $1594323$.

</details>

### Exercise 12
<a id="induction-recursion-loop-reasoning-ex-12"></a>

A desired balance-safety statement uses a bookkeeping field that may be negative during a pending phase. Explain what is needed to formulate a valid strengthened invariant.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Distinguish the observable balance from an internal intermediate field.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A preservation proof requires actual transitions and a relation between phase and amounts.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

12. **Invariant strengthening.** Desired safety is $balance\ge 0$, but deposits
    in a pending phase can temporarily make a bookkeeping field negative.
    **Solution:** state which balance is observable and add a relation among
    phase, pending amount, and ledger total; the unqualified field inequality
    may not be the right invariant.

The verbal description alone does not determine one correct invariant. State the transitions, define the observable quantity and relate it to ledger and pending amounts in every phase. For example, if the observable balance is ledger plus a pending credit, nonnegativity of that sum can coexist with a negative ledger field. One must then prove the phase relation and sum bound initially and after every permitted transition. Replacing the desired field inequality with an unproved slogan does not establish safety.

</details>

### Exercise 13
<a id="induction-recursion-loop-reasoning-ex-13"></a>

Trace lower-bound binary search on $[1,3,3,8]$ for target $3$, and explain why the answer is the first matching index.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use half-open endpoints and zero-based indices.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

At each comparison preserve the already excluded lower and upper regions.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

13. **Lower-bound trace.** Trace the half-open binary search on
    $a=[1,3,3,8]$, $x=3$. **Solution:** $(lo,hi)=(0,4)$, $mid=2$ has value 3 so
    $hi=2$; $mid=1$ has value 3 so $hi=1$; $mid=0$ has value 1 so $lo=1$.
    Exit returns the first qualifying index 1.

The states and comparisons are exactly those in the retained solution. At exit $lo=hi=1$. The invariant says index zero has value below three and every index at least one has value at least three. The value at index one equals the target, so it is a match and no earlier match exists. This conclusion is stronger than merely returning some index carrying three, which could also be two.

</details>

### Exercise 14
<a id="induction-recursion-loop-reasoning-ex-14"></a>

In a structural proof for a conditional evaluator, why do available hypotheses for both branches not mean that both branches execute?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

A mathematical induction hypothesis is a proved fact, not an instruction.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The run selects its branch after evaluating the test.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

14. **Evaluator branch.** Why does the conditional proof not need induction
    hypotheses for both branches to execute? **Solution:** structural induction
    supplies facts for both proper subtrees, but a given run invokes only the
    branch chosen by the test. The unused hypothesis remains available but is
    not operational work.

Structural induction provides termination and semantic facts for every proper subtree. A particular run uses the test fact and the fact for the selected branch; the other fact can remain unused. The operational definition and mathematical meaning must choose the same branch from the same test value. Eager execution of both branches would be another evaluator, especially if errors or effects were later added.

</details>

### Exercise 15
<a id="induction-recursion-loop-reasoning-ex-15"></a>

Why does topological dynamic programming not directly solve $x=y+1$ and $y=x-1$?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Draw the dependency arrows before trying an evaluation order.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Eliminate one variable to see whether a unique value is determined.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

15. **Cyclic dependency.** Equations $x=y+1$, $y=x-1$ form a cycle.
    **Solution:** topological dynamic programming does not apply. The equations
    have many solutions, so a fixed point, boundary condition, or rejection
    policy is needed; arbitrary recursive evaluation can diverge.

The dependency graph has a directed cycle, so neither value can be computed from already supplied earlier values by this scheme. Substitution yields $x=x$, leaving every pair $(t+1,t)$ as a solution over the integers. A boundary condition, a specified fixed-point semantics or a rejection contract is needed. The algebraic existence of solutions does not make naive recursive evaluation terminate or identify a unique intended result.

</details>

### Exercise 16
<a id="induction-recursion-loop-reasoning-ex-16"></a>

For the half-open binary search, prove the interval shrinks even when $mid=lo$, and diagnose the update $lo:=mid$ instead of $mid+1$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

An active interval has positive integer length.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Check the two branches separately at the smallest positive sizes.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

16. **Variant check.** In binary search, could $hi-lo$ stay unchanged when
    $mid=lo$? **Solution:** if the lower branch is taken, $lo$ becomes
    $mid+1$; otherwise $hi$ becomes $mid=lo$. Either makes a positive interval
    strictly shorter. Assigning $lo=mid$ in the lower branch would fail this
    proof and can loop on a two-element interval.

With the correct lower update, the new length is $hi-mid-1<hi-lo$; with the upper update it is $mid-lo<hi-lo$. In particular, when $mid=lo$, either the lower endpoint increases by one or the upper endpoint becomes the lower endpoint. The incorrect update can leave $(lo,hi)$ unchanged when the interval has length one or two and the comparison takes the lower branch, allowing an infinite loop with the same target and midpoint.

</details>

## Summary

Induction follows the way inputs or states are generated. Strong and structural hypotheses support recursive calls; invariants express the same reasoning at a program point. Strengthened claims make accumulator and evaluator proofs compositional. Partial correctness and termination require separate arguments, and a variant must descend in a genuinely well-founded order. Recurrence bounds additionally depend on the chosen operation and representation costs. The next chapter applies finite decomposition and indicator reasoning to counting, randomness and adversarial inputs.
