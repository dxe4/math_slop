
# Percentages

A sale promises twenty per cent off, a class report says sixty per cent of
responses were correct, and a battery display shows thirty-five per cent.
A percentage compares an amount with a reference whole using a standard
base of one hundred. The number alone is incomplete: twenty per cent of
ten pounds differs from twenty per cent of one hundred pounds.

Read [Ratio and Proportion](ratio-proportion.md) first. You should understand
fractions, decimal place value, and scaling by a fixed factor. All reference
amounts used in percentage comparisons are positive. We work with exact
arithmetic in the examples; a real price is rounded to an allowed currency
unit only when the question specifies that step. A percentage describes a
comparison rather than the reason a quantity changed.

## Per hundred of a named whole

**Definition 7.1 (Percentage).** One per cent, written $1\%$, is one
hundredth of a specified reference amount. A percentage $p\%$ means the
fraction $p/100$ of that reference amount.

Thus $25\%=25/100=1/4=0.25$. These are different notations for the
same scale factor. The symbol does not by itself give a count or a unit:
$25\%$ of forty counters is ten counters, while $25\%$ of eighty
counters is twenty counters. State the whole before finding the part.
A percentage can exceed one hundred: $150\%$ of an amount is one and a
half times that amount. It can also be fractional, such as $2.5\%$.

**Worked example 7.2 (Use friendly pieces).** Find $15\%$ of $£80$.
Ten per cent is $£8$ because it is one tenth of the amount. Five per cent
is half of that, $£4$. Combining the parts gives $£12$. The direct
calculation agrees: $0.15\times£80=£12$. The percentage is a fraction
of the eighty-pound reference, not an amount of fifteen pounds.

Useful landmarks are $50\%=1/2$, $25\%=1/4$, $10\%=1/10$
and $1\%=1/100$. To find $35\%$, one can combine three ten-per-cent
parts and one five-per-cent part. This is distributivity: taking a total
fraction of an amount can be decomposed into several fractions with the
same reference whole. If the reference changes midway, the parts can no
longer be added so casually.

## Find the percentage from a part and a whole

**Definition 7.3 (A part as a percentage).** If a part has amount $a$
and its positive reference whole has amount $b$ in the same units, its
percentage of that whole is $(a\div b)\times100\%$.

The units cancel in the quotient, leaving a comparison. If twenty-four of
thirty counters are blue, the blue fraction is $24/30=4/5=0.8$,
so the blue percentage is $80\%$. This does not require there to be
exactly one hundred counters. A percentage describes what the fraction
would be on the common comparison scale.

**Worked example 7.4 (Two denominators in one room).** A room has twenty
adults and ten children. Children are $10/30=1/3$ of all people,
which is $33\frac13\%$. The number of children is also $10/20=1/2$
of the number of adults, or $50\%$. Both comparisons are correct, but
answer different questions. “Children are fifty per cent” is incomplete
unless the reference is given. Counting groups separately before selecting
the denominator prevents this ambiguity.

When reporting a percentage from observations, keep its underlying count.
Eight out of ten and eight hundred out of one thousand both give $80\%$,
but they describe different numbers of observations. A percentage alone
does not establish that either collection is representative of a larger
population. This chapter calculates comparisons; questions about sampling
need further assumptions.

## Increases and decreases use the starting amount

**Definition 7.5 (Percentage change).** For a positive starting amount,
a percentage increase compares the amount added with that starting amount;
a percentage decrease compares the amount removed with that starting amount.

An increase by $20\%$ leaves $120\%$ of the original, so its scale
factor is $1.2$. A decrease by $20\%$ leaves $80\%$, with scale
factor $0.8$. The final amount can be calculated directly using the
remaining fraction rather than by computing the change separately.

**Worked example 7.6 (A sale followed by a rise).** A price of $£100$
falls by $20\%$ to $£80$. It then rises by $20\%$ of its new
price, adding $£16$ and reaching $£96$. The two percentages have
different reference amounts. Multiplying factors gives the same result:
$£100\times0.8\times1.2=£96$. Equal percentage falls and rises do
not generally undo one another. The inverse of multiplying by $0.8$
is dividing by $0.8$, not multiplying by $1.2$.

This also explains why a decrease from fifty to forty is $20\%$,
while the reverse increase from forty to fifty is $25\%$.
The numerical difference is ten in each direction, but the reference
amount changes. A phrase such as “ten per cent more” always needs a
clear answer to “more than what?”

## Recover the starting whole

Sometimes the final amount and the percentage relationship are known, while
the original amount is missing. Work backwards using the scale factor.
Do not take the same stated percentage of the final amount unless that is
what the problem actually specifies.

**Worked example 7.7 (Undo a discount).** A jacket costs $£54$ after a
$10\%$ discount. The sale price is $90\%$ of its original price,
so the original price is $£54\div0.9=£60$. Checking gives
$10\%$ of $£60$ equal to $£6$, and $£60-£6=£54$.
Adding $10\%$ of $£54$ would give $£59.40$, which does not
undo the stated discount because the added fraction uses the wrong whole.

A ratio table is another way to reason: ninety parts correspond to
fifty-four pounds, so ten parts correspond to six pounds and one hundred
parts to sixty pounds. Both methods preserve the same proportional
comparison. Choose the explanation you can justify clearly rather than a
rule based on the visible word “discount”.

## Percentage points compare percentages

If a reported percentage rises from $30\%$ to $40\%$, the numerical
change is ten **percentage points**. Relative to the original thirty
per cent, the increase is $10/30=1/3$, or $33\frac13\%$.
Ten percentage points and a ten-per-cent relative increase are different
changes. A ten-per-cent increase on thirty per cent would give thirty-three
per cent, since $30\%\times1.1=33\%$.

This distinction matters whenever the measured quantity is already a
percentage. Say whether a comparison subtracts percentages directly or
scales the starting percentage. Also check whether their underlying
reference populations agree: combining a percentage from one group with
a percentage from another may require the group sizes, just as combining
pack prices requires their numbers of items.

For instance, one group has two correct responses out of four and another
has nine out of ten. Their percentages are fifty and ninety. Together,
there are eleven correct responses out of fourteen, giving
$11/14\times100\%$, about $78.6\%$. The simple average seventy
per cent gives equal weight to the two groups, rather than equal weight
to the fourteen responses. State the intended weighting before averaging.

## State the comparison before calculating a change

The same pair of amounts can support several true percentage statements.
If a quantity moves from twenty to thirty, it gains ten, which is fifty
per cent of the starting twenty. The gain is also one third of the final
thirty. The latter fraction is not the usual percentage increase, because
that phrase uses the starting amount. A complete explanation names the
chosen reference instead of treating one percentage as the only possible
comparison of the two numbers.

A zero reference needs care. If an amount increases from zero to five,
the ordinary relative percentage increase would require dividing the change
by zero. It is therefore undefined under the positive-reference definition
used here. The absolute increase of five is still perfectly meaningful.
Saying “an infinite per cent increase” would introduce a different convention
without explaining it and is not the arithmetic definition in this chapter.

For a nonzero starting amount, percentage loss and the increase needed to
recover it can differ dramatically. Losing fifty per cent leaves half the
original; regaining the original then requires adding the entire remaining
amount, a one-hundred-per-cent increase. Losing twenty-five per cent leaves
three quarters; recovering the missing quarter requires adding one third
of those remaining three quarters. Drawing four equal original parts makes
the changing denominator visible. The fraction lost from the original and
the fraction needed from the remainder answer different questions.

Before presenting a percentage, write its numerator quantity, its positive
reference quantity and their units. Decide whether the result is an exact
fraction, a terminating decimal or an approximation. This small record is
often more informative than displaying additional percentage digits without
a stated denominator.

## Worked example: undoing a reduction uses a new whole

A toy has a marked price of forty pounds. A reduction of twenty-five per cent
removes one quarter of that price, which is ten pounds, leaving thirty pounds.
Suppose the shop later raises the reduced price by twenty-five per cent. This
time the whole used in the calculation is thirty pounds. Its quarter is seven
pounds and fifty pence, so the raised price is thirty-seven pounds and fifty
pence. The two percentage changes have equal names but different bases.

To restore the original forty pounds from thirty, the increase must instead
be ten pounds. Compare that increase with the current whole, thirty pounds:
ten is one third of thirty. Thus the required increase is one third, or
thirty-three and one third per cent. There is no contradiction with the
original quarter reduction. A quarter of forty and a third of thirty both
describe ten pounds.

When a percentage answer seems surprising, draw a bar for the whole and label
its numerical value before marking the changed part. A second bar should show
the new whole before the next change is applied. Equal lengths on the page
must not silently stand for different amounts. The choice of whole is part
of every percentage statement, including a percentage used to undo another.

## Exercises

### Exercise 7.1
<a id="percentages-ex-1"></a>

Write $35\%$ as a fraction in simplest terms and as a decimal.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Percent means per hundred.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Thirty-five and one hundred have a common factor of five.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$35\%=35/100=7/20=0.35$. Each notation describes the same
scale factor, without yet specifying a quantity to which it is applied.

</details>

### Exercise 7.2
<a id="percentages-ex-2"></a>

Find $25\%$ of sixty counters.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Twenty-five per cent is one quarter.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Split sixty into four equal groups.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

One quarter of sixty is $60\div4=15$ counters. The unit remains
counters; twenty-five is the percentage, not the count selected.

</details>

### Exercise 7.3
<a id="percentages-ex-3"></a>

Eighteen of twenty-four tiles are blue. What percentage are blue?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Divide the blue count by the complete tile count.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Simplify eighteen twenty-fourths to quarters.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$18/24=3/4=0.75$, so $75\%$ of the tiles are blue.
The denominator is all twenty-four tiles, not only the six non-blue tiles.

</details>

### Exercise 7.4
<a id="percentages-ex-4"></a>

A price of $£45$ increases by $20\%$. Find the new price.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Find twenty per cent of the original forty-five pounds.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Twenty per cent is one fifth, and the increase is added to the original.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The increase is $£45\div5=£9$, so the new price is $£54$.
Equivalently $£45\times1.2=£54$. The starting price supplies
the reference amount.

</details>

### Exercise 7.5
<a id="percentages-ex-5"></a>

A price of $£80$ is reduced by $15\%$. Find the sale price.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

After the reduction, eighty-five per cent remains.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Ten per cent of eighty is eight and five per cent is four.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The discount is $£8+£4=£12$, leaving $£80-£12=£68$.
The direct scale-factor calculation is $£80\times0.85=£68$.

</details>

### Exercise 7.6
<a id="percentages-ex-6"></a>

A $£50$ price falls by $10\%$ and then rises by $10\%$ of
its new value. Find the final price.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Calculate the reduced price before computing the rise.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The second ten per cent uses forty-five pounds as its reference.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The fall leaves $£45$. The increase is then $£4.50$, giving
$£49.50$. The factors $0.9$ and $1.1$ multiply to $0.99$,
so the original price is not recovered.

</details>

### Exercise 7.7
<a id="percentages-ex-7"></a>

A ticket costs $£36$ after a $20\%$ discount. Find its original price.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Thirty-six pounds represents eighty per cent of the original.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Divide by the retained fraction, not by the discount fraction.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The original price is $£36\div0.8=£45$. Twenty per cent of
forty-five is nine, and subtracting it gives thirty-six, checking the
original relationship.

</details>

### Exercise 7.8
<a id="percentages-ex-8"></a>

Find $150\%$ of twelve metres and explain why the result exceeds twelve.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

One hundred and fifty per cent is one and a half.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Take the complete twelve metres and half of it again.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$150\%$ of twelve metres is $12+6=18$ metres. A scale factor
of $1.5$ exceeds one, so it enlarges the starting positive length.

</details>

### Exercise 7.9
<a id="percentages-ex-9"></a>

A percentage changes from $20\%$ to $25\%$. Give the percentage-point
change and the relative percentage increase.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Subtract directly for percentage points.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

For relative change, divide the five-point difference by the original twenty.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The change is five percentage points. Relative to the original value,
$5/20=1/4$, so the increase is $25\%$. These two descriptions
use different comparison operations.

</details>

### Exercise 7.10
<a id="percentages-ex-10"></a>

A group records one success out of two attempts, and another records eight
out of eight. What percentage of all ten attempts succeeded?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Combine successes and attempts separately.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Do not give the two groups equal weight when their attempt counts differ.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

There are nine successes out of ten attempts, so the combined percentage
is $90\%$. Averaging the group percentages fifty and one hundred would
give seventy-five, which weights groups rather than attempts and answers
a different question.

</details>

## Summary and next connection

A percentage uses one hundred as a comparison scale, but its reference whole
must still be named. Scale factors explain percentage changes and their
inverses. Percentage points differ from relative percentage changes, and
combined groups need their underlying counts. The next chapter extends the
number line to negative positions and directed changes.
