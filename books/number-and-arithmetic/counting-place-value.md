
# Counting and Place Value

A cupboard contains cups, a timetable records minutes, and a parcel label gives
a mass. Before calculating with their numbers, ask what one unit means. Twelve
cups and twelve minutes share the number twelve, but they are different
quantities. This first chapter develops whole-number counting, the role of
zero, and the decimal notation used to record a count. It also explains why
the same digit can represent different amounts in different positions.

No earlier mathematics course is required. Practise with a small collection
of counters, buttons or marks on paper. Reading the digits zero through nine
is useful, but their grouping into larger numbers is developed here. By the
end, you should be able to count a collection without omissions or repetitions,
explain the value of every digit in a whole number, compare whole numbers, and
locate them on a number line. Fractions and negative numbers come later.

## Counting is matching, one object at a time

**Definition 1.1 (Whole numbers).** The whole numbers are
$0,1,2,3,4,\ldots$. Zero names the count of an empty collection. Each next
number counts one more object than the previous number.

To count six buttons, point to one button while saying “one”, another while
saying “two”, and continue until each button has been used exactly once. The
last number spoken gives the count. Saying the number words without matching
them to objects is reciting a list; counting also requires that matching.
A useful physical method is to move each counted object to another side of
the table. The two groups then record which objects have already been counted.

Rearranging a collection does not change its count if no objects are added,
removed or combined. A long row of seven counters and a tightly packed group
of the same seven counters have equal counts. Length of the picture is not
number of objects. Likewise, large buttons do not count as more buttons than
small ones unless the question asks about mass, area or some other quantity.

**Worked example 1.2 (An arrangement that looks larger).** Put five counters
in a short row. Spread them into a longer row without adding or removing any.
The count remains five: every original counter is still present exactly once.
To compare with another collection, pair one counter from each collection.
If every counter on both sides is paired, the counts agree, regardless of the
space occupied. If one collection has unpaired counters after the other is
exhausted, that collection has the larger count.

A count need not describe physical objects. Four empty seats are four places
where somebody could sit; zero passengers still describes an actual empty bus.
An empty collection is different from unknown information. “There are zero
messages” states a count. “The message count is unavailable” does not tell us
whether there are zero, five or fifty messages.

## Bundling into tens

A long list of marks is inconvenient to read. Our decimal system groups ten
ones into one ten, ten tens into one hundred, and ten hundreds into one
thousand. The word decimal refers to this use of groups of ten. A bundle is
a different description of the same collection, so exchanging ten individual
counters for one marked ten-bundle preserves the count.

**Definition 1.3 (Place value).** In a decimal whole-number numeral, each
position has ten times the value of the position immediately to its right.
The rightmost position counts ones, the next tens, then hundreds, thousands,
and so on. A digit tells how many units of that position are present.

Thus $347$ means three hundreds, four tens and seven ones. In expanded form,

$$347=300+40+7.$$

The digit $4$ represents forty in $347$, but four in $374$. Digits are the
symbols $0,1,\ldots,9$; a numeral such as $347$ uses those symbols to name
a number. A numeral can contain repeated digits with different values. In
$444$, the three occurrences represent four hundred, forty and four.

A standard decimal place contains a digit from zero to nine because ten units
can be exchanged for one of the next place. Twelve tens can therefore be
regrouped as one hundred and two tens. This does not make twelve tens an
invalid quantity; it makes $120$ its usual decimal notation. Standard notation
is a consistent way of choosing among equivalent descriptions.

**Worked example 1.4 (Regrouping a collection).** A box has twenty-seven loose
counters and three ten-bundles. Exchange twenty of the loose counters for two
new bundles. The collection is then five ten-bundles and seven ones, so its
count is $57$. Equally, the original description gives $27+30=57$. The exchange
changes the packaging, not the number of counters. Counting three bundles as
three counters would confuse the units being counted.

## Zero holds a place

In $407$, the zero says that there are no tens after the hundreds and ones
have been recorded. The numeral means $400+0+7$. Removing its zero gives $47$,
which instead means four tens and seven ones. Zero therefore records a real
absence at a specified place; it is not decoration that can always be erased.

Leading zeros are different. The whole-number numerals $007$ and $7$ name the
same count, because the extra places contain no hundreds or tens. A code or
identifier may nevertheless require its leading zeros. Bus service 007, a
postcode, a room label or a password is not necessarily a count. Its notation
follows the rules of that labelling system. Arithmetic place-value rules should
not silently change an identifier.

Grouping separators can help readers recognise large whole numbers. Here
$12\,405$ means twelve thousand four hundred and five; the thin space only
groups digits. The digit order remains important. When writing a number from
words, make a place-value table first if a missing place is easy to overlook.
For “six thousand and eight”, the thousands digit is six, hundreds and tens
are zero, and ones are eight, giving $6008$.

**Worked example 1.5 (Reading an inventory).** An inventory shows $30\,204$
screws. Its nonzero positions are three ten-thousands, two hundreds and four
ones, so the expanded form is $30\,000+200+4$. There are no thousands or tens
in the standard regrouping. Saying “thirty thousand two hundred and four”
records every nonzero place. It does not mean thirty separate boxes unless
a box size has also been supplied.

## Comparing whole numbers

Write $a<b$ when $a$ is smaller than $b$, and $a>b$ when $a$ is larger than
$b$. Equality, written $a=b$, means the two expressions name the same number.
When comparing counts of one kind of object, pairing gives a concrete meaning
for these relations. A count with objects left over is larger.

For ordinary decimal whole-number numerals without leading zeros, more digits
means a larger number. Any three-digit number is at least $100$, while any
two-digit number is at most $99$. For numerals with the same number of digits,
compare from the left. At the first different position, the larger digit
identifies the larger number. Later positions cannot overturn that difference:
one hundred is larger than all ninety-nine units that could follow it.

For example, $582$ and $579$ have equal hundreds digits. Their tens digits
are eight and seven, so $582>579$. The ones digits do not change that result.
Comparing from the right would be unreliable: nine ones cannot compensate for
the missing ten in the smaller number. Strip off leading zeros before applying
the digit-count rule to a whole-number value.

A **number line** places numbers at positions along a straight line, with equal
spacing for equal differences. Put zero at a starting mark and one at the
next mark. Continuing the same spacing locates two, three and the other whole
numbers. A number farther to the right is larger. Between the marks for three
and four there is no whole number, though later chapters introduce other kinds
of numbers lying there.

Counting positions differs from counting gaps. The marks from zero through
five are six marks, but there are five unit gaps between them. This distinction
appears in fence-post, calendar and queue problems. Write down whether the
question counts objects, positions, intervals or moves before choosing an
answer. A ruler measures intervals from its zero mark; it does not start by
calling the zero mark “one centimetre”.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1.1
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-1"></a>

A collection contains no counters. What is its count, and how does that differ
from a collection whose count has not been reported?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

An empty collection still has a definite size.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Separate the amount present from what a reader knows about that amount.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Its count is zero. An unreported count is unknown information and need not
be zero: there could be any permitted number of counters. The numeral $0$
states an amount rather than a lack of knowledge.

</details>

### Exercise 1.2
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-2"></a>

Write $526$ in hundreds, tens and ones and in expanded form.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Start from the rightmost digit.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The middle digit counts groups of ten.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

There are five hundreds, two tens and six ones. Their values are $500,20,6$,
so $526=500+20+6$. The digit two represents twenty because of its position.

</details>

### Exercise 1.3
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-3"></a>

What does the digit $8$ represent in $804$, in $482$, and in $248$?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Its value depends on its distance from the rightmost place.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Identify hundreds, tens and ones separately.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

In $804$ it represents eight hundreds, or $800$; in $482$, eight tens, or
$80$; in $248$, eight ones, or $8$. The symbol is unchanged but its place
changes the unit it counts.

</details>

### Exercise 1.4
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-4"></a>

Write “seven thousand and thirty-two” as a decimal whole-number numeral.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Make four places: thousands, hundreds, tens and ones.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

No hundreds are named, so that place needs zero.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The digits are seven thousands, zero hundreds, three tens and two ones,
giving $7032=7000+30+2$. Writing $732$ would move the seven into the hundreds
place and describe a different number.

</details>

### Exercise 1.5
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-5"></a>

Regroup fourteen tens and six ones into standard decimal notation.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Exchange ten tens for one hundred.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Four tens remain after that exchange.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Fourteen tens are $140$, or one hundred and four tens. With six ones the
count is $146=100+40+6$. The exchange leaves the number of counted objects
unchanged.

</details>

### Exercise 1.6
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-6"></a>

Put $708,780,078,807$ in increasing order when each is read as a whole number.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

The leading zero in $078$ does not count a nonzero hundred.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

After identifying $78$, compare the hundreds and then tens of the others.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The order is $078<708<780<807$, with $078=78$. Among the two numbers with
seven hundreds, zero tens comes before eight tens. Eight hundreds is greater
than either of them.

</details>

### Exercise 1.7
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-7"></a>

A shelf contains four boxes of ten cups and nine loose cups. How many cups
are present? Explain why the answer is not thirteen.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

A box counts ten cups, not one cup.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Translate the four boxes into tens before combining them with the ones.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Four tens and nine ones give $40+9=49$ cups. Thirteen counts four boxes plus
nine loose items without using a consistent unit. It is not the cup count.

</details>

### Exercise 1.8
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-8"></a>

A straight fence has posts at every whole-number metre from zero through
six, including both endpoints. How many posts and how many one-metre gaps
are there?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

List the positions rather than treating the first mark as a gap.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Every consecutive pair of posts creates one gap.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The post positions are $0,1,2,3,4,5,6$, seven positions. The gaps are
$0$ to $1$, $1$ to $2$, through $5$ to $6$, six gaps. Endpoint objects and
intervals between them have different counts.

</details>

### Exercise 1.9
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-9"></a>

Explain why erasing the zero in $305$ changes its value but erasing the
leading zero in $035$ does not.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Expand each numeral into place values.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Ask whether a nonzero digit moves into a different-valued place.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$305=300+5$, whereas $35=30+5$, so the three moves from hundreds to tens
when the middle zero is erased. But $035=0+30+5=35$: the leading zero adds
no value and the nonzero digits retain their places.

</details>

### Exercise 1.10
<a id="counting-place-value-ex-10"></a>

A child counts eight buttons, then spreads them out and reports eleven.
Describe a way to establish the count while guarding against repeated or
missed buttons.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Changing the spacing alone cannot change the collection.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Separate counted buttons physically from uncounted ones.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Move each button once from an uncounted group to a counted group, speaking
one next counting word for each move. Stop when the uncounted group is empty.
If the collection is the original eight buttons, the final word is eight.
Spreading changes no objects; eleven indicates a counting or reporting error,
not a different size caused by spacing.

</details>

## Summary and next connection

Counting matches number words to objects without omissions or repetitions.
Decimal notation bundles ones into tens, hundreds and larger places; zero
records an empty place. Compare numbers by their values, and distinguish
counts from codes and from intervals between objects. The next chapter uses
these bundles to explain addition and subtraction, including the exchanges
behind written arithmetic.
