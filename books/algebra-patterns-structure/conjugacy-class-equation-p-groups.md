
# Conjugacy, the Class Equation, and p-Groups

Conjugation compares an element with all its internal changes of coordinates.
The resulting orbits are conjugacy classes; their stabilizers are centralizers.
Orbit–stabilizer then converts noncommutativity into an exact partition of the
group called the class equation.

For a finite group whose order is a power of a prime, every nontrivial orbit
size is divisible by that prime. The class equation therefore forces fixed
points: every nontrivial finite $p$-group has nontrivial center. That one fact
starts a strong structural theory and supplies the missing bridge into the
Sylow chapter.

The prerequisites are normality and quotient groups, permutation conjugacy,
and the action results of Chapter 8. Chapter 10 will use the $p$-group
fixed-point principle developed here to prove the Sylow theorems.

## 1. Conjugation is an action

For $g \in G$, define the inner automorphism

$c_g(x)=gxg^{-1}$.

It preserves multiplication because

$c_g(xy)=gxyg^{-1}=gx(g^{-1}g)yg^{-1}=c_g(x)c_g(y)$.

Also $c_g \circ c_h=c_{gh}$. Thus $G$ acts on itself by conjugation:

$g \cdot x=gxg^{-1}$.

**Definition 9.1 (Conjugacy class).**

The **conjugacy class** of $x$ is

$Cl_G(x)=\{gxg^{-1}:g \in G\}$.

The **centralizer** of $x$ is

$C_G(x)=\{g \in G:gx=xg\}$.

It is exactly the stabilizer of $x$, since $gxg^{-1}=x$ iff $gx=xg$.
Orbit–stabilizer therefore gives, for finite $G$,

$|Cl_G(x)|=[G:C_G(x)]$.

In particular, every conjugacy-class size divides $|G|$.

### Worked example in $S_3$

**Worked example 9.2 (Classes and centralizers in S₃).**

The conjugacy class of $(1 2)$ consists of all three transpositions, since
conjugation relabels moved points. Its centralizer has order $6/3=2$ and is
$\{e,(1 2)\}$. The two three-cycles form a class of size two, with centralizer
$A_3$ of order three.

## 2. The center is the fixed-point set

The **center** of $G$ is

$Z(G)=\{z \in G:zg=gz \text{ for every } g \in G\}$.

It is an abelian normal subgroup. It is a subgroup because elements commuting
with everything remain so under products and inverses. It is normal because
for $z \in Z(G)$, conjugation gives $gzg^{-1}=z$.

An element lies in $Z(G)$ exactly when its conjugacy class has one element.
Equivalently,

$x \in Z(G)$ iff $C_G(x)=G$.

The kernel of the conjugation homomorphism

$G\to \operatorname{Aut}(G)$, $g\to c_g$,

is $Z(G)$. The first isomorphism theorem identifies the inner automorphism
group with

$\operatorname{Inn}(G) \cong G/Z(G)$.

An abelian group has $Z(G)=G$ and trivial inner automorphism group. The
converse is immediate as well.

## 3. The class equation

**Theorem 9.3 (Class equation).**

Conjugacy classes partition $G$. Separate the one-element classes, whose
elements are central, from representatives $x_1,...,x_r$ of the noncentral
classes. The **class equation** is

$|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{i=1}^r [G:C_G(x_i)]$.

This is not a new counting principle beyond actions: it is the orbit
partition for conjugation with orbit–stabilizer substituted into every term.
Its force comes from the restrictions subgroup indices place on the summands.

### $S_3$

The classes are

$\{e\}$, $\{(1 2),(1 3),(2 3)\}$, $\{(1 2 3),(1 3 2)\}$.

Thus

$6=1+3+2$.

The center is trivial, meaning it contains only identity.

### The square group $D_4$

**Worked example 9.4 (Five square-group classes).**

Use the order-eight convention

$D_4=\langle r,s | r^4=e,s^2=e,srs=r^{-1}\rangle$.

Its conjugacy classes are

$\{e\}$, $\{r^2\}$, $\{r,r^3\}$, $\{s,r^2s\}$, $\{rs,r^3s\}$.

Hence

$8=1+1+2+2+2$, and $Z(D_4)=\{e,r^2\}$.

The two reflection classes correspond to the two kinds of axes through a
square.

### The quaternion group

**Worked example 9.5 (Matching class sizes without isomorphism).**

Let $Q_8=\{1,-1,i,-i,j,-j,k,-k\}$ be the multiplicative subgroup of the Hamilton quaternions generated by $i,j$, where $i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1$ and $ij=k=-ji$ (with cyclic analogues). Associativity follows from quaternion multiplication, for example its faithful realization inside $2\times2$ complex matrices by $i=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm i&0\\0&-\mathrm i\end{pmatrix}$ and $j=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, with $k=ij$. Its conjugacy classes are

$\{1\}$, $\{-1\}$, $\{i,-i\}$, $\{j,-j\}$, $\{k,-k\}$.

Again $8=1+1+2+2+2$, although $Q_8$ is not isomorphic to $D_4$. A class-size
list is useful but not always a complete group invariant.

## 4. Conjugacy preserves structural data

Conjugate elements have the same order because

$(gxg^{-1})^n=gx^ng^{-1}$.

Their centralizers are conjugate:

$C_G(gxg^{-1})=gC_G(x)g^{-1}$.

Any homomorphism $\phi:G\to H$ sends conjugates to conjugates because

$\phi(gxg^{-1})=\phi(g)\phi(x)\phi(g)^{-1}$.

It can merge different conjugacy classes, especially when it has nontrivial
kernel, but it cannot split one class across unrelated target classes.

For $S_n$, Chapter 7 proved that conjugacy is exactly equality of cycle type.
The class size for a permutation can therefore be found by counting
relabelings or by calculating its centralizer.

## 5. Normal subgroups are unions of classes

A subgroup $N\le G$ is normal exactly when it is closed under conjugation.
Therefore it is normal exactly when it is a union of conjugacy classes that
includes the identity class.

This gives a finite normality filter: candidate normal-subgroup orders must be
obtainable by summing class sizes, always including one for identity. It is a
necessary condition, not always sufficient, because a union of classes need
not be closed under multiplication.

For example, in $S_3$ the possible class sums including identity are
$1$, $1+2=3$, $1+3=4$, and $6$. Lagrange rules out four; the order-three union
is $A_3$, which is indeed normal. Thus the only normal subgroups are
$\{e\}$, $A_3$, and $S_3$.

## 6. Fixed-point congruence for $p$-group actions

Let a finite $p$-group $P$ act on a finite set $X$. Orbit–stabilizer says
every orbit size $[P:P_x]$ is a power of $p$. The one-point orbits are exactly
the points fixed by all of $P$; every other orbit size is divisible by $p$.
Therefore

$|X| \equiv |X^P| \bmod p$,

where $X^P=\{x:g \cdot x=x \text{ for every } g \in P\}$.

The set $X^P$ is the common fixed-point set, not the union of fixed-point sets
of individual elements. If $p$ does not divide $|X|$, the congruence forces at
least one common fixed point.

This result will reappear in Chapter 10 when a $p$-subgroup acts on a coset set
whose size is prime to $p$.

## 7. Every nontrivial finite $p$-group has nontrivial center

A finite $p$-group has order $p^a$ for some integer $a\ge0$; the case $a=0$ is the trivial group. Let a nontrivial finite $p$-group $P$ act on itself by conjugation. Its common
fixed-point set is $Z(P)$. The fixed-point congruence gives

$|P| \equiv |Z(P)| \bmod p$.

Since $p$ divides $|P|$, it divides $|Z(P)|$. The center contains identity and
has size at least $p$; it is nontrivial.

Equivalently, inspect the class equation. Every noncentral class has size a
power of $p$ larger than one, hence a multiple of $p$. Subtracting them from
$|P|$ leaves $|Z(P)|$ divisible by $p$.

This theorem does not say a $p$-group is abelian. $D_4$ and $Q_8$ are
nonabelian $2$-groups with centers of order two.

## 8. Groups of order $p^2$ are abelian

First prove a general lemma.

**Lemma 9.2 (Cyclic central quotient).** If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is
abelian.

Suppose $G/Z(G)=\langle gZ(G)\rangle$. Every element has form $g^a z$ with
$z \in Z(G)$. Two such elements commute:

$(g^a z)(g^b w)=g^{a+b}zw=g^{b+a}wz=(g^b w)(g^a z)$.

Now let $|G|=p^2$. The center has order divisible by $p$, so it has order $p$
or $p^2$. In the latter case $G$ is already abelian. In the former case
$G/Z(G)$ has order $p$ and is cyclic, so the lemma again makes $G$ abelian.

Combining this with cyclic-group classification shows every group of order
$p^2$ is isomorphic either to $C_{p^2}$ or to $C_p \times C_p$. The final
isomorphism classification uses the existence and orders of elements: if some
element has order $p^2$, the group is cyclic; otherwise every nonidentity
element has order $p$, and choosing two independent elements yields the
product.

## 9. Normal subgroups meet the center

Let $N$ be a nontrivial normal subgroup of a finite $p$-group $P$. Conjugation
by $P$ preserves $N$, so $P$ acts on the set $N$. The common fixed points are

$N \cap Z(P)$.

Because $N$ is a nontrivial subgroup of a $p$-group, $|N|$ is divisible by
$p$. The fixed-point congruence gives

$|N \cap Z(P)| \equiv |N| \equiv 0 \bmod p$.

The intersection therefore contains a nonidentity element.

Two useful consequences follow.

1. Every normal subgroup of order $p$ lies in $Z(P)$, since its nontrivial
   intersection with the center must be all of it.
2. Every nontrivial finite $p$-group has a normal subgroup of order $p$: choose nonidentity
   $z \in Z(P)$. If $\operatorname{ord}(z)=p^k$, then $z^{p^{k-1}}$ has order $p$; its cyclic
   subgroup is central and therefore normal.

Repeatedly quotienting by central order-$p$ subgroups builds a central series.
This is one reason finite $p$-groups are nilpotent, though a full theory of
nilpotence lies beyond this first course.

## 10. Consequences for order $p^3$

Let $|P|=p^3$. If $P$ is nonabelian, its center cannot have order $p^2$,
because then $P/Z(P)$ would be cyclic of order $p$ and the cyclic central
quotient lemma would make $P$ abelian. It also cannot have order $p^3$.
Therefore

$|Z(P)|=p$.

The quotient $P/Z(P)$ has order $p^2$ and is abelian. Hence every commutator
lies in the center:

$[P,P]\le Z(P)$.

For noncentral $x$, the centralizer contains both $x$ and $Z(P)$ and has order
at least $p^2$. It is proper, so its order is exactly $p^2$; every noncentral
conjugacy class has size $p$. Thus a nonabelian order-$p^3$ group has class
equation

$p^3=p+r p$

for $r=p^2-1$ noncentral classes. This determines class sizes, not the full
isomorphism type.

## 11. Counting commuting pairs

Let $k(G)$ be the number of conjugacy classes of finite $G$. Count ordered
commuting pairs $(x,g)$ with $xg=gx$.

For fixed $x$, there are $|C_G(x)|$ choices of $g$, so the count is

$\sum_{x \in G}|C_G(x)|$.

Group this sum by conjugacy classes. A class represented by $x$ has
$[G:C_G(x)]$ elements, each with a centralizer of the same size. Its total
contribution is

$[G:C_G(x)] |C_G(x)|=|G|$.

Therefore the number of ordered commuting pairs is

$|G| k(G)$.

The probability that two uniformly chosen group elements commute is
$k(G)/|G|$. This is one quantitative measure of noncommutativity, but equal
probabilities do not imply isomorphic groups.

## 12. Common errors

- A conjugacy class is a subset of $G$; a centralizer is a subgroup of $G$.
- The center consists of elements fixed by every conjugation, not elements
  commuting with at least one other element.
- Class sizes divide $|G|$, but arbitrary divisors need not occur.
- A union of conjugacy classes containing identity is conjugation-stable; it
  still needs subgroup closure before it is a normal subgroup.
- A nontrivial center does not make a group abelian.
- The fixed-point congruence uses a $p$-group acting on a finite set.
- $G/Z(G)$ cyclic forces $G$ abelian; an arbitrary cyclic quotient does not.
- The class equation constrains finite groups but rarely classifies them by
  itself.

## 13. Chapter summary and retrieval prompts

Conjugation turns classes into orbits and centralizers into stabilizers. The
center is the common fixed-point set, and the class equation partitions group
order into central points and centralizer indices. For a finite $p$-group,
nontrivial orbit sizes vanish modulo $p$, forcing nontrivial center. This proves
all order-$p^2$ groups abelian, makes normal subgroups meet the center, and
strongly constrains groups of order $p^3$.

The types and counts are: identity, $1$; transpositions, choose
two moved points, $6$; double transpositions, partition four points into two
pairs, $3$; three-cycles, choose three points and one of two orientations,
$8$; four-cycles, $(4-1)!=6$. Thus
$24=1+6+3+8+6$. These are all partitions
$1+1+1+1$, $2+1+1$, $2+2$, $3+1$, and $4$ of four.

## Summary

Conjugation classes and centralizers give the class equation. In a finite p-group, all nontrivial orbit sizes vanish modulo p, forcing central elements and constraining normal subgroups. Cyclic central quotients force commutativity, but class-size data alone does not classify a group. The Sylow theorems next turn prime-power arithmetic into general finite-group structure.
