
# Cyclic Groups and Element Orders

The subgroup generated by one element is the smallest laboratory in group theory. Its elements are powers of a single reversible move, so group equations become integer arithmetic. This makes cyclic groups completely classifiable and provides the local model for the behavior of individual elements in any group.

The local prerequisite is [Groups, Subgroups, and Generated Structure](groups-subgroups-generated-structure.md). Additive notation is used for $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; multiplicative notation is used for a general group.

## 1. Generated by one element

**Definition 3.1 (Cyclic subgroup).**

For $g \in G$, the **cyclic subgroup generated by $g$** is

$\langle g\rangle=\{g^k:k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.

It is a subgroup: $g^a(g^b)^{-1}=g^{a-b}$. It contains $g$, and every subgroup containing $g$ must contain all positive powers, the inverse, and all negative powers, so $\langle g\rangle$ is the smallest such subgroup.

A group $G$ is **cyclic** when $G=\langle g\rangle$ for some $g$. Then $g$ is a **generator** of $G$. A cyclic group can have several generators.

Every cyclic group is abelian:

$g^a g^b=g^{a+b}=g^{b+a}=g^b g^a$.

The converse fails. For example, in the four-element group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, every nonidentity element has order two, so no one element generates all four elements, even though the group is abelian.

### Examples

- $(\mathbb{Z},+)=\langle 1\rangle=\langle -1\rangle$ is infinite cyclic.
- $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},+)=\langle [1]_n\rangle$ is finite cyclic.
- Rotations of a regular $n$-gon form a cyclic group generated by one step of rotation.
- The $n$th roots of unity form a cyclic group generated by $\exp(2 \pi i/n)$.
- A reflection generates only $\{e,s\}$, not the full dihedral group when the polygon has at least three vertices.

## 2. Order measures when repetition closes

The **order** of an element $g$, written $|g|$ or $\operatorname{ord}(g)$, is the least positive integer $n$ with $g^n=e$, if one exists. If no positive power is the identity, $g$ has infinite order.

The order of $g$ is exactly the number of elements in $\langle g\rangle$.

If $\operatorname{ord}(g)=n$, then

$e,g,g^2,...,g^{n-1}$

are distinct. Otherwise $g^a=g^b$ with $0\le a<b<n$; multiplying by $g^{-a}$ gives $g^{b-a}=e$, contradicting minimality. Every power reduces modulo $n$, so these $n$ elements exhaust the cyclic subgroup.

If no positive power returns to $e$, then all integer powers are distinct: $g^a=g^b$ would imply a positive power is the identity after cancellation. Thus $\langle g\rangle$ is infinite.

**Exponent equality criterion.** If $\operatorname{ord}(g)=n<\infty$, then

$g^a=g^b$ exactly when $n$ divides $a-b$.

One direction follows because powers differing by a multiple of $n$ differ by powers of $g^n=e$. Conversely, divide $a-b$ by $n$: $a-b=qn+r$ with $0\le r<n$. If $g^{a-b}=e$, then $g^r=e$; minimality forces $r=0$.

### Worked computation: powers of a rotation

**Worked example 3.2 (A twelve-gon return time).**

Let $r$ rotate a regular twelve-gon by $30$ degrees. Then $\operatorname{ord}(r)=12$. The element $r^8$ rotates by $240$ degrees. Its successive powers return to the identity when $8k$ is divisible by $12$; the least positive such $k$ is $3$. Thus $\operatorname{ord}(r^8)=3$.

The general formula comes next.

## 3. The order of a power

**Theorem 3.3 (Order of a power).**

If $\operatorname{ord}(g)=n<\infty$, then

$\operatorname{ord}(g^k)=n/\gcd(n,k)$.

Let $d=\gcd(n,k)$, and write $n=dn'$, $k=dk'$ with $\gcd(n',k')=1$. Then

$(g^k)^{n'}=g^{kn'}=g^{dk'n'}=g^{k'n}=e$,

so the order divides $n'$. Conversely, if $(g^k)^m=e$, then $n$ divides $km$, so $dn'$ divides $dk'm$ and $n'$ divides $k'm$. Coprimality forces $n'$ to divide $m$. Therefore no smaller positive exponent works, and the order is $n'$.

Consequences:

- $g^k$ generates $\langle g\rangle$ exactly when $\gcd(n,k)=1$.
- A cyclic group of order $n$ has $\phi(n)$ generators, where Euler's totient $\phi(n)$ counts integers $1\le k\le n$ coprime to $n$.
- If $g$ has infinite order, then $g^k$ has infinite order for every nonzero $k$.

### Worked generator list

**Worked example 3.4 (Generators and gcd).**

In a cyclic group $\langle g\rangle$ of order $12$, the generators are $g^k$ for

$k \in \{1,5,7,11\}$,

the residue classes coprime to $12$. Thus there are $\phi(12)=4$ generators. The element $g^4$ has order $12/\gcd(12,4)=3$; the element $g^6$ has order two.

## 4. All cyclic groups have two models

**Classification of cyclic groups.** Every infinite cyclic group is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z},+)$. Every cyclic group of finite order $n$ is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},+)$.

If $G=\langle g\rangle$ and $g$ has infinite order, define

$\phi:\mathbb{Z}\to G$, $\phi(k)=g^k$.

It is a homomorphism because $g^{a+b}=g^ag^b$, onto because $g$ generates, and one-to-one because distinct powers remain distinct.

If $\operatorname{ord}(g)=n$, define

$\phi:\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\to G$, $\phi([k]_n)=g^k$.

It is well-defined by the exponent equality criterion. It is a homomorphism and onto. If $g^a=g^b$, then $n$ divides $a-b$, so $[a]_n=[b]_n$; hence it is injective.

This classification does not say every group of order $n$ is cyclic. It says every **cyclic** group of that order has the same abstract structure.

## 5. Subgroups of cyclic groups

**Theorem 3.4 (Subgroup).** Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

Let $G=\langle g\rangle$ and let $H\le G$. If $H=\{e\}$, it is generated by $e$. Otherwise consider the positive integers $m$ such that $g^m \in H$. This set is nonempty: if $g^k$ is a nonidentity member, then either $k>0$ or its inverse $g^{-k}$ supplies a positive exponent. Let $d$ be the least positive exponent with $g^d \in H$.

We claim $H=\langle g^d\rangle$. The inclusion $\langle g^d\rangle \subseteq H$ is immediate. For $g^m \in H$, divide $m=qd+r$ with $0\le r<d$. Then

$g^r=g^m(g^d)^{-q}$

lies in $H$. Minimality of $d$ forces $r=0$, so $g^m$ is a power of $g^d$. Thus the reverse inclusion holds.

### Finite cyclic subgroup lattice

If $G=\langle g\rangle$ has order $n$, then for every positive divisor $d$ of $n$, there is exactly one subgroup of order $d$, namely

$\langle g^{n/d}\rangle$.

The generator $g^{n/d}$ has order

$n/\gcd(n,n/d)=d$.

Conversely, any subgroup is $\langle g^k\rangle$ and has order $n/\gcd(n,k)$, a divisor of $n$. A subgroup of a specified order $d$ must consist of precisely the solutions of $x^d=e$ in $G$, or equivalently be $\langle g^{n/d}\rangle$; hence uniqueness.

For $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition, the subgroups are $m\mathbb{Z}$. Inclusion reverses divisibility:

$a\mathbb{Z} \subseteq b\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $b$ divides $a$.

For a finite cyclic group of order $n$, subgroup containment has the same divisor pattern.

### Worked subgroup lattice of order twelve

**Worked example 3.5 (The cyclic divisor lattice).**

Let $G=\langle g\rangle$ have order $12$. Its positive divisors are $1,2,3,4,6,12$. The corresponding unique subgroups are:

| order | subgroup |
| ---: | --- |
| 1 | $\langle g^{12}\rangle=\{e\}$ |
| 2 | $\langle g^6\rangle$ |
| 3 | $\langle g^4\rangle$ |
| 4 | $\langle g^3\rangle$ |
| 6 | $\langle g^2\rangle$ |
| 12 | $\langle g\rangle$ |

For example, $\langle g^4\rangle$ is contained in $\langle g^2\rangle$ because the order-three subgroup corresponds to divisor $3$ of $6$, and directly $g^4=(g^2)^2$.

## 6. Homomorphisms from cyclic groups

A homomorphism out of a cyclic group is determined by the image of one generator. If $G=\langle g\rangle$ and $\phi:G\to H$, then

$\phi(g^k)=\phi(g)^k$.

For infinite cyclic $G$, any choice of $h \in H$ defines a unique homomorphism $g^k \to  h^k$.

For finite cyclic $G$ of order $n$, a choice $h \in H$ defines such a homomorphism exactly when $h^n=e$. The condition is necessary because

$e=\phi(g^n)=h^n$,

and sufficient because it makes the rule independent of the exponent representative modulo $n$.

### Worked count: maps from $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$

An additive homomorphism is determined by the image $a$ of $[1]$. It must satisfy $12a=[0]$ modulo $8$, equivalently $4a=[0]$ modulo $8$. Thus $a$ must be even: $[0],[2],[4],[6]$. There are four homomorphisms.

The image generated by $a$ and the kernel size depend on its order. For $a=[2]$, the image has order four. For $a=[4]$, it has order two.

## 7. Products of cyclic groups

The direct product of two cyclic groups need not be cyclic. If $a$ has order $m$ and $b$ has order $n$, then $(a,b)$ has order

$\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$.

Indeed, $(a,b)^k=(e,e)$ exactly when both $m$ and $n$ divide $k$; the least positive such $k$ is their least common multiple.

Therefore $C_m \times C_n$ is cyclic exactly when $\gcd(m,n)=1$. If coprime, a pair of generators has order $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=mn$, the size of the product, so it generates all. If they share a factor greater than one, every element has order at most $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)<mn$, so none generates the product.

### Worked comparison of order four groups

$C_4$ has an element of order four. In $C_2 \times C_2$, every nonidentity element has order two. Element order is preserved by isomorphism, so these groups are not isomorphic. This is the smallest example showing cardinality alone does not classify groups.

## 8. Element order as an invariant

If $\phi:G\to H$ is an isomorphism, then $\operatorname{ord}(\phi(g))=\operatorname{ord}(g)$. For finite order $n$,

$\phi(g)^n=\phi(g^n)=e$,

so the image order divides $n$. Applying the inverse isomorphism gives the reverse divisibility, hence equality. Infinite order is preserved for the same reason: a finite relation on the image would pull back.

For a general homomorphism, order can decrease but cannot acquire a new incompatible value: if $g$ has finite order $n$, then $\operatorname{ord}(\phi(g))$ divides $n$. The kernel measures how much repetition collapses.

This gives a fast nonisomorphism certificate. If two finite groups have different counts of elements of some order, they cannot be isomorphic. Matching order counts does not necessarily prove isomorphism; an invariant can distinguish without completely classifying.

## 9. Common errors

- The group order $|G|$ and element order $|g|$ are different types of quantity.
- $g^a=g^b$ permits reducing exponents modulo $\operatorname{ord}(g)$, not modulo an unrelated group size unless Lagrange's theorem has already connected them.
- A group with $n$ elements need not be cyclic.
- The order of $g^k$ is not generally $\operatorname{ord}(g)/k$; the gcd formula is required.
- Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic, but a group in which every proper subgroup is cyclic need not be cyclic.
- Writing $g^x=h$ as a numerical logarithm is only an analogy. In a finite cyclic group, finding $x$ is the discrete logarithm problem and may be computationally difficult even though existence is structurally simple.

## 10. Chapter summary and retrieval prompts

A cyclic group is generated by powers of one element and is therefore abelian. Element order is the first return time to identity and equals the size of the generated cyclic subgroup. Finite cyclic groups are modeled by $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, infinite ones by $\mathbb{Z}$. Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic; finite subgroup structure matches divisors. Homomorphisms from cyclic groups are completely determined by a generator image satisfying the source relation.

### ALG-CH03-E020 — Exercise 20: Transfer problem: periodic schedules

A system advances through $n$ states by one reversible step $r$, returning after exactly $n$ steps. Two observation schedules sample every $a$th and every $b$th state. Determine the periods of the sampled schedules, decide when their joint schedule visits every ordered pair of reachable sampled phases, and explain whether it can visit all $n^2$ ordered pairs of the original states.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Compute each sampled period with the gcd formula.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The joint period is their lcm; compare with their product and with n².

</details>

#### ALG-CH03-E020 — Solution

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The $a$-step schedule is generated by $r^a$ and has period $m=n/\gcd(n,a)$; similarly the other has period $k=n/\gcd(n,b)$. Their joint step has period $\operatorname{lcm}(m,k)$. It visits all $mk$ reachable phase pairs exactly when $\operatorname{lcm}(m,k)=mk$, equivalently $\gcd(m,k)=1$. Since $m,k\le n$, visiting all $n^2$ original-state pairs would require $m=k=n$ and a joint orbit of length $n^2$; but then its length is $\operatorname{lcm}(n,n)=n$. Hence all $n^2$ pairs are possible only in the trivial case $n=1$. This is a structural periodicity statement; synchronizing physical clocks would require a separate timing model.


</details>

### Solution framework and rubric

For a proposed generator $g$, list $e,g,...,g^{17}$ and verify all are distinct and $g^{18}=e$; this certifies generation and fixes the multiplication rule by exponent addition modulo $18$. The order inventory is given by $\operatorname{ord}(g^k)=18/\gcd(18,k)$: one element of order 1, one of order 2, two of order 3, two of order 6, six of order 9, and six of order 18. Divisors $1,2,3,6,9,18$ give unique subgroups $\langle g^{18/d}\rangle$ of order $d$; $H_d\subseteq H_e$ exactly when $d\mid e$. This lists every subgroup because a subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic and its order divides 18. Generator exponents are the six residues coprime to $18$: $1,5,7,11,13,17$. A repeated power before step $18$ or a missing element refutes this *candidate generator*, but does not alone refute cyclicity: the identity also fails as a proposed generator of a cyclic group. An exhaustive check that every candidate fails, an inconsistent product, or two distinct subgroups of the same order refutes the claimed cyclic model. The report must distinguish exhaustive finite verification from extrapolation beyond the supplied table.

## Summary

A cyclic group is governed by integer exponents and a first return to identity. The gcd formula determines orders of powers and generators, while least-positive-exponent arguments classify cyclic subgroups. Homomorphisms are fixed by a compatible generator image, and products combine return times by lcm. Cosets next connect these local orders with finite group size.
