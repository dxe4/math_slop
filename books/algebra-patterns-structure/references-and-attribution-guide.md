
# References, Reading Routes, and Attribution Guide

## How to use these references

This bibliography identifies useful comparison texts and mathematical
lineages. The book is written under CC BY-SA 4.0; standard definitions and
theorem statements belong to shared mathematical practice. References are
included for further reading, historical context, alternative proofs, and
broader coverage.

Most textbook bullets below are intentionally short, unversioned reading routes. They do not identify a unique edition or its metadata: titles such as Gallian's, Fraleigh's, and Stewart's have materially different editions and, in some editions, different coauthor credits. Use the bounded citation-audit inventory when an exact edition matters; do not infer edition, year, publisher, pagination, or authorship from these route labels alone.

A theorem name is a retrieval convention, not a complete history. Mathematical
ideas are often the product of long development across languages, institutions,
and communities; a modern statement may differ sharply from an early source.

## First-course comparison routes

- Michael Artin, *Algebra* — structural examples, linear algebra connections,
  groups, rings, and fields.
- David S. Dummit and Richard M. Foote, *Abstract Algebra* — broad reference
  coverage and extensive examples beyond one semester.
- Joseph A. Gallian, *Contemporary Abstract Algebra* — example-rich first-course
  pacing and applications.
- I. N. Herstein, *Topics in Algebra* — a concise classical route with strong
  problem-solving emphasis.
- Thomas W. Hungerford, *Abstract Algebra: An Introduction* — a first-course
  path through groups, rings, and fields.
- John B. Fraleigh, *A First Course in Abstract Algebra* — a traditional
  sequence useful for comparing terminology and exercise scope.

These works use different conventions for rings, homomorphisms, permutation
composition, dihedral notation, and the order of topics. Comparison is most
useful when those conventions are recorded rather than blended.

## Groups, actions, and finite structure

- Joseph J. Rotman, *An Introduction to the Theory of Groups* — continuation
  into deeper group structure and presentations.
- John S. Rose, *A Course on Group Theory* — a proof-focused development of
  finite and general group methods.
- Peter J. Cameron, *Permutation Groups* — a route beyond elementary actions
  into permutation-group structure.

For further study, proceed from actions and Sylow theory to solvable groups,
composition series, representation theory, or computational group theory.
Each direction needs new invariants: characters, normal-series factors, or
algorithmic encodings.

## Rings, fields, and Galois theory

- Keith Conrad, [*Remarks about Euclidean Domains*](https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/ringtheory/euclideanrk.pdf), Theorem 5.22, pp. 12–13 — source for the imported example of a PID with no Euclidean function. This is stronger than showing only that its norm fails as a Euclidean function.

- Serge Lang, *Algebra* — a broad graduate reference whose abstraction level
  is substantially beyond this first course.
- Ian Stewart, *Galois Theory* — a focused continuation from polynomial roots
  to field automorphisms and solvability questions.
- Emil Artin, *Galois Theory* — a concise classical presentation useful for
  comparing the automorphism-first viewpoint.
- Nathan Jacobson, *Basic Algebra I* — a systematic bridge toward graduate
  algebra.

The next steps after Chapter 20 include finite fields and coding, algebraic
number theory, commutative algebra, and representation theory. Galois theory
over infinite extensions, inseparable extensions, and the inverse Galois
problem require separate treatments.

## Computation and exact algebra

Computational algebra references should be used with a representation contract:
state coefficient domains, normal forms, algorithms, and failure modes. A
computer algebra system can verify a multiplication table, Euclidean reduction,
factorization, or Smith form instance. It does not establish a general theorem
unless the code embodies a proved exhaustive argument for the declared scope.

The sibling Math Atlas volume *Finite Algebra by Hand and Program* is intended
to extend operation tables, exact finite-field arithmetic, permutation data,
and inspectable algorithms. The sibling *Morphisms First* continues from the
abelian presentation chapter into modules and categories.

## Historical attribution checklist

Every historical or priority claim should record:

1. a classification as contemporary exposition, secondary history, translated
   primary source, or primary source;
2. verified author, title, edition, publisher or archive, and stable locator;
3. a note explaining whether the citation supports provenance, formulation,
   proof, example, or broader context;
4. a check that the modern terminology is not projected unchanged backward;
5. independent review of culturally broad claims and disputed priority.

Named results requiring particular care include Lagrange's theorem, Cayley's
theorem, the Sylow theorems, Noether's isomorphism framework, Gauss's lemma,
Eisenstein's criterion, the Chinese remainder theorem, Galois theory, and Smith
normal form. Their names are useful handles but incomplete historical accounts.

## Reading-route recommendations

For a second algebra course, combine module theory over PIDs with group
representations and a deeper Galois course. For number theory, retain quotient
rings, finite fields, factorization, and extensions. For computer science,
retain actions, permutations, finite fields, presentations, and exact
algorithms. For geometry or physics, continue into representations and Lie
groups through a dedicated text; finite-group intuition alone is insufficient.

## Citation policy for contributors

Paraphrase sources and cite the job they perform. Do not copy exercise
sequences, distinctive examples, diagrams, or prose merely because a theorem
is standard. Direct quotations require exact locators and license or fair-use
review. When sources disagree, record the disagreement. When provenance is
unknown, label it unknown rather than inventing a clean lineage.
