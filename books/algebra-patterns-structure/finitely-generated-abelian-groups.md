
# Finitely Generated Abelian Groups and Classification

Knowing that a group is abelian is only the beginning. The structure theorem
turns every finitely generated abelian group into a finite list of integers,
and it says that this list determines the group up to isomorphism. The result
unifies cyclic groups, products, quotient presentations, prime-power torsion,
and integer row-and-column reduction.

We use additive notation. The prerequisites are [cyclic groups](cyclic-groups-element-orders.md),
[homomorphisms and quotients](group-homomorphisms-isomorphism-theorems.md),
[products and presentations](products-semidirect-products-presentations.md),
and the Euclidean-domain methods of [divisibility](divisibility-euclidean-pid-ufd.md).

## 1. From generators to integer relations

**Proposition 16.1 (Finite integer presentations).**

If $G$ is generated by $g_1,...,g_n$, the map

$\phi:\mathbb{Z}^n \to  G$,  $(a_1,...,a_n) \to  \sum a_i g_i$

is surjective. The first isomorphism theorem gives $G \cong \mathbb{Z}^n/R$,
where $R=\ker \phi$ is the subgroup of integer relations. Thus classification
means understanding subgroups and quotients of finite-rank free abelian groups.

The relation subgroup is itself finitely generated and free. Prove this by
induction on $n$. Project $R\le\mathbb Z^n$ to the last coordinate. Its image is
$d\mathbb Z$ for some $d\ge0$, while its kernel is a subgroup of
$\mathbb Z^{n-1}$ and hence has a finite free basis by induction. If $d=0$ that
basis already generates $R$. If $d>0$, choose $v\in R$ whose last coordinate
is $d$. Every $r\in R$ has last coordinate $qd$ and therefore
$r-qv$ lies in the kernel. The kernel basis together with $v$ generates $R$
and is independent by inspecting the last coordinate. Thus a finite relation
matrix really does exist; this also proves the subgroup-of-free claim used
later.

A relation matrix records generators of $R$ as columns. Changing the relation
generators performs an invertible integer column operation; changing the group
generators performs an invertible integer row operation. Only operations with
determinant $\pm 1$ are reversible over $\mathbb{Z}$. Dividing a row by two is legal over
$\mathbb Q$ but can change an integer quotient, so ordinary real Gaussian elimination
is not the right algorithm.

## 2. Free rank and torsion

**Definition 16.1 (Torsion subgroup and free rank).**

An element $g$ is torsion if $ng=0$ for some positive integer $n$. The set

$T(G)=\{g \in G : \text{there exists }n\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\text{ with }ng=0\}$

is a subgroup when $G$ is abelian: if $mg=0$ and $nh=0$, then
$mn(g-h)=0$. It is the **torsion subgroup** and is characteristic because
homomorphisms send torsion elements to torsion elements.

For finitely generated $G$, tensoring the presentation informally with $\mathbb Q$
removes finite-order information. Concretely, the maximum size of an
independent infinite-order family is the **free rank** $r$. The classification
will yield $G \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus T(G)$, with $T(G)$ finite. Neither
the complement nor its basis is canonical, but $r$ and the torsion subgroup
are intrinsic.

## 3. The structure theorem: invariant factors

**Theorem 16.2 (Classification by invariant factors).**

Every finitely generated abelian group has an isomorphism

$G \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_1 \mathbb{Z} \oplus ... \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_t \mathbb{Z}$,

where $r\ge 0$, each $d_i>1$, and

$d_1 | d_2 | ... | d_t$.

The integers $r,d_1,...,d_t$ are unique. They are the **invariant factors**.
Factors equal to one are omitted because $\mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z}$ is trivial. A finite group has
$r=0$; a torsion-free finitely generated abelian group has $t=0$ and is free.

The order of a finite group is $\prod d_i$, but order alone does not classify:
$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ both have four elements and different
element orders.

## 4. Elementary divisors and primary components

**Definition 16.2 (Primary components and elementary divisors).**

Factor every invariant factor into prime powers, then use the Chinese
remainder theorem:

$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \bigoplus_{p^a \parallel  n} \mathbb{Z}/p^a \mathbb{Z}$.

This gives the **elementary-divisor form**

$G \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \bigoplus \mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i} \mathbb{Z}$.

For a fixed prime $p$, the elements killed by some power of $p$ form the
$p$-primary component $G_{p}$. A finite abelian group is the direct sum of its
primary components, and homomorphisms preserve them. The multiset of prime
powers is unique and contains the same information as the invariant factors.

The elementary coprime step needed here does not depend on a later ring theorem. If $\gcd(m,n)=1$, reduction gives an additive homomorphism $\mathbb Z/(mn)\to\mathbb Z/m\oplus\mathbb Z/n$. A class in its kernel is divisible by both $m$ and $n$, hence by $mn$, so the map is injective. Domain and codomain each have $mn$ elements, making it surjective as well. This proves the cyclic CRT isomorphism used for the classification directly from divisibility and finite counting.

To return to invariant factors, align the prime-power lists at their largest
ends and multiply down columns. For example,

$\mathbb{Z}/4 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/9$

has $2$-lists $2,4$ and $3$-list $9$; right alignment gives invariant factors
$2,36$, so the group is $\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/36$.

## 5. Smith normal form

**Theorem 16.3 (Integer Smith reduction).**

For an $n\times m$ integer matrix $A$, there exist $U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z)$ and $V\in GL_m(\mathbb Z)$ such that

$UAV=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,...,s_k,0,...,0)$, with $s_1|...|s_k$ and $s_i>0$.

This is **Smith normal form**. Unimodular means determinant $\pm 1$, precisely
the condition for an inverse with integer entries. The permitted operations
are row or column swaps, sign changes, and adding an integer multiple of one
row or column to another.

Here is the reduction argument, including termination. If $A$ is zero there
is nothing to do. Otherwise move a nonzero entry to the upper-left corner and
make it positive, say $a$. Divide each other entry in the first column by $a$
and subtract the corresponding multiple of the first row. If a nonzero
remainder occurs, swap its row into the first row; the new positive corner is
strictly smaller. Do the analogous column divisions in the first row. Repeat
these operations whenever a remainder appears. A positive corner cannot
strictly decrease indefinitely, so eventually $a$ divides every entry of its
row and column. Clear those entries by row and column subtraction, leaving
$a$ and a lower-right block $B$.

If an entry $b_{ij}$ of $B$ is not divisible by $a$, add row $i$ to the first
row. The first row now contains $a$ and $b_{ij}$; dividing $b_{ij}$ by $a$
and subtracting a multiple of the first column from column $j$ leaves a
nonzero remainder in the first row. Swapping column $j$ with the first column
makes a new positive corner strictly smaller than $a$. Run the clearing
procedure again. Strict descent shows that this outer loop terminates too.
At termination $a$ divides every entry of $B$. Apply induction to $B$:
integer row and column operations within $B$ preserve divisibility of all
its entries by $a$, so each later diagonal entry is a multiple of $a$.
Induction also gives divisibility among the later entries. Thus the entire
diagonal has the required chain, without a separate adjustment.

For example, $\begin{pmatrix}2&6\\4&8\end{pmatrix}$ reduces by $R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1$, $C_2\leftarrow C_2-3C_1$, then $R_2\leftarrow-R_2$ to $\operatorname{diag}(2,4)$. The diagonal $\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$ shows why the block test matters: its first pivot does not divide $3$, and the reduction continues to $\operatorname{diag}(1,6)$.

If $G=\mathbb{Z}^n/\operatorname{im}(A)$ and the nonzero Smith entries are $s_1,...,s_k$, then

$G \cong \mathbb{Z}^{n-k} \oplus \bigoplus_i \mathbb{Z}/s_i \mathbb{Z}$,

with unit factors removed. Right multiplication by $V$ preserves $\operatorname{im}(A)$ because $V$ is an automorphism of $\mathbb Z^m$; left multiplication by $U$ induces the isomorphism $x+\operatorname{im}(A)\mapsto Ux+\operatorname{im}(UAV)$. This proves existence in the structure theorem.

## 6. Why the invariants are unique

The free rank is unique because $G/nG$ grows like $n^r$ up to a bounded
torsion factor, or equivalently because rationalizing a presentation gives an
$r$-dimensional vector space.

For torsion, fix a prime $p$. The numbers

$b_k=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\left(p^{k-1}G_{p}/p^k G_{p}\right)$

count how many cyclic $p$-power summands have exponent at least $k$. Therefore
$b_k-b_{k+1}$ counts summands of exact order $p^k$. These dimensions are
defined intrinsically from $G$, so the elementary divisors and hence invariant
factors are unique.

This uniqueness proof also gives a practical isomorphism test: compare free
rank and, prime by prime, the cyclic prime-power summands.

## 7. Reading presentations exactly

**Worked example 16.1 (A full-rank integer presentation).**

Consider generators $x,y$ with relations $2x+4y=0$ and $6x+8y=0$. The relation
matrix has columns $(2,4)$ and $(6,8)$, with determinant $-8$. Its entries have
gcd two, so the first Smith entry is $2$; the product of the nonzero Smith
entries is $8$, hence the second is $4$. Therefore the presented group is
$\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4$.

More generally, for a full-rank square relation matrix, the finite group order
is $|det A|$. That determinant does not determine the group: Smith divisibility
data is still needed. For rank-deficient matrices, each missing pivot creates
one free $\mathbb{Z}$ summand.

## 8. Consequences and limitations

For a nontrivial finite abelian group, the exponent is its largest invariant factor $d_t$; the trivial group has exponent $1$. For a general finitely generated abelian group with free rank $r$ and $t$ nontrivial invariant factors, the minimal number of generators is $r+t$, so it is cyclic exactly when $r+t\le1$.

Every subgroup and quotient of a finitely generated abelian group is again
finitely generated abelian. A subgroup of a free abelian group is free, but a
quotient of one can have torsion: $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$ by $2\mathbb{Z}$.
Finiteness of the generating set is essential. The additive group $\mathbb Q$ is
torsion-free but not free abelian, and an infinite direct sum of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not
covered by the finite classification list.

## 9. Common errors

- Row reduction over $\mathbb Q$ forgets integer index and torsion.
- The order of a finite group is not a complete invariant.
- $\mathbb{Z}/m \oplus \mathbb{Z}/n$ is cyclic exactly when $\gcd(m,n)=1$.
- Elementary divisors are prime powers; invariant factors form a divisibility chain.
- A free part is not the same thing as a set of all infinite-order elements.
- The theorem classifies finitely generated **abelian** groups, not arbitrary groups.

## Looking forward

Classification is powerful because it names exactly which information is
complete: free rank and prime-power torsion. The final chapter places this
theorem beside group, ring, and field results to practice transferring proof
patterns without transferring hypotheses that do not survive.

## Classification certificates and uniqueness in full

The classification proof uses integer coordinates, so one should distinguish three objects: a generator list, the subgroup of relations, and the quotient group. Unimodular operations change the first two descriptions reversibly. An operation with determinant of absolute value greater than one can change a lattice index and therefore change the quotient. The following examples make both the isomorphism and the limitations visible.

**Worked example 16.2 (A rectangular presentation and an explicit isomorphism).** Consider
$$
G=\mathbb Z^3/\langle(2,4,0),(0,6,0)\rangle.
$$
The unimodular coordinate change $U(x,y,z)=(x,y-2x,z)$ has inverse $(u,v,w)\mapsto(u,v+2u,w)$. It sends the two relation vectors to $(2,0,0)$ and $(0,6,0)$. Hence its induced quotient map gives
$$
G\cong C_2\oplus C_6\oplus\mathbb Z.
$$
Explicitly, send the class of $(x,y,z)$ to $([x]_2,[y-2x]_6,z)$. Adding either original relation leaves this tuple unchanged. For the inverse choose representatives $u,v$ of the two finite classes and send the tuple to the class of $(u,v+2u,w)$. Changing $u$ by two adds $(2,4,0)$, and changing $v$ by six adds $(0,6,0)$, so this inverse is well-defined. Substitution in both directions gives the identity.

The torsion subgroup has order twelve and exponent six, while the whole group is infinite because of the last coordinate. These conclusions are read from the certified quotient, not from the determinant of a nonsquare matrix. The example also shows why rational rank only identifies the free rank: it would retain one free coordinate but lose the two finite factors.

**Worked example 16.3 (Convert and distinguish primary classifications).** Compare $G=C_8\oplus C_9\oplus C_6$ with $H=C_{12}\oplus C_{36}$. Splitting into primary cyclic factors gives
$$
G\cong C_2\oplus C_8\oplus C_3\oplus C_9.
$$
Right-aligning the two prime-power lists $(2,8)$ and $(3,9)$ yields invariant factors $6,72$. Thus $|G|=432$ and its exponent is seventy-two. For $H$, the primary lists are $(4,4)$ and $(3,9)$, giving invariant factors $12,36$. Its order is also $432$, but its exponent is thirty-six. The groups are not isomorphic.

Every conversion here is reversible through CRT because powers of different primes are coprime. Powers of the same prime cannot simply be multiplied into one cyclic factor: $C_2\oplus C_8$ has no element of order sixteen. Comparing group orders alone would miss exactly that restriction. The exponent distinguishes this pair quickly, while the full invariant-factor lists provide the complete certificate.

**Proposition 16.4 (Intrinsic recovery of the free rank).** The integer $r$ in the classification is determined by $G$.

*Proof.* The torsion subgroup is characteristic, as established above, and the existence theorem identifies the canonical quotient $G/T(G)$ with $\mathbb Z^r$. Quotient that group by its subgroup of doubles. The resulting group is an $\mathbb F_2$-vector space of dimension $r$, since each coordinate of $\mathbb Z^r/2\mathbb Z^r$ has exactly two values. Both quotient operations are defined intrinsically. Consequently any group isomorphism induces an isomorphism of these vector spaces and preserves $r$. This proves uniqueness without relying on an informal tensor operation. ∎

The torsion-layer calculation likewise has a direct justification. On a cyclic summand $C_{p^a}$, the quotient $p^{k-1}C_{p^a}/p^kC_{p^a}$ has order $p$ if $k\le a$ and is zero if $k>a$. Multiplication by $p$ kills the quotient, making it an $\mathbb F_p$-vector space. Direct sums preserve these quotients, so its dimension is the number of summands with exponent at least $k$. Taking consecutive differences recovers the exact number of summands of each order $p^k$. The subgroups $p^kG_p$ and their quotient dimensions are intrinsic, so this proves uniqueness of the elementary divisors as well as their computational recovery.

**Proposition 16.5 (Minimum number of generators).** If the nontrivial invariant factors are $d_1|\cdots|d_t$, the minimum number of generators of $\mathbb Z^r\oplus C_{d_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{d_t}$ is $r+t$.

*Proof.* The displayed summands provide that many generators. If $t>0$, choose a prime $p$ dividing $d_1$. Divisibility makes $p$ divide every $d_i$. The quotient $G/pG$ is therefore an $\mathbb F_p$-vector space of dimension $r+t$: each free summand and each finite summand contributes one dimension. A generating list of $G$ maps to a spanning list in this quotient and so has at least $r+t$ entries. If $t=0$, use any prime and the same argument on the free group. The trivial group needs zero generators. ∎

Finally finite generation is an actual boundary. The additive group $\mathbb Q$ is not finitely generated: a finite list of rationals has a common denominator $D$, and all their integer combinations lie in $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$, which omits $1/(2D)$. It is not free abelian either. In any nonzero free abelian group, a basis element cannot be twice another group element, because its coordinate one would have to be twice an integer. Every rational, however, is twice another rational. Thus torsion-free does not imply free without the finite-generation hypothesis.

These arguments complete the original classification statements using integer presentations, elementary quotient vector spaces and divisibility. General modules over arbitrary rings require additional hypotheses and belong to the later algebra route.

## Summary

Finite integer presentations, unimodular reduction and Smith form give existence of the classification. The torsion subgroup, free rank and prime-layer dimensions give uniqueness. Invariant factors and elementary divisors encode the same isomorphism data; finite generation and commutativity remain essential.
