
# Affine Varieties and the Zariski Topology

Polynomial equations define sets, but their geometry becomes clearer when we remember every polynomial that vanishes on the set. This chapter establishes the resulting dictionary, proves the topology and decomposition statements, and distinguishes an ordinary zero set from the extra algebra carried by nonradical equations.

Throughout, $k$ is an algebraically closed field of arbitrary characteristic, and affine space means $\mathbb A^n(k)=k^n$ with finitely many coordinates. All rings are commutative with identity. Read Commutative Algebra on [radicals](../commutative-algebra/rings-and-ideals.md), [Noetherian polynomial rings](../commutative-algebra/noetherian-hilbert-basis.md) and [the Nullstellensatz](../commutative-algebra/nullstellensatz.md). The latter already proves Zariski's lemma and both Nullstellensatz forms; we use those proved preparation results rather than importing their conclusions without a source. No complex topology is used.

We call an arbitrary closed zero set an **affine algebraic set**. A **variety** here is nonempty and irreducible; a reducible algebraic set will not silently be called a variety. Its coordinate ring is reduced by definition. Scheme chapters later allow more rings and points, while preserving this classical dictionary under its stated hypotheses.

## 1. Equations and vanishing ideals reverse containment

Let $R=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$. For a subset $S$ of $R$, define $V(S)$ to be the set of points where every member of $S$ vanishes. It depends only on the ideal generated by $S$: a polynomial combination of vanishing equations also vanishes. For a subset $T$ of affine space, let $I(T)$ be the ideal of polynomials vanishing at every point of $T$. Closure under addition and multiplication by an arbitrary polynomial follows by evaluation. It is radical, because a field element whose positive power is zero is itself zero.

If $S\subseteq S'$, then $V(S')\subseteq V(S)$. If $T\subseteq T'$, then $I(T')\subseteq I(T)$. The two universal containments are $S\subseteq I(V(S))$ and $T\subseteq V(I(T))$. These elementary statements express an order-reversing relation even over fields that are not algebraically closed; the stronger inverse statement needs the preparation theorem.

The strong Nullstellensatz states $I(V(J))=\sqrt J$ for every ideal $J$ of $R$. Consequently radical ideals correspond bijectively to affine algebraic subsets, via $J\mapsto V(J)$ and $X\mapsto I(X)$. To check the other composite directly, if $X=V(J)$ then $J\subseteq I(X)$ gives $V(I(X))\subseteq X$, and the universal containment gives equality. Thus the dictionary is actually invertible, rather than merely a useful assignment.

The empty set corresponds to the unit ideal, because every polynomial vanishes vacuously there. The whole affine space corresponds to the zero ideal. For the latter assertion the Nullstellensatz gives $I(V(0))=\sqrt0=0$, since a polynomial ring over a field is a domain. In zero coordinates, affine space is a single point and its ring is $k$; these endpoint conventions still agree.

## 2. Verify the closed-set axioms

The sets $V(J)$ are the closed sets of the Zariski topology. The empty and whole sets have just been identified. For any family of ideals, the intersection of their zero sets is the zero set of their sum: to vanish on every equation in every family member is exactly to vanish on all finite polynomial combinations of those equations.

For two ideals $J,K$, one has

$$V(J)\cup V(K)=V(JK)=V(J\cap K).$$

A point in either zero set kills every product, proving one containment for $JK$. If a point lies in neither, choose $f\in J$ and $g\in K$ with both values nonzero; their product is nonzero in the field, so the point does not lie in $V(JK)$. This proves the reverse containment. Since $JK\subseteq J\cap K$, and a point outside both also has the nonzero product in the intersection, the third expression agrees. The proof uses the field property at the point; it does not equate the three ideals themselves.

On a closed algebraic subset $X$, closed sets in the induced topology are $X\cap V(J)$. Its coordinate ring is $A=k[X]=R/I(X)$. Ideals in $A$ are preimages of ideals containing $I(X)$, and the same zero-set dictionary holds relative to $X$. In particular every closed subset has the form $V_X(J)$ for an ideal of $A$, and its vanishing ideal in $A$ is radical.

For $f\in A$, write $D_X(f)=\{a\in X:f(a)\ne0\}$. These principal open sets form a basis: the complement of $V_X(J)$ is the union of the $D_X(f)$ for $f\in J$. Their intersections satisfy $D_X(f)\cap D_X(g)=D_X(fg)$. They need not be closed. The notation concerns a function on $X$, so choosing a different polynomial representative modulo $I(X)$ does not change the open set.

## 3. Irreducibility is a prime-ideal condition

A nonempty topological space is irreducible when it is not a union of two proper closed subsets. Equivalently, any two nonempty opens intersect: take complements in the two definitions. For a nonempty affine algebraic set $X$,

$$X\text{ is irreducible}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I(X)\text{ is prime}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k[X]\text{ is a domain}.$$

If $fg$ vanishes on $X$, then $X$ is the union of its intersections with $V(f)$ and $V(g)$. Irreducibility forces one intersection to be all of $X$, making $f$ or $g$ lie in $I(X)$. This proves primality, including properness because the set is nonempty. Conversely, if $X=Y\cup Z$ with both closed and proper, choose $f$ vanishing on $Y$ but not all of $X$, and $g$ vanishing on $Z$ but not all of $X$. Such choices exist from their defining ideals. Then $fg$ vanishes on $X$ but neither factor does, contradicting primality.

Every nonempty open subset of an irreducible space is dense and irreducible. For density, a nonempty open disjoint from its closure would contradict the open-intersection characterization. For irreducibility of an open $U$, two nonempty relative opens in $U$ are also opens of the whole space and therefore intersect. These proofs are topological, so apply to all the varieties considered here without repeating coordinate calculations.

A finite union of proper closed subsets cannot cover an irreducible space: induct on the number of subsets using the two-set definition. This finite form will prove uniqueness of irreducible components. It does not assert that arbitrary unions of proper closed subsets are smaller: an algebraic curve is the union of its closed points as a set.

## 4. Finiteness from Hilbert's theorem

Hilbert's basis theorem makes $R$ Noetherian, and its quotient $A$ is Noetherian. A descending chain of closed subsets of $X$ gives an ascending chain of radical ideals in $A$ under the inverse dictionary. It therefore stabilizes. This is the Noetherian topological property.

Every subspace of a Noetherian topological space is Noetherian. To prove this using open chains, write an increasing sequence of relative opens as intersections of the subspace with opens $U_i$ of the ambient space. Replace $U_i$ by the increasing unions of the first $i$ such opens. Those ambient unions stabilize, so their intersections stabilize. This also proves the corresponding descending-closed condition by complements.

Every open subset of a Noetherian space is quasi-compact, meaning that each open cover has a finite subcover. If no finite subcover existed, start with one cover member and repeatedly choose another containing a point not yet covered. The finite unions would be a strictly increasing chain of opens, contradiction. Quasi-compactness makes no Hausdorff assertion. In particular an affine variety can have very different separation behavior from a familiar compact metric space.

Every closed subset is a finite union of irreducible closed subsets. Otherwise choose a minimal closed counterexample using the descending-chain condition. It cannot itself be irreducible, so it is a union of two proper closed subsets. Minimality makes both finite unions of irreducibles, contradicting that it was a counterexample. Remove pieces contained in other pieces to obtain the maximal irreducible closed subsets, called components.

The resulting components are unique up to order. Given two finite irredundant decompositions, each irreducible component in the first is contained in one component of the second, by the finite-union property. That component is contained in one of the first. Maximality forces equality, so every member occurs in both lists. Algebraically these components correspond to the minimal prime ideals over the vanishing ideal. The correspondence reverses inclusions, converting maximal irreducible subsets into minimal containing primes.

## 5. Worked example: two axes and their components

In the affine plane, $X=V(xy)$ is the union of the two axes. Its vanishing ideal is $(xy)=(x)\cap(y)$. To prove this directly, a polynomial vanishing on the vertical axis has $f(0,y)=0$ for every $y\in k$. The field is infinite, so the one-variable root bound makes that polynomial identically zero and $x$ divides $f$. The same argument on the horizontal axis makes $y$ divide it. Dividing by $x$ and then setting $y=0$ shows that $xy$ divides it. The reverse containment follows by evaluation.

Each axis is irreducible because its coordinate ring is the domain $k[t]$. Neither is contained in the other, so these are exactly the two irreducible components. The full coordinate ring has nonzero zero divisors, the classes of $x$ and $y$, whose product is zero. It is nevertheless reduced because $(xy)$ is radical. Reduced and irreducible are different properties.

## 6. Worked example: a cusp in every characteristic

Let $C=V(y^2-x^3)$ and map $k[x,y]$ to $k[t]$ by $x\mapsto t^2,y\mapsto t^3$. Its kernel is exactly $(y^2-x^3)$. Divide any polynomial by this monic polynomial in $y$ to obtain a remainder $A(x)+yB(x)$. After substitution it becomes $A(t^2)+t^3B(t^2)$. The first summand has only even powers and the second only odd powers, so their sum is zero only when both polynomials vanish. This proves the kernel assertion without using an unproved irreducibility criterion.

The quotient is the subring $k[t^2,t^3]$ of a domain, so the ideal is prime and radical. The Nullstellensatz identifies it with the vanishing ideal of $C$, which is therefore irreducible. Every point has the form $(t^2,t^3)$: if $x=0$ the equation forces $y=0$; otherwise take $t=y/x$ and verify $t^2=x,t^3=y$. All these calculations work in arbitrary characteristic. The existence of a polynomial parametrisation does not yet prove an isomorphism of varieties; the inverse at the origin needs the regular-function analysis of the next chapter.

## 7. Worked example: projection need not preserve closedness

The hyperbola $H=V(xy-1)$ is closed in the affine plane. Its projection to the first coordinate has image $k\setminus\{0\}$: a nonzero first coordinate has the unique partner $1/x$, while zero has none. On the affine line every proper closed subset is finite. Indeed it is contained in the zero set of one nonzero polynomial, which has at most its degree many roots, and every finite set is the zero set of a product of its linear factors.

Because $k$ is infinite, the image is infinite and proper, so it is not closed. Projection is nevertheless continuous: pulling back a polynomial equation in the first coordinate gives the same equation in the plane. Thus continuity preserves inverse images of closed sets, not arbitrary direct images. Later projective properness statements require extra hypotheses precisely because the affine example already fails.

On the affine line the complement of any finite set is open, giving the cofinite topology. Two nonempty opens always meet, so it is irreducible. Distinct points cannot be separated by disjoint nonempty opens, so it is not Hausdorff. Closedness of every point is compatible with that failure. These observations concern the Zariski topology, even when $k$ happens to be the complex numbers.

## 8. What zero sets forget

The equations $y=0$ and $y^2=0$ give the same ordinary points. The first coordinate ring is $k[x]$, while the second quotient $k[x,y]/(y^2)$ has a nonzero square-zero class of $y$. The latter is not the coordinate ring of the classical algebraic set under our definition: taking its vanishing ideal removes that nilpotent. The later scheme viewpoint retains the thicker ring instead. This explains why radicalization is intrinsic to the classical dictionary rather than a harmless change of presentation in every geometry.

Algebraic closure is also substantive. Over the reals, the ideal $(x^2+1)$ is proper and radical but has no real zeros, whose vanishing ideal is the whole ring. Over a finite field of size $q$, the nonzero polynomial $x^q-x$ vanishes on every field point. Thus even the vanishing ideal of the full affine line need not be zero in that setting. Our hypotheses prevent these failures; changing the ground field requires changing the statement, not just its notation.

## 9. Classical points correspond to maximal ideals

For a point $a$ of $X$, evaluation $k[X]\to k$ is onto because it fixes constants. Its kernel is therefore maximal. Conversely, every maximal ideal in the finite-type coordinate algebra has residue field $k$ by the proved weak Nullstellensatz. The residues of the coordinate generators give a unique point of $X$, recovering that maximal ideal as its evaluation kernel. Thus the points of $X$ are exactly its maximal ideals, with their coordinate values retained.

The bijection respects topology: a closed zero set of an ideal consists precisely of the evaluation kernels containing that ideal. It is therefore a homeomorphism onto the maximal-ideal subset equipped with these ideal-containment closed sets. The full prime spectrum has additional points in general. For example the zero prime of $k[x]$ is not a maximal ideal and is not a classical coordinate point of the line.

Every classical point is closed, because its coordinate linear equations cut out that singleton. An irreducible variety with more than one point consequently has no classical point whose closure is the whole variety. Its irreducible component is a closed set with a prime vanishing ideal, but that prime need not itself be a coordinate point. The scheme route will retain such a prime as a generic point. This is an explicit change of the point set and topology, not a renaming of one of the existing classical points. The maximal-ideal correspondence here fixes exactly which part of that later dictionary has already been proved.

## Exercises

### AG01-01. A finite union of zero sets

Prove $V(JK)=V(J)\cup V(K)$ and explain why the statement does not imply $JK=J\cap K$.

**Hint 1.** At a point outside both zero sets choose one nonvanishing member of each ideal.

**Hint 2.** Try the same principal ideal for both factors in one variable.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

A point in either zero set kills every product. Outside both, some $f\in J,g\in K$ have nonzero values, so $fg$ excludes that point from $V(JK)$. Yet with $J=K=(x)$, the product is $(x^2)$ while the intersection is $(x)$. Zero sets identify their radicals, not their full nonradical ideal structures. This example also records exactly the multiplicity information ordinary points discard.

</details>

### AG01-02. Close an arbitrary subset

Show that the closure of $T\subseteq\mathbb A^n(k)$ is $V(I(T))$.

**Hint 1.** This closed set contains $T$ by definition.

**Hint 2.** Any closed set containing $T$ has defining equations in $I(T)$.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Every point of $T$ kills its vanishing ideal, so the displayed closed set contains it. If $T\subseteq V(J)$, then $J\subseteq I(T)$ and containment reversal gives $V(I(T))\subseteq V(J)$. Hence it lies in every closed set containing $T$, proving it is exactly the closure. This proof does not require choosing finitely many points or taking a metric limit.

</details>

### AG01-03. A dense principal open

For irreducible $X$ and nonzero $f\in k[X]$, prove $D_X(f)$ is nonempty and dense.

**Hint 1.** A function zero at every point is the zero coordinate-ring class.

**Hint 2.** Every nonempty open in an irreducible space is dense.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If the principal open were empty, $f$ would vanish on all points, placing its representative in $I(X)$ and making its coordinate class zero. Thus it is nonempty. If its closure were proper, the complementary nonempty open would be disjoint from it, contradicting irreducibility. The argument uses the reduced coordinate-ring definition and the nonzero condition explicitly.

</details>

### AG01-04. A reducible zero set

Find the irreducible components of $V(xy(x-y))\subseteq\mathbb A^2(k)$.

**Hint 1.** Factor the defining polynomial into its three linear factors.

**Hint 2.** Each line has a polynomial-ring coordinate ring in one variable.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The zero set is the union of the lines $x=0,y=0,x=y$. Each is irreducible and no one is contained in another; they are therefore the components by uniqueness of the irredundant finite decomposition. The three linear forms define distinct lines even in characteristic two. Their product is a squarefree polynomial, but the component conclusion already follows from the proved topological argument and the domain coordinate rings.

</details>

### AG01-05. Reduced need not mean a domain

Show $k[x,y]/(xy)$ is reduced but not a domain.

**Hint 1.** Its defining ideal is the intersection of the two prime ideals $(x)$ and $(y)$.

**Hint 2.** The coordinate classes are nonzero but multiply to zero.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

An intersection of radical ideals is radical: if a power lies in both, the element lies in both. Thus $(xy)=(x)\cap(y)$ is radical and the quotient is reduced. Neither $x$ nor $y$ belongs to $(xy)$, but their product does, proving failure of the domain property. Geometrically the first property discards nilpotent thickness, while the second would require irreducibility.

</details>

### AG01-06. The cusp's coordinate kernel

Prove the kernel of $x\mapsto t^2,y\mapsto t^3$ using monic division in $y$.

**Hint 1.** The remainder has the form $A(x)+yB(x)$.

**Hint 2.** After substitution the two parts have disjoint exponent parity.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Division gives a multiple of $y^2-x^3$ plus that remainder. Its image is $A(t^2)+t^3B(t^2)$. Even powers cannot cancel odd powers in a polynomial ring, so both parts vanish coefficientwise. Substitution into $t^2$ is injective on $k[x]$, forcing $A=B=0$. Thus every kernel element is in the displayed principal ideal, and the reverse containment is immediate. No characteristic restriction entered the parity of integer exponents.

</details>

### AG01-07. A nonclosed image

Determine the image of $V(xy-1)$ under projection and prove it is not closed.

**Hint 1.** A first coordinate has a partner exactly when it is nonzero.

**Hint 2.** Proper closed subsets of the affine line are finite.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The image is $k\setminus\{0\}$. It is proper, and it is infinite because an algebraically closed field is infinite: if a field were finite, the polynomial product of $X-a$ over all its elements, plus one, would have no root. An infinite proper subset of the line is not closed by the root bound. This proves failure of closed direct images despite continuity of projection.

</details>

### AG01-08. A Noetherian open cover

Prove every open subset of $X$ is quasi-compact directly from stabilization of increasing opens.

**Hint 1.** Suppose a cover has no finite subcover.

**Hint 2.** Repeatedly choose a cover member meeting a point missed by the previous finite union.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Starting with one cover member, the assumed failure allows a new uncovered point and then a member containing it. Finite unions therefore form a strictly increasing sequence of opens in the subspace. The subspace is Noetherian by the proved hereditary property, so such a sequence cannot exist. Hence a finite subcover exists. No separation axiom or metric argument is part of this proof.

</details>

### AG01-09. Components and minimal primes

Explain why an irredundant finite irreducible decomposition corresponds to the minimal primes above the vanishing ideal.

**Hint 1.** Irreducible closed subsets correspond to containing primes.

**Hint 2.** The correspondence reverses inclusion.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

A maximal irreducible closed subset gives a containing prime with no smaller containing prime, because a smaller prime would define a larger irreducible subset. Conversely a minimal containing prime defines an irreducible subset not properly contained in another such subset. Thus it is a component. The finite decomposition and its uniqueness show that every such prime occurs and none of its corresponding subsets is redundant.

</details>

### AG01-10. Points are closed, but the line is not Hausdorff

Prove both assertions for the affine line.

**Hint 1.** A point is cut out by its linear equation.

**Hint 2.** Complements of finite sets are the nonempty opens.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The singleton $\{a\}=V(x-a)$ is closed. Every proper closed subset is finite, so every nonempty open has finite complement. In the infinite field two such opens intersect. Distinct points consequently cannot have disjoint open neighborhoods, and the line is not Hausdorff. Closed singletons are a weaker property and do not imply that separation axiom.

</details>

### AG01-11. Two equations, one set

Compare $V(y)$ and $V(y^2)$ and their polynomial quotients.

**Hint 1.** A field element with square zero is zero.

**Hint 2.** The class of $y$ in the second quotient is nonzero and nilpotent.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Both point sets are the horizontal line, with vanishing ideal $(y)$. The quotient by $(y)$ is reduced, while the quotient by $(y^2)$ contains a nonzero square-zero element. These rings cannot be isomorphic because a ring isomorphism preserves nilpotence. Classical coordinate rings use the vanishing ideal, so they identify the line's reduced geometry; retaining the second ring requires the later scheme structure.

</details>

### AG01-12. A ground-field boundary

Over the reals, compare $(x^2+1)$ with the ideal of its real zero set. Explain which theorem hypothesis failed.

**Hint 1.** The real zero set is empty.

**Hint 2.** The polynomial is irreducible over the reals, so its ideal is proper and prime.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

There are no real zeros because a real square is nonnegative. The vanishing ideal of the empty set is the unit ideal, whereas $(x^2+1)$ is proper and radical, being the kernel of the quotient field $\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+1)$. Thus the inverse radical-ideal dictionary fails for real points. The ground field was not algebraically closed; the ordinary Nullstellensatz has not made the claimed assertion in that setting.

</details>

## Closing summary

Vanishing ideals and zero sets give an inverse radical dictionary over an algebraically closed field. The topology follows from finite products and arbitrary sums of equations; primality detects irreducibility, and Noetherianity gives finite unique components and quasi-compact opens. The examples distinguish reducedness, irreducibility, direct-image closedness and nilpotent thickness. The next chapter develops maps and regular functions so that the dictionary includes morphisms, not just objects.
