
# Projective Varieties

Projective coordinates represent a line by many proportional vectors. Homogeneous equations respect that equivalence, affine charts recover local coordinates, and a finite-dimensional rank argument proves the closed-image property that affine projection lacks. This chapter develops those constructions algebraically over the same algebraically closed field, in arbitrary characteristic.

Read the first two chapters on radical ideals, regular functions and affine morphisms. We also use finite-dimensional matrix rank and the prepared Hilbert basis and Nullstellensatz theorems. Projective means a closed algebraic subset of projective space; the full scheme definition of properness will come later. Every classical variety here remains nonempty, irreducible and reduced.

## 1. Homogeneous coordinates and equations

Define $\mathbb P^n(k)$ as nonzero vectors in $k^{n+1}$ modulo multiplication by a nonzero scalar. Write a class as $[x_0:\cdots:x_n]$. If a homogeneous polynomial $F$ has degree $d$, then $F(\lambda x)=\lambda^dF(x)$, so its zero condition is independent of representative. A general polynomial need not have that property. Closed sets are common zeros of homogeneous polynomials; arbitrary intersections and finite unions follow by the same sum/product argument as in affine space, using homogeneous generators.

Let $S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$ with its degree grading, and let $\mathfrak m=(x_0,\ldots,x_n)$. A homogeneous ideal is generated by homogeneous elements, equivalently it contains every homogeneous component of each of its members. Hilbert's theorem gives finitely many homogeneous generators. For such an ideal $J$, the affine zero set is a cone, stable under scalar multiplication and containing the origin if $J$ is proper. Its nonzero points modulo scaling give the projective zero set $V_+(J)$.

The projective empty-set criterion is

$$V_+(J)=\varnothing\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathfrak m^N\subseteq J\text{ for some }N\ge1.$$

If the projective set is empty, the affine zero set is contained in the origin. The affine Nullstellensatz makes every $x_i$ belong to $\sqrt J$, including the case of a unit ideal. Choose exponents $e_i$ with $x_i^{e_i}\in J$. Every monomial of degree greater than $\sum_i(e_i-1)$ has one of those factors, so the entire graded piece at that degree, and every higher one, lies in $J$. Conversely such a power containment forces every coordinate of a zero vector to vanish, leaving no projective point. This proves the criterion and the required uniform exponent explicitly.

For a nonempty projective closed set $X$, its homogeneous vanishing ideal consists of homogeneous equations vanishing there and their sums. It is the vanishing ideal of its affine cone. To verify that it is homogeneous, evaluate a polynomial on every scalar multiple of a nonzero cone point: as a polynomial in the scalar, its vanishing over the infinite field forces every degree coefficient to vanish. The origin imposes no additional condition on positive degrees. The affine Nullstellensatz then identifies this ideal with the radical of any homogeneous defining ideal for $X$.

The empty set is exceptional in that last sentence: $(x_0,\ldots,x_n)$ is radical but defines no projective point, while every homogeneous equation vanishes on the empty projective set. This is one reason arbitrary homogeneous ideals are not themselves a one-to-one encoding of projective geometry without a saturation convention. We keep the actual nonempty vanishing ideal when speaking of the homogeneous coordinate ring.

## 2. Affine charts and their transitions

The standard open $U_i=\{x_i\ne0\}$ is affine $n$-space, using coordinates $u_j=x_j/x_i$ for $j\ne i$. Each point has a unique representative with $x_i=1$, giving the inverse coordinate map. For a projective closed set defined by homogeneous equations, its intersection with $U_i$ is the affine algebraic set obtained by putting $x_i=1$. Its coordinate ring is the degree-zero part

$$\bigl(S/I(X)\bigr)_{x_i,0}.$$

To check this algebraically, send each affine coordinate to $x_j/x_i$. Every degree-zero localized fraction is represented by a homogeneous numerator divided by the same-degree power of $x_i$, so these ratios generate. Dehomogenizing the relations gives exactly the kernel. The quotient is reduced because localisation and taking a subring preserve reducedness, so the Nullstellensatz identifies the algebraic quotient with the chart's actual coordinate ring.

On $U_i\cap U_j$, the new coordinates divide the old ones by $u_j$; the coordinate for index $i$ becomes $1/u_j$. These are regular functions on the principal open where $u_j$ is nonzero. The transitions are inverse morphisms by direct substitution. Thus the charts agree in the affine morphism sense already proved; a chart change is not just a bijection of point sets.

The topology is Noetherian. Each of the finitely many affine charts is Noetherian, and an increasing chain of opens stabilizes on each chart. Taking the largest of their finitely many stabilization indices makes the whole chain stabilize. Closed subsets therefore have finite irreducible decompositions, just as in the affine case. Nonempty projective varieties correspond to homogeneous prime vanishing ideals other than the irrelevant maximal ideal. For this assertion it suffices first to test homogeneous factors: a vanishing product splits the projective set into their two closed zero sets. Primality on homogeneous factors implies primality in the graded quotient, since the highest nonzero homogeneous components of two elements cannot have zero product. This supplies the ordinary prime conclusion.

## 3. Morphisms described by forms

A map between projective or open algebraic sets is a morphism if, near each point, its expressions in affine source and target charts are morphisms. The transition formulas show this is independent of chart choices. Homogeneous forms $F_0,\ldots,F_m$ of a common positive degree define a morphism to $\mathbb P^m$ on the open set where they are not all zero. Scaling the input scales all outputs by the same scalar power. On the target chart $F_j\ne0$, its coordinate functions are the regular ratios $F_i/F_j$, proving the local morphism claim.

A base point is a point where every chosen form vanishes. The expression has no value there as written. Sometimes a common factor can be cancelled and the map extends, but that is an additional argument about the underlying rational map. For example $[x:y]\mapsto[x^2:xy]$ agrees with the identity where $x\ne0$ and extends as the identity, although the two displayed forms both vanish at $[0:1]$. The failed presentation does not prove nonexistence of an extension.

### Worked example 1: a conic with two inverse charts

The map $\nu:\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb P^2$ given by $[s:t]\mapsto[s^2:st:t^2]$ has no base point. Its image is the conic $XZ=Y^2$. If $X\ne0$, the inverse is $[X:Y]$; if $Z\ne0$, it is $[Y:Z]$. These charts cover the conic because $X=Z=0$ would force $Y=0$, which is not a projective point. The two inverse expressions agree on the overlap by the conic equation, and substitution checks both composites. Thus this is an isomorphism onto the conic in every characteristic. A familiar parametrisation becomes an isomorphism only after those inverse regularity checks.

## 4. Products remain projective: the Segre construction

Send $([x_0:\cdots:x_r],[y_0:\cdots:y_s])$ to the matrix of products $[z_{ij}]=[x_iy_j]$ in projective space of dimension $(r+1)(s+1)-1$. Changing either representative scales the entire matrix, so the map is defined. Local ratios make it a morphism. Its image is exactly the closed set cut out by all two-by-two minors.

The minors vanish on product matrices. Conversely, for a nonzero matrix satisfying them, choose a nonzero entry $z_{i_0j_0}$. Set projective coordinates $x_i=z_{ij_0}$ and $y_j=z_{i_0j}$. The minor equations give

$$x_iy_j=z_{ij_0}z_{i_0j}=z_{i_0j_0}z_{ij},$$

so their product represents the original point. On this chart the reconstructed coordinates are regular, and the two factors are recovered uniquely as projective classes. These local inverse maps agree where their chosen nonzero entries overlap, because they recover those same classes. This proves an isomorphism onto a closed algebraic set, including the inverse structure, and shows products of projective algebraic sets are projective by imposing their own homogeneous equations as well.

### Worked example 2: two families of lines on a quadric

For $r=s=1$, the image is $z_{00}z_{11}-z_{01}z_{10}=0$ in $\mathbb P^3$. Fixing the first projective factor and varying the second traces a projective line; fixing the second gives another family. Two lines from the same family with different fixed factors are disjoint, while a line from each family meets in the unique paired point. These claims follow from the product inverse just proved, with no drawing-based assumption about intersection. This quadric is a surface; it is not the plane conic of the preceding example despite both being described by quadratic equations.

## 5. Projection from a projective factor is closed

**Theorem 3.1.** For an algebraic set $Y$, the projection $\mathbb P^n\times Y\to Y$ takes closed subsets to closed subsets.

Work first over an affine open of $Y$, with coordinate ring $A$. A closed subset $Z$ of the product is defined by homogeneous polynomials in the projective variables with coefficients in $A$. Here is a justification of this description. At a point outside $Z$, choose a projective affine chart containing it. In that chart a polynomial separates the point from the closed subset. Homogenize this polynomial in the chart coordinate and multiply by that chart's homogeneous coordinate once more. The result vanishes also on the chart boundary, vanishes on all of $Z$, and remains nonzero at the selected point. These forms therefore cut out exactly $Z$. The Noetherian ring $A[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$ supplies finitely many homogeneous generators $F_1,\ldots,F_r$.

If the fiber at $y\in Y$ is empty, the projective empty-set criterion gives a degree $N$ for which the specialized forms generate every homogeneous monomial of degree $N$. Choose $N$ at least all their degrees. The linear map

$$\bigoplus_i k[x]_{N-\deg F_i}\longrightarrow k[x]_N,
\qquad(H_i)\longmapsto\sum_i H_iF_i(y,x)$$

is therefore surjective. In monomial bases it is a finite matrix whose entries are regular coefficient functions evaluated at $y$. Surjectivity means one full-row-rank minor is nonzero. That same minor remains nonzero on a principal neighborhood of $y$. At every point in this neighborhood the specialized degree-$N$ map is still surjective, so the corresponding fiber is empty by the same criterion.

The empty-fiber locus is thus open, which says the image of $Z$ is closed. On a general $Y$, apply this proof on each affine chart; closedness is local in an open cover. The exponent and the nonzero minor are allowed to depend on the chosen empty fiber. No uniform degree bound over every possible input is asserted or needed.

The theorem explains the contrast with affine projection. The hyperbola $xy=1$ has a missing projected value in affine space because the other coordinate can escape its affine chart. In a projective compactification the limit is represented by another chart. The proof here establishes the closed-image conclusion algebraically, even in positive characteristic; it does not use metric compactness.

## 6. Consequences for images and regular functions

The diagonal of projective space is closed, cut out by $x_iy_j-x_jy_i$. If all these minors vanish, the nonzero coordinate vectors are proportional by the same reconstruction used in the rank-one matrix argument. For an algebraic subset of projective space the diagonal is the corresponding restriction. Affine targets also have closed diagonals by coordinate differences. A morphism to such a target has closed graph, since its graph is the inverse image of that diagonal under the map comparing its value with the target coordinate.

If $X$ is projective and $f:X\to Y$ is a morphism to an affine or projective algebraic set, its graph is closed in $X\times Y$, hence in $\mathbb P^n\times Y$ after a projective embedding of $X$. Projection from the projective factor proves $f(X)$ closed in $Y$. This statement supplies its precise projectivity hypothesis; Chapter 1's affine hyperbola violates that hypothesis.

### Worked example 3: every global function on a projective variety is constant

Let $X$ be irreducible and projective, and let $h:X\to\mathbb A^1$ be regular. Compose with the open inclusion into $\mathbb P^1$. The image is closed by the theorem and misses infinity, so is a proper closed subset of $\mathbb P^1$, hence finite by its two affine charts and the polynomial root bound. The continuous image of an irreducible space is irreducible: a closed decomposition would pull back to one upstairs. A finite set of closed points is irreducible only when it is one point. Therefore $h$ is constant.

It follows that $\mathbb P^1$ is not affine. If it were, its coordinate ring would be the ring of its global regular functions, namely $k$, so the affine correspondence would identify it with a single point. It has more than one point, contradiction. More generally a positive-dimensional projective variety cannot be affine with a nonconstant coordinate function. This argument uses global regularity; a rational function such as $x_1/x_0$ is not globally regular across its poles.

The same argument describes global functions on a reduced projective algebraic set with several components. Each irreducible component is projective, so the function is constant on each. If two components meet, the two constants must agree at their common point. Form the finite graph whose vertices are irreducible components and whose edges record nonempty intersections. A function is therefore constant on every connected part of that graph.

Conversely, the union of the components in one graph component is closed, and its complement is the union of the finitely many other components, also closed. These subsets are consequently open as well. Assigning one scalar to each of them defines a regular function, since it is constant on an open neighborhood of every point. The unions are connected: irreducible spaces are connected, and a chain of overlapping connected pieces has connected union. Thus they are exactly the connected components of the algebraic set.

It follows that the ring of global functions is a product of one copy of the field for each connected component. In particular a connected reduced projective algebraic set has only constant global functions, even if reducible. Reducedness belongs to this classical assertion: a nonreduced projective scheme may carry nilpotent sections invisible at all its classical points, which this pointwise proof has not addressed.

## Exercises

### AG03-01. Homogeneous scaling

Explain why a common zero condition for homogeneous forms is independent of representatives, and give a nonhomogeneous expression whose value-zero condition is not.

**Hint 1.** A degree-$d$ form gains the factor $\lambda^d$.

**Hint 2.** In one projective coordinate pair, compare a linear coordinate minus a scalar under scaling.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

A nonzero scalar power cannot turn a zero value into a nonzero one, so each homogeneous zero condition is well-defined. The expression $x_0-1$ vanishes at the vector $(1,0)$ but not at a different scalar multiple $(\lambda,0)$ when $\lambda\ne1$. Those vectors represent the same projective point. Its nonhomogeneous zero condition is therefore not a projective equation as written.

</details>

### AG03-02. An empty projective set

Prove $V_+(x_0^2,x_1^3)=\varnothing$ in $\mathbb P^1$ and find a power of the irrelevant ideal contained in that equation ideal.

**Hint 1.** Both coordinates would have to vanish.

**Hint 2.** Every monomial of degree four contains either $x_0^2$ or $x_1^3$.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The equations force both field coordinates zero, disallowed in projective space. A degree-four monomial with exponent of $x_0$ below two has exponent of $x_1$ at least three; all others contain $x_0^2$. Thus $(x_0,x_1)^4$ lies in the defining ideal. This explicitly certifies the empty-set criterion rather than merely observing a lack of listed points.

</details>

### AG03-03. Chart transition

On $\mathbb P^2$, express the coordinates of $U_1$ in terms of $u=x_1/x_0,v=x_2/x_0$ on $U_0\cap U_1$.

**Hint 1.** Divide each homogeneous coordinate by $x_1$.

**Hint 2.** The overlap is the principal open where $u$ is nonzero.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The new coordinates are $x_0/x_1=1/u$ and $x_2/x_1=v/u$. Both are regular on $D(u)$, and the inverse formulas divide by the first new coordinate. These expressions verify the chart isomorphism using principal localisation. They do not extend to points where the denominator vanishes, which belong to a different chart.

</details>

### AG03-04. Projective closure of an affine parabola

Homogenize $y=x^2$ in $\mathbb P^2$ and identify its points outside the chart $Z\ne0$.

**Hint 1.** Make both terms degree two.

**Hint 2.** Put the homogenizing coordinate equal to zero after homogenization.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The equation is $YZ=X^2$. On $Z=0$ it forces $X=0$, leaving the single point $[0:1:0]$. On $Z\ne0$ it becomes the original affine equation in $x=X/Z,y=Y/Z$. The homogeneous polynomial is irreducible, for example by the conic parametrisation after relabeling coordinates, so this closed conic is the closure of its nonempty affine open.

</details>

### AG03-05. A presentation with a base point

Analyze $[x:y]\mapsto[x^2:xy]$. State where the displayed formula is defined and construct its extension.

**Hint 1.** Both outputs vanish at $[0:1]$.

**Hint 2.** On the other chart their common factor cancels.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The displayed expression is defined where $x\ne0$. There it equals $[x:y]$, so the identity map is an extension to all of $\mathbb P^1$. This proves existence of an extension by an explicit global morphism and agreement on the domain. It does not assign the meaningless value $[0:0]$ to the base point of the chosen expression.

</details>

### AG03-06. Invert the conic map

Verify the two inverse charts for $[s:t]\mapsto[s^2:st:t^2]$ and show they cover the conic.

**Hint 1.** Use $[X:Y]$ when $X\ne0$ and $[Y:Z]$ when $Z\ne0$.

**Hint 2.** On the overlap, the equation $XZ=Y^2$ makes these pairs proportional.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If $X$ and $Z$ both vanished, the equation would force $Y=0$, so the charts cover. On their overlap the determinant of the two pairs is $XZ-Y^2=0$, and each pair is nonzero. Substituting the parametrisation gives $[s^2:st]=[s:t]$ where $s\ne0$, and the other chart does the same where $t\ne0$. Substituting the inverse into the conic map returns its original homogeneous coordinates, proving the isomorphism.

</details>

### AG03-07. Segre reconstruction

For a nonzero matrix with all two-by-two minors zero, prove the reconstruction using a chosen nonzero entry gives its unique pair of projective factors.

**Hint 1.** Use its chosen column and row.

**Hint 2.** The minor equation expresses every entry as their product divided by the chosen entry.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

With entry $z_{i_0j_0}\ne0$, choose $x_i=z_{ij_0},y_j=z_{i_0j}$. The minors give $x_iy_j=z_{i_0j_0}z_{ij}$, so the matrix is their projective product. Any other factorisation has its first factor proportional to that nonzero column and its second proportional to that row. Hence the projective factors are unique. The same formulas are regular on the chosen chart, supplying the inverse morphism.

</details>

### AG03-08. Why an empty fiber stays empty nearby

Identify the finite matrix and open condition used in the closed-projection theorem.

**Hint 1.** At a sufficiently high degree the specialized forms span all monomials.

**Hint 2.** Surjectivity of a finite matrix is witnessed by one nonzero maximal minor.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The matrix represents multiplication by the defining homogeneous forms from the sum of the degree-complement spaces into the degree-$N$ homogeneous space. At the chosen empty fiber it is surjective by the irrelevant-power criterion. One full-row-rank determinant is nonzero there and remains nonzero on its principal open neighborhood. The same matrix is therefore surjective at every neighboring point, so the degree criterion makes every such fiber empty.

</details>

### AG03-09. Separate inverse and direct images

Explain why the affine hyperbola projection does not contradict projective closed projection.

**Hint 1.** Its second-coordinate factor is affine, not projective.

**Hint 2.** Closing the hyperbola in a projective factor adds boundary behavior.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The closed-image theorem concerns closed subsets of $\mathbb P^n\times Y$. The hyperbola is originally closed in $\mathbb A^1\times\mathbb A^1$, which is a smaller open product. Its projective closure includes the boundary needed over the missing base value. Thus it is not the same closed subset in the theorem's domain. Continuity of the original morphism only controlled inverse images and never guaranteed its direct image closed.

</details>

### AG03-10. Global functions on the projective line

Give a direct two-chart proof that every global regular function on $\mathbb P^1$ is constant.

**Hint 1.** On the two affine charts it is a polynomial in $t$ and a polynomial in $t^{-1}$.

**Hint 2.** Equality on the overlap is an equality of Laurent polynomials.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The first chart supplies $P(t)$ and the second $Q(t^{-1})$. On the nonzero overlap they agree; the infinite-field root bound makes that equality hold in $k[t,t^{-1}]$. A Laurent polynomial having only nonnegative and only nonpositive exponents can have only its constant coefficient. Hence both charts carry the same constant. This proves the claim independently of the more general projective closed-image argument.

</details>

### AG03-11. The projective diagonal

Prove the equations $x_iy_j-x_jy_i=0$ cut out the diagonal in $\mathbb P^n\times\mathbb P^n$.

**Hint 1.** Equal projective points satisfy all equations.

**Hint 2.** Choose one nonzero coordinate of the first vector and solve for the scalar relating the second.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If $x_i\ne0$, the equations give $y_j=(y_i/x_i)x_j$ for all $j$. The scalar cannot be zero, because then every coordinate of the second vector would vanish. Thus the vectors are proportional and represent the same point. The reverse implication follows directly from proportionality. These equations are homogeneous in each variable block and therefore define a closed subset of the product.

</details>

### AG03-12. Why the projective line is not affine

Deduce nonaffineness from its global regular functions and explain why an affine chart does not refute the conclusion.

**Hint 1.** An affine set is recovered from its entire coordinate algebra.

**Hint 2.** A chart is a proper open subset with a different ring of functions.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If the projective line were affine, its coordinate algebra would be $k$ because all global regular functions are constant. The affine correspondence would identify it with one point, contradicting its actual point set. Its standard chart is an affine line with coordinate ring $k[t]$, whose nonconstant function has a pole at the omitted point. Local affineness therefore does not imply global affineness, and the two rings describe different domains.

</details>

## Closing summary

Homogeneous equations and affine charts give projective geometry with explicit transitions. Conic and Segre constructions include regular inverses, while base-point examples distinguish a formula from its possible extension. The projective empty-set criterion and a finite rank minor prove closed projection; closed graphs then prove closed images and constancy of global functions on irreducible projective varieties. These algebraic proofs hold under the stated field hypotheses without analytic compactness.
