
# Probability Spaces, Sigma-Algebras and Pi-Systems

Measure-theoretic probability begins by specifying which subsets are events and which countably additive probability measure governs them. The event class matters whenever infinitely many observations, limiting events or null-set modifications enter an argument. This chapter proves the pi-lambda theorem, probability-measure uniqueness and the measurable-function extension principle used repeatedly later.

Required preparation is [Measure and Integration](#/books/measure-and-integration) and the [L3 probability course text](#/books/probability-models-proofs). The product-measure discussion also draws on the retained Euler source “Product Measures, Fubini, and Probability,” originally at modules/textbooks/real_analysis/chapters/22-product-measures-probability.qmd. That chapter, its examples and its exercise identities remain in their original collection; this is a new L4 treatment rather than a replacement record.

## Events are selected by a sigma-algebra

A probability space consists of a set Omega, a sigma-algebra F of its subsets and a nonnegative countably additive measure of total mass one. A sigma-algebra contains the full space, is closed under complements and under countable unions. It is consequently closed under countable intersections, relative differences and limits formed from countable unions and intersections.

Given any family of subsets, its generated sigma-algebra is the intersection of all sigma-algebras containing the family. This is itself a sigma-algebra: each closure property survives intersection. The construction specifies a smallest event class, not the ability to take arbitrary uncountable unions. A probability assignment on a small family must extend countably additively to that generated class before it defines a probability space.

Countable additivity yields continuity from below by disjointifying an increasing sequence of events. Continuity from above follows by applying continuity from below to complements, since total mass is finite. Hence a decreasing sequence whose intersection is empty has probabilities tending to zero. This finite-measure step is used later in extension constructions and cannot be copied without adjustment into an infinite-measure context.

The Borel sigma-algebra on a metric space is generated by its open sets. On the real line, rational half-lines generate it: complements and countable unions make open intervals, and every open set is a countable union of rational intervals. Real random variables are measurable into this Borel structure. Completing the underlying probability space is a separate operation, discussed below.

## A lambda-system is weaker than a sigma-algebra

A lambda-system contains Omega, is closed under complement relative to Omega and under countable disjoint unions. A pi-system is closed under finite intersections. In the theorem below we include Omega in the pi-system; if necessary it can be added without changing its generated sigma-algebra.

A lambda-system is closed under relative difference when the smaller event is contained in the larger. If A is contained in B, then A and the complement of B are disjoint members, so complementing their union gives B minus A. It need not be closed under intersections of arbitrary pairs. That missing closure is exactly what the pi-system argument recovers.

Let D be the smallest lambda-system containing a pi-system P. For a fixed member A of P, consider all B for which the intersection A with B belongs to D. This collection is a lambda-system: its full-space intersection is A, complements are handled by relative difference inside A, and disjoint unions remain disjoint after intersection. It contains P because P is closed under intersections. Thus it contains D.

Now fix B in D and repeat the same argument with the roles reversed. The collection of A whose intersection with B lies in D is a lambda-system and contains P by the preceding paragraph. Hence it also contains D. We have proved that D is closed under intersections of its own members.

A lambda-system closed under intersections is a sigma-algebra. Complements and finite intersections give finite unions, and an arbitrary countable union can be disjointified using finite preceding unions and relative differences. Therefore D equals the sigma-algebra generated by P. This proves the pi-lambda theorem with the two extension steps explicit, rather than assuming a lambda-system is already closed under intersections.

## Uniqueness of probability measures

Let two probability measures agree on a pi-system generating F. Their equality class is a lambda-system: both assign one to Omega, equality passes to complements by subtracting from one, and countable additivity passes it to disjoint unions. The pi-lambda theorem therefore makes the measures equal on all F.

The theorem requires both a suitable intersection family and the correct generated sigma-algebra. Equal probabilities for a collection of interesting events do not automatically identify every joint probability. The class might not be a pi-system, or might generate only a coarser observation structure.

### Worked example: a distribution function determines a real law

For a real random variable, its distribution function gives the probability of every closed lower half-line. Those half-lines, with the full line added, form a pi-system and generate the Borel sigma-algebra. Two probability laws with the same distribution function agree on that pi-system, so the uniqueness theorem identifies their full Borel laws.

It is enough to know the function at rational arguments and recover other half-line probabilities by continuity from above along decreasing rational endpoints. This is an identification theorem about the probability law. It does not recover a unique random variable on a particular probability space; many measurable maps can have that same law.

## Extending independence from generators

Suppose two sigma-algebras are generated by pi-systems containing Omega, and the probability of an intersection factors for each pair of generator events. Fix one generator B from the second family. The first-family events A satisfying factorization with B form a lambda-system. Complement and disjoint-union closure follow from finite probability and countable additivity. Pi-lambda extends factorization to every A in the first sigma-algebra.

Now fix such an arbitrary A and extend in B through the second pi-system. Factorization holds for every pair of events from the two full sigma-algebras. This proves independence from a manageable set of generator comparisons, without an unjustified leap from pairwise numerical examples to all measurable events.

For more than two sigma-algebras, mutual independence requires factorization for every finite selection, not only every pair. The same iterative generator extension applies to a finite product of events. Pairwise independence can hold without that full condition; a later chapter gives a binary construction where the third variable is determined by the first two.

### Worked example: two independent partitions on six outcomes

On a fair six-sided die, let one observation reveal parity and another reveal divisibility by three. Their positive events have probabilities one half and one third. Every parity/divisibility cell has the product of its corresponding marginal probabilities: the even divisible cell is the singleton six, the odd divisible cell singleton three, and each nondivisible parity cell has two outcomes.

Their two generated sigma-algebras are therefore independent. Neither observation is independent of the full die outcome, because each is a nonconstant function of it. The example separates independence of two coarse observations from the impossible assertion that a nontrivial event is independent of itself.

## A bounded-function extension principle

Let V be a vector space of bounded measurable real functions containing the constant one. Suppose it contains indicators of a generating pi-system and is closed under bounded increasing pointwise limits. Define D as the sets whose indicators lie in V. Constants and subtraction give complements, and finite sums followed by bounded monotone limits give indicators of disjoint countable unions. Thus D is a lambda-system containing the pi-system, and contains its generated sigma-algebra.

Every bounded nonnegative measurable function is the increasing limit of bounded simple functions, each a finite linear combination of these indicators. Hence it belongs to V. Subtract positive and negative parts for signed bounded functions. This proves the indicated functional monotone-class principle.

The theorem is useful for identities first checked on simple events. To extend an integral equality to all bounded measurable functions, verify linearity and monotone-limit closure under the actual finite measures, then invoke the principle. An equality on some smooth or continuous test functions instead needs a separate density or measure-determination argument; it is not automatically an application of this indicator theorem.

## Completing a probability space

A complete probability space includes every subset of every null event. To complete an arbitrary probability space, add all sets differing from an original measurable event by a subset of an original null event. Assign such a set the probability of its measurable representative.

This is well-defined. Two representatives differ only inside a union of null events, so their probabilities agree. Complements preserve the same null modification and countable unions are controlled by a countable union of null events. Countable additivity follows by replacing each event with a measurable representative and removing the common null union, on whose complement all disjointness relations agree. Thus the construction gives a complete probability extension.

Almost-sure equality means equality outside some measurable null event. It permits replacing one version in integrals, but does not turn an undefined pointwise expression into a defined one at every outcome. When a theorem uses a pathwise identity, distinguish an identity for all points from one holding on a single full-probability set. For a countable family of almost-sure claims the exceptional null sets can be united. For an uncountable family, that argument no longer follows from countable additivity.

### Worked example: completion adds a diagonal subset

In the unit square, the Borel diagonal has two-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero by the product-measure section formula. Choose a non-Borel subset A of the unit interval and take the diagonal points with coordinate in A. This set is contained in a Borel null set, so it belongs to the completed planar Lebesgue sigma-algebra.

It is not Borel: its inverse image under the continuous diagonal map would be A, contradicting the choice of A. Its vertical sections are either a singleton or empty, so every section is Borel despite the set not being jointly Borel. Measurable sections alone therefore do not characterize membership in the product Borel sigma-algebra. This example uses completion precisely, rather than treating every subset of a product as an event by default.

## Product probability and the imported measure construction

For two probability spaces, their rectangle probabilities define a unique product measure on the product sigma-algebra. Existence uses the product-measure theorem already proved in Measure and Integration; finite probability measures satisfy its sigma-finiteness assumptions. Uniqueness is the rectangle pi-system result just established. Thus this chapter identifies the measure-construction input and proves the probability-specific uniqueness and extension steps built on it.

Sections of product-measurable sets are measurable because rectangles have that property and the class with measurable sections is a sigma-algebra. Under nonnegative integrands Tonelli permits infinite integrals; for signed integrands Fubini requires absolute integrability. The next chapters use this distinction to construct independent copies and factor expectations. Neither marginal laws nor equal-looking one-dimensional summaries determine a product coupling without the independence condition.

## Events modulo null differences form a complete metric space

Define event distance as the probability of their symmetric difference. Symmetry and nonnegativity are immediate; the inclusion of A symmetric-difference C in the union of the two intermediate symmetric differences proves the triangle inequality. Distance zero identifies events equal almost surely, so passing to those equivalence classes gives a genuine metric.

This metric is complete. From a Cauchy sequence choose a subsequence whose successive difference probabilities are at most two to the minus k. The probability that any change occurs after its kth term is at most the corresponding geometric tail sum. Outside a null limiting exceptional set, the subsequence indicators eventually stabilize. Their lower-limit event is measurable and differs from the kth subsequence event only where some later change occurs. Its distance is therefore bounded by that same tail sum, tending to zero. The Cauchy property then makes the full sequence converge to this event class.

This is a metric statement about measurable events modulo null differences. It holds even before adding every subset of a null set to the sigma-algebra. Completing the probability measure and proving completeness of this event metric are distinct operations; the shared word complete should not obscure which mathematical object is being changed.

## Exercises

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-1"></a>

### Exercise 1

Show that the sigma-algebra generated by a finite partition consists of all unions of its atoms.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

All atom unions form a sigma-algebra.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Any sigma-algebra containing the partition must contain those unions.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The atom unions are closed under complement and countable unions, since there are only finitely many atoms. They contain the partition. Conversely every sigma-algebra containing its atoms contains every finite atom union. The minimal generated sigma-algebra is exactly that family.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-2"></a>

### Exercise 2

Prove continuity from below for probability measures.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Disjointify the increasing events.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Apply countable additivity to their union.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Write the first event and successive set differences as disjoint increments. Their finite partial unions are the original increasing events. Countable additivity makes the limiting probability equal to the sum of increment masses, which is the limit of the finite partial probabilities.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-3"></a>

### Exercise 3

Why is a lambda-system closed under difference of nested events?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Complement the larger event.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Its complement is disjoint from the smaller event.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

For $A\subset B$, the disjoint union $A\cup B^c$ belongs to the lambda-system. Complementing gives $B\setminus A$. This does not establish difference closure for arbitrary nonnested events without another step.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-4"></a>

### Exercise 4

Give equal generator probabilities that do not determine a joint law when the generator family is not a pi-system.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use four points and two overlapping two-point events.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compare uniform mass with equal mass on the first and fourth points.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

On four points let $A=\{1,2\}$ and $B=\{1,3\}$. Uniform mass and mass one half at each of 1 and 4 both give A and B probability one half, yet assign their intersection probability one quarter and one half. A and B generate every singleton but their family is not intersection-closed. Agreement on this family therefore cannot invoke the uniqueness theorem.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-5"></a>

### Exercise 5

Prove that an event independent of itself has probability zero or one.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use factorization with the same event twice.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Solve the resulting scalar equation.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Self-independence gives p equal to p squared. The only probabilities satisfying this are zero and one. Thus a nontrivial observation event cannot be independent of the entire sigma-algebra that contains it.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-6"></a>

### Exercise 6

Show that completing two independent sub-sigma-algebras preserves their independence.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Replace completed events by original representatives.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Their intersections differ only inside a null union.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Each completed event equals an original event outside a null set. The intersection of two completed events equals the original intersection outside the union of those null sets. All probabilities in the factorization are therefore unchanged, so independence passes to the completions.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-7"></a>

### Exercise 7

On an uncountable set, verify the countable/co-countable probability assignment.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Take the event family consisting of countable sets and their complements.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

A disjoint collection has at most one co-countable member.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

This family is a sigma-algebra: complements swap its two kinds, and a countable union is countable unless it contains a co-countable member. Assign zero to countable sets and one to co-countable sets. For disjoint countable collections, either all are countable or exactly one is co-countable, proving countable additivity and total mass one.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-8"></a>

### Exercise 8

Why do measurable sections not suffice for joint Borel measurability?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use the non-Borel diagonal subset in the worked example.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Its inverse image under the diagonal map detects the obstruction.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Every section is empty or a singleton and hence Borel. If the whole set were Borel, continuity of the diagonal map would make its preimage A Borel, a contradiction. Section measurability is a necessary condition established by a sigma-algebra argument, not a sufficient one.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-9"></a>

### Exercise 9

Explain why countably many almost-sure assertions can be imposed on one full-probability set.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Unite their exceptional null events.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The union still has probability zero.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Countable subadditivity makes the union of the exceptional sets null, and outside it every assertion holds. An arbitrary uncountable union need not be null or even measurable, so that proof does not establish an uncountable simultaneous statement.

</details>

<a id="probability-spaces-sigma-algebras-pi-systems-ex-10"></a>

### Exercise 10

Prove that a measurable real function on a finite partition sigma-algebra is constant on each atom.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Two distinct values can be separated by a rational threshold.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Its inverse half-line would split the atom.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If values differed within one atom, choose a rational strictly between them. The inverse lower half-line is measurable and contains some but not all of that atom, impossible because measurable sets are unions of whole atoms. Conversely a function constant on each atom has measurable inverse images.

</details>
