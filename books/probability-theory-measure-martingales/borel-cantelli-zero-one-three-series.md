
# Borel-Cantelli, Zero-One Laws and the Three-Series Theorem

Independent infinite series require three kinds of control: rare large jumps, accumulated truncated drift and accumulated truncated variance. This chapter proves their exact equivalence to almost-sure convergence. The necessity argument is as important as sufficiency; it uses bounded symmetrized variables rather than assuming almost-sure convergence already controls expectations.

Preparation is independence, product copies, integrable moments and the preceding convergence chapter. All variables in the series theorem are real and finite almost surely. The complex exponential used in one proof is defined by its bounded sine and cosine parts, so no characteristic-function continuity theorem is needed.

## Borel-Cantelli in both directions

For events A n, the event of infinitely many occurrences is the intersection over m of the union over n at least m. If their probabilities sum finitely, its probability is at most every tail probability sum, which tends to zero. This first Borel-Cantelli conclusion needs no independence.

If the events are mutually independent and their probability sum diverges, the probability of no occurrence between m and N is the product of one minus their probabilities, bounded by the exponential of minus their probability sum. Let N increase to infinity to get probability zero of no occurrence after m. Taking the countable union over m proves infinitely many occurrences with probability one. The proof states exactly where independence enters.

A divergent probability sum without independence has no such conclusion: the nested events that a uniform variable is below one over n occur only finitely often almost surely despite their harmonic probability sum. The earlier integral example and the independent theorem therefore address different joint laws.

## Kolmogorov's tail zero-one law

For independent variables, a tail event belongs to every sigma-algebra generated by variables after the first N. It is independent of the sigma-algebra generated by the first N, using independence extended from finite cylinders. Since this holds for every N, pi-lambda extension makes the tail event independent of the sigma-algebra generated by the entire sequence.

The event itself belongs to that full sigma-algebra, so self-independence forces its probability to equal its square. It is therefore zero or one. Identical distributions are not required. The theorem concerns tail events, not every event built from independent observations.

Series convergence is a tail event. The Cauchy criterion expresses it using countable intersections and unions of events controlling finite tail sums. Removing any fixed initial segment does not change convergence, so the event is measurable using only the remaining coordinates for every N. The zero-one law says its probability is extreme, but does not decide which extreme holds; the three-series criterion will do that.

## Kolmogorov's maximal inequality with a complete proof

Let independent centred variables have finite variances and let S k be their partial sums through k. For a positive threshold a, partition the event that some absolute partial sum through N reaches a by the first crossing time k. Each first-crossing event is determined by the first k variables. The remaining sum is independent of that event and of S k, and has mean zero.

Expanding the final squared sum on the first-crossing event makes its cross term vanish. Its expected square there is at least the expected S k squared there, hence at least a squared times the event probability. Sum over the disjoint first-crossing events and bound by the total expected final square. Independence and centring make that square equal the sum of variances, proving
$$P\{\max_{k\le N}|S_k|\ge a\}\le
a^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^N\operatorname{Var}(X_j).$$
The same proof applies to any finite tail block. Finite second moments justify every expanded product; Cauchy–Schwarz supplies integrability of the cross terms.

## Summable variances give an almost-sure convergent centred series

Assume the independent variables are centred and their variances sum finitely. Choose tail starting positions m k whose remaining variance sum is at most two to the minus three k. Apply the maximal inequality at threshold two to the minus k, then let the finite ending point grow. The probability that any such tail partial sum exceeds the threshold is at most two to the minus k.

These probabilities are summable, so the first Borel-Cantelli result makes all sufficiently late selected tails uniformly small almost surely. Any difference between two partial sums after m k is at most twice the corresponding maximal tail magnitude. Thus the series partial sums are Cauchy almost surely and converge in the complete real line.

Fatou applied to squared tail discrepancies also gives expected square error from the limit at most the corresponding remaining variance sum. Therefore convergence holds in L two as well. This proves the convergence criterion directly, without invoking an unproved equivalence between almost-sure and norm convergence.

### Worked example: a random harmonic series

Let independent fair signs multiply one over n. Their means are zero and variance sum is the convergent sum of one over n squared. The criterion proves almost-sure and L two convergence of the signed series. Its absolute series is the harmonic series and diverges on every outcome.

Thus convergence of an independent series is not necessarily absolute convergence. A drift series in the theorem below can also be conditionally convergent; replacing its convergence by absolute summability would strengthen the criterion incorrectly.

## Statement of the three-series theorem

Fix any positive truncation level c and independent real variables X n. Put Y n equal to X n when its absolute value is at most c and zero otherwise. Then the series of X n converges almost surely if and only if all three conditions hold:
$$\sum_nP(|X_n|>c)<\infty,\qquad
\sum_n EY_n\ \hbox{converges in }\mathbb R,\qquad
\sum_n\operatorname{Var}(Y_n)<\infty.$$
The second is a signed numerical series, and the third is a nonnegative numerical series. Truncation before taking moments is material. The original variables can have nonexistent first or second moments even when their sample-path series converges.

If the conditions hold, first Borel-Cantelli makes X n and Y n differ only finitely often almost surely. The centred truncated series converges by the variance criterion, and its deterministic mean series converges by assumption. Their sum gives convergence of the Y n series, hence the X n series. This proves sufficiency in full.

Necessity needs a further argument; a bounded first moment of partial sums alone would not supply it.

## First necessity step: large jumps must be summable

Almost-sure convergence forces X n to tend to zero almost surely. The events absolute X n exceeding c are independent, so the second Borel-Cantelli result would force infinitely many large terms if their probabilities summed infinitely. That contradicts convergence to zero. Their probability sum is therefore finite.

Consequently the original and truncated series differ only finitely often, so the bounded independent series of Y n also converges almost surely. We must prove that a convergent bounded independent series has summable variances and a convergent mean series. The next argument supplies that often omitted necessity.

## Symmetrization proves bounded-series variance necessity

Construct an independent copy Y n prime of the whole independent sequence, using the product probability space. The difference variables D n equal Y n minus its copy are independent, centred and bounded in absolute value by twice c. Their series converges almost surely on the product space because both copied series do.

Define the characteristic function of a real variable V as the expectation of exp of i t V. Independence and the conjugate identity show
$$\varphi_{D_n}(t)=|\varphi_{Y_n}(t)|^2,$$
a nonnegative real number at most one. For nonzero t with absolute value at most one over twice c, absolute t D n is at most one. The elementary bound one minus cosine u at least u squared divided by four on that interval yields
$$1-\varphi_{D_n}(t)\ge
\frac{t^2}{4}ED_n^2
=\frac{t^2}{2}\operatorname{Var}(Y_n).$$
One can obtain the cosine bound by integrating sine v at least v divided by two for v between zero and one; symmetry handles negative u.

Suppose the variances summed infinitely. Independence of the D n makes the characteristic function of any tail partial sum the product of their nonnegative characteristic functions. Using one minus a at most exp of minus a, the preceding inequality forces that product to tend to zero as the ending index grows, for every fixed starting index.

But almost-sure convergence of the D n series makes the supremum of the absolute tail partial sums tend to zero almost surely as the starting index grows. The characteristic function of every such partial sum differs from one by at most the expectation of the minimum of two and absolute t times that supremum. Bounded convergence makes this bound tend to zero uniformly over ending indices.

For a sufficiently late start all these characteristic functions are therefore, for example, within one half of one. They cannot at the same time tend to zero as the ending index grows. The contradiction proves summability of the truncated variances. Nonnegative symmetrized characteristic functions and the uniform tail control are both essential parts of this argument.

## Final necessity step and cutoff independence

The summable-variance criterion now gives almost-sure convergence of the centred Y n series on the original space. The uncentred Y n series already converges there. On the intersection of those two full-probability events, subtract their partial sums to conclude that the deterministic partial sums of E Y n converge. This proves the second condition and finishes necessity.

The theorem was proved for every fixed positive c. If its conditions hold at one cutoff, sufficiency gives almost-sure series convergence; necessity then gives them at every other cutoff. Thus cutoff independence is a consequence of the proved equivalence, not a heuristic that truncating differently never changes numerical series.

### Worked example: finitely many large jumps with infinite expected total

Let independent B n be Bernoulli with success probability one over n squared and set X n equal to n B n. At cutoff one, all sufficiently late truncated variables are zero, and the large-jump probability sum is finite. The theorem gives almost-sure convergence; in fact only finitely many terms are nonzero almost surely.

All terms are nonnegative, but their expected sum is the harmonic sum of one over n, hence infinite by Tonelli. The random finite total has infinite expectation. This is an independent example showing that almost-sure convergence alone does not supply uniform integrability or expectation interchange.

### Worked example: the exact Gaussian-series criterion

Let X n equal a n times independent standard normal variables. If the squared coefficients sum finitely, the centred variance criterion gives convergence. Conversely, suppose the series converges. Then X n tends to zero in probability, forcing the coefficients to tend to zero: a subsequence bounded away from zero would retain a fixed positive Gaussian tail probability.

For a fixed cutoff c and sufficiently small absolute coefficients, the symmetric truncated mean is zero and truncated variance is at least a positive constant times a n squared, since the standard-normal second moment restricted to absolute value at most one is positive. Three-series necessity therefore forces the squared coefficients to sum finitely. Thus the criterion is exactly squared-coefficient summability, not absolute coefficient summability.

The argument proves both directions under the actual Gaussian law. It does not transfer that exact coefficient criterion to arbitrary heavy-tailed innovations without checking their truncated moments.

## Bounded independent nonnegative terms give a simpler criterion

Suppose independent terms are nonnegative and share one finite deterministic upper bound c. Their series is finite almost surely if and only if the sum of their expectations is finite. Sufficiency needs no independence: Tonelli makes the total expectation finite, forcing an almost-sure finite sum.

For necessity, truncate above the common bound, so every term remains unchanged. Almost-sure convergence and the proved three-series theorem make the nonnegative mean series converge to a finite number. There is no cancellation in that numerical series, so this is exactly finite total expectation.

The common bound and independence are doing different work. The rare-jump example has independent nonnegative terms but no common bound; its series is finite almost surely with infinite mean sum. The earlier uniform-variable indicator example has bounded nonnegative terms but dependence; it also has a finite realized count with divergent mean sum. Neither violates this corollary because each omits one of its hypotheses.

Finally, zero-one law means that failure of any three-series condition gives convergence probability zero, rather than merely failing to establish probability one. A positive probability of convergence for independent terms already forces almost-sure convergence and hence all three conditions. This strengthens how the exact criterion can be used without adding an expectation-interchange claim.

### Exercise 1

Prove the first Borel-Cantelli implication without independence.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Bound a union of late events by its probability tail sum.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Intersect those tail unions.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The infinitely-often event lies in every late union, whose probability is at most the tail sum. A summable probability series has tails tending to zero, so the infinitely-often event is null. No factorization was used.

</details>

### Exercise 2

Where is independence used in the divergent Borel-Cantelli direction?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Consider no occurrences in a finite block.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Its probability factors into a product.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The no-occurrence probability is the product of one minus each event probability, bounded by an exponential negative partial sum. Divergence makes this probability vanish for every tail start. Without independence the product identity need not hold.

</details>

### Exercise 7

What does almost-sure series convergence contribute to the characteristic-function contradiction?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Its tail partial sums are uniformly small eventually on each convergent path.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Use a bounded exponential discrepancy.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The supremum tail magnitude tends to zero almost surely. The difference from one of every tail characteristic function is bounded by expectation of the minimum of two and absolute t times that supremum. Bounded convergence gives uniform-in-ending-index closeness to one, contradicting the variance-driven product limit zero.

### Exercise 8

Does the mean series in the theorem need absolute convergence? Give an example.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

### Exercise 10

Prove that the Gaussian coefficients must tend to zero under series convergence.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Terms of a convergent series tend to zero in probability.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

A coefficient subsequence bounded away from zero preserves a positive tail probability.

</details>

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If absolute a n is at least b positive along a subsequence, the chance absolute a n Z n exceeds b divided by two is at least the fixed positive chance absolute standard normal exceeds one half. This contradicts convergence in probability to zero. The truncated-variance lower bound then establishes squared summability as in the worked proof.

</details>
