
# Modules and Exact Sequences

Vector spaces are the backbone of undergraduate linear algebra, but their good behaviour depends on the field hypothesis: every nonzero scalar is invertible, every subspace has a complement, and every linear map has a matrix representation after choosing bases. When the scalars come from a general ring, many of these privileges disappear. The resulting theory---modules over a ring---is richer, more subtle, and essential for modern algebra, algebraic geometry, and homological methods.

This adapted Euler chapter preserves the earlier module examples and exercises from Morphisms First, whose original source and book-scoped identity remain intact. Read [Rings and Ideals](rings-and-ideals.md) first. This chapter defines modules and module homomorphisms, studies free and finitely presented modules, introduces Noetherian finiteness, and uses exact sequences to organise the relationship between submodules, quotients, and extensions. The examples stay concrete: abelian groups as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, matrices over principal ideal domains, and explicit short exact sequences that fail to split.

## 1.1 Definition and first examples

Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity. If $R$ is the zero ring, the unital-module axiom forces every module to be zero. A **left $R$-module** $M$ is an abelian group $(M,+)$ together with a scalar multiplication $R \times M \to M$, written $(r,m) \mapsto rm$, satisfying the following axioms for all $r,s \in R$ and $m,n \in M$:

1. $r(m+n) = rm + rn$,
2. $(r+s)m = rm + sm$,
3. $(rs)m = r(sm)$,
4. $1m = m$.

A **right $R$-module** is defined analogously with multiplication $M \times R \to M$ written $mr$ and associativity $(mr)s = m(rs)$. When $R$ is commutative, the two notions coincide up to notation; we work with left modules unless stated otherwise.

**Example 1.1 (Vector spaces).** If $R = k$ is a field, a $k$-module is exactly a vector space over $k$. All four axioms are the familiar vector space laws.

**Example 1.2 (Abelian groups).** If $R = \mathbb{Z}$, a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is precisely an abelian group. The scalar multiplication is forced by the axioms:

$$nm = \underbrace{m + m + \cdots + m}_{n\text{ times}}, \qquad (-n)m = -(nm).$$

Conversely, every abelian group admits this unique $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure. Thus the theory of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is the theory of abelian groups.

**Example 1.3 (Ideals).** If $R$ is commutative, every ideal $I \subseteq R$ is an $R$-module under the ring multiplication. In particular, $R$ itself is an $R$-module.

**Example 1.4 (Matrices).** The set $M_{m \times n}(R)$ of $m \times n$ matrices over $R$ is an $R$-module under entrywise addition and scalar multiplication. When $m=n$, it is also a ring, but the module structure does not require matrix multiplication.

**Example 1.5 (The zero module).** The trivial group $\{0\}$ with $r \cdot 0 = 0$ is the **zero module**, denoted $0$. It plays the role of the zero vector space.

### Submodules and quotient modules

A **submodule** $N \subseteq M$ is a subgroup closed under scalar multiplication: if $n \in N$ and $r \in R$, then $rn \in N$. The quotient $M/N$ is the quotient abelian group with scalar multiplication $r(m+N) = rm + N$. One checks this is well defined exactly when $N$ is a submodule.

**Definition 1.6.** An $R$-module $M$ is **cyclic** if it is generated by a single element: $M = Rm$ for some $m \in M$. It is **finitely generated** if there exist $m_{1}, \dots, m_{n} \in M$ such that every element of $M$ is an $R$-linear combination $r_{1}m_{1} + \cdots + r_{n}m_{n}$.

The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic for every $n$. The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}^{2}$ is finitely generated but not cyclic.

## 1.2 Module homomorphisms

An **$R$-linear map** (or **module homomorphism**) $f: M \to N$ is a homomorphism of abelian groups satisfying $f(rm) = rf(m)$ for all $r \in R$ and $m \in M$. The set of all such maps is denoted $\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,N)$; it is an abelian group under pointwise addition and, when $R$ is commutative, an $R$-module via $(rf)(m) = r \cdot f(m)$.

**Proposition 1.7 (First isomorphism theorem).** Let $f: M \to N$ be an $R$-linear map. Then

$$M/\operatorname{ker} f \cong \operatorname{im} f$$

via the map $m + \operatorname{ker} f \mapsto f(m)$.

*Proof.* The map is well defined because $f(m) = f(m')$ whenever $m - m' \in \operatorname{ker} f$. It is $R$-linear because $f$ is, and it is bijective by construction. $\square$

The kernel and image of an $R$-linear map are submodules of $M$ and $N$ respectively. The cokernel is $\operatorname{coker} f = N/\operatorname{im} f$.

**Definition 1.8.** A map $f: M \to N$ is **injective** (a **monomorphism**) if $\operatorname{ker} f = 0$. It is **surjective** (an **epimorphism**) if $\operatorname{im} f = N$. It is an **isomorphism** if there exists $g: N \to M$ with $gf = \operatorname{id}_{M}$ and $fg = \operatorname{id}_{N}$.

## 1.3 Free modules and the universal property

A **free $R$-module** on a set $S$ is an $R$-module $F(S)$ together with a map $i: S \to F(S)$ such that for every $R$-module $M$ and every set map $\varphi: S \to M$, there exists a unique $R$-linear map $\tilde{\varphi}: F(S) \to M$ with $\tilde{\varphi} \circ i = \varphi$.

Concretely, $F(S)$ is the direct sum of copies of $R$ indexed by $S$:

$$F(S) = \bigoplus_{s \in S} R = \{ (r_{s})_{s \in S} : r_{s} = 0 \text{ for all but finitely many } s \}.$$

The map $i$ sends $s$ to the basis element $e_{s}$ which is $1$ in coordinate $s$ and $0$ elsewhere. When $S = \{1, \dots, n\}$, we recover $R^{n}$ with its standard basis.

**Proposition 1.9.** The free module $F(S)$ is unique up to unique isomorphism.

*Proof.* If $(F', i')$ is another free module on $S$, the universal property of $F$ gives a unique linear map $F \to F'$ extending $i'$, and vice versa. The composites in either direction are linear maps extending the identity on $S$, hence equal to the identity by uniqueness. $\square$

**Worked example 1.10.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and $S = \{a,b\}$. Then $F(S) = \mathbb{Z}^{2}$. Given the set map $\varphi(a) = 3$ and $\varphi(b) = 5$ into $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, the unique extension is

$$\tilde{\varphi}(x,y) = 3x + 5y \pmod{12}.$$

We verify: $\tilde{\varphi}(1,0) = 3 = \varphi(a)$ and $\tilde{\varphi}(0,1) = 5 = \varphi(b)$. Linearity follows from the ring structure of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.

### Presentations

A finitely presented $R$-module is one that can be described by generators and relations. For the matrix convention below, assume $R$ is commutative. Explicitly, there exists an exact sequence

$$R^{m} \xrightarrow{A} R^{n} \to M \to 0,$$

where $A$ is an $n \times m$ matrix over $R$. With column vectors, the columns of $A$ are relations among $n$ generators. For a noncommutative ring, left-module presentations require a compatible row-vector convention or matrices over the opposite ring. Over a principal ideal domain, the Smith normal form diagonalizes $A$ and yields the structure theorem for finitely generated modules.

**Worked example 1.11.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$$

gives the presentation $\mathbb{Z}^{2} \xrightarrow{A} \mathbb{Z}^{2} \to M \to 0$. The cokernel is

$$M \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}.$$

The order of $M$ is $60$, and by the Chinese remainder theorem,

$$M \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/30\mathbb{Z}.$$

**Verification script.**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, ZZ
from sympy.matrices.normalforms import smith_normal_form

A = Matrix([[6, 0], [0, 10]])
print(smith_normal_form(A, domain=ZZ))
```

This optional source snippet describes the Smith calculation; it is not an execution record. The invariant factors are $2$ and $30$ by the exact divisor argument.

## 1.4 Noetherian modules and finite control

An $R$-module $M$ is **Noetherian** if every ascending chain of submodules stabilizes: given $N_{1} \subseteq N_{2} \subseteq \cdots$, there exists $k$ such that $N_{k} = N_{k+1} = \cdots$.

**Proposition 1.12.** An $R$-module $M$ is Noetherian if and only if every submodule of $M$ is finitely generated.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $N \subseteq M$ is not finitely generated. Choose $n_{1} \in N$. Since $N \neq Rn_{1}$, choose $n_{2} \in N \setminus Rn_{1}$. Inductively choose $n_{k+1} \in N \setminus (Rn_{1} + \cdots + Rn_{k})$. The chain

$$Rn_{1} \subsetneq Rn_{1} + Rn_{2} \subsetneq \cdots$$

never stabilizes, contradicting the Noetherian hypothesis.

($\Leftarrow$) Given an ascending chain $N_{1} \subseteq N_{2} \subseteq \cdots$, the union $N = \bigcup_{i} N_{i}$ is a submodule. By hypothesis, $N$ is finitely generated, say by $x_{1}, \dots, x_{r}$. Each $x_{j}$ lies in some $N_{i_{j}}$; let $k = \max\{i_{1}, \dots, i_{r}\}$. Then all $x_{j} \in N_{k}$, so $N = N_{k} = N_{k+1} = \cdots$. $\square$

A commutative ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if it is Noetherian as a module over itself, equivalently if every ideal is finitely generated.

**Examples 1.13.** Fields, principal ideal domains, and $\mathbb{Z}$ are Noetherian. The polynomial ring $k[x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots]$ in infinitely many variables is not: the ideal $(x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots)$ is not finitely generated, and the chain

$$(x_{1}) \subsetneq (x_{1}, x_{2}) \subsetneq (x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}) \subsetneq \cdots$$

never stabilizes.

**Theorem 1.14 (Hilbert basis theorem).** If $R$ is a commutative Noetherian ring, then $R[x]$ is Noetherian.

*Proof.* Include zero with the leading coefficients of nonzero polynomials in I to form J. It is an ideal: align degrees by powers of x before adding two polynomials, and multiply coefficients by ring scalars; a vanished coefficient contributes zero. If J=0 then I=0. Otherwise choose generators a_i of J and corresponding polynomials f_i∈I of degrees d_i. Put d=max d_i.

The polynomials of I of degree less than d form an R-submodule of R^d. A finite free module over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian, by the exact-sequence argument immediately below and induction on its rank. Hence this submodule has finite generators h_1,…,h_s. For f∈I with degree N≥d, express its leading coefficient as Σ_i r_i a_i. Subtract Σ_i r_i x^(N−d_i)f_i to obtain an element of I of strictly smaller degree. Repeating terminates when the degree is below d, where the h_j suffice. Thus the f_i and h_j generate I as an ideal. This proves the theorem, with no field or domain assumption. ∎

Iterating, $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ is Noetherian for every field $k$ and every finite $n$. The infinite-variable counterexample shows the finiteness hypothesis is essential.

**Proposition 1.15.** In a short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$, the module $B$ is Noetherian if and only if both $A$ and $C$ are Noetherian.

*Proof.* Identify A with its image in B. If B is Noetherian, chains in A are chains in B, and preimages lift chains in C to chains in B, so both A and C are Noetherian. Conversely let B_i be an ascending chain. The intersections B_i∩A stabilise because A is Noetherian, and the images q(B_i) stabilise because C is Noetherian. Choose k after both stabilisations. If x∈B_j for j≥k, choose y∈B_k with q(y)=q(x). Then x−y∈B_j∩A=B_k∩A, so x∈B_k. Thus every B_j=B_k and B is Noetherian. ∎

## 1.5 Exact sequences

A sequence of $R$-module maps

$$\cdots \to A_{i-1} \xrightarrow{f_{i-1}} A_{i} \xrightarrow{f_{i}} A_{i+1} \to \cdots$$

is **exact at $A_{i}$** if $\operatorname{im} f_{i-1} = \operatorname{ker} f_{i}$. It is **exact** if it is exact at every internal position.

A **short exact sequence** is a five-term exact sequence

$$0 \to A \xrightarrow{i} B \xrightarrow{p} C \to 0.$$

Exactness encodes three facts simultaneously: $i$ is injective, $p$ is surjective, and $\operatorname{im} i = \operatorname{ker} p$. Thus $A$ is identified with a submodule of $B$, and $C \cong B/A$.

**Worked example 1.16.** For any positive integer $n$,

$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\times n\,} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\bmod n\,} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to 0$$

is exact. Multiplication by $n$ is injective; reduction modulo $n$ is surjective; and $\operatorname{ker}(\bmod n) = n\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{im}(\times n)$.

**Worked example 1.17 (Nonsplitting).** The sequence

$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\times 2\,} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\bmod 2\,} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$$

does **not** split as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules. A splitting would require a section $s: \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ with $(\bmod 2) \circ s = \operatorname{id}$. The image of $1 \in \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ would be an integer $z$ with $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, so $z$ is odd. But $2z = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ only when $z = 0$, a contradiction. Hence $\mathbb{Z} \not\cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

This failure is the germ of extension theory: short exact sequences classify the ways $C$ can be built from $A$ with a given quotient, and the nontrivial extensions are measured by $\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{R}(C,A)$.


## Splitting and maps out of a quotient

**Theorem 2.1 (Splitting criterion).** For a short exact sequence 0→A→B→C→0 with maps i,q, the following are equivalent: q has an R-linear section; i has an R-linear retraction; and B is isomorphic to A⊕C through a map compatible with i and q.

*Proof.* If qs=id_C, the map (a,c)↦i(a)+s(c) is injective because applying q first forces c=0, then a=0. It is surjective because b−s(qb) lies in ker q=im i. Thus it gives the compatible direct sum. Conversely such a direct sum supplies both section and retraction by inclusion and projection. If a retraction r:B→A is given first, B=i(A)⊕ker r: write b=i(rb)+[b−i(rb)], and the intersection is zero. Restricting q to ker r is an isomorphism onto C, supplying the section. ∎

A free target C always gives a splitting: choose a preimage in B of each basis element and extend R-linearly. There is no corresponding statement for every finitely generated target, as the integer examples show.

For any module M,
$$\operatorname{Hom}_R(R/I,M)\cong\{m\in M:Im=0\}.$$
A map is determined by m=f(1+I), and the relation I=0 in R/I forces Im=0. Conversely such an m defines f(r+I)=rm, independently of the representative. In particular every cyclic module Rm is isomorphic to R/Ann_R(m).

**Theorem 2.2 (Left exactness of Hom).** If 0→A→B→C→0 is exact, then
$$0\to\operatorname{Hom}_R(X,A)\to\operatorname{Hom}_R(X,B)
\to\operatorname{Hom}_R(X,C)$$
is exact for every X.

*Proof.* Composing with an injection gives an injection on maps. A map X→B goes to zero in Hom(X,C) exactly when its image lies in ker q=im i. The inverse of i onto its image then gives a unique R-linear factor X→A. This identifies the kernel with the preceding image. ∎

There is generally no terminal zero: maps to C may not lift to B. For X=Z/2Z and the nonsplit quotient Z→Z/2Z, the identity on X has no lift, since every map X→Z is zero. This precise failure motivates later projective modules and derived functors, without requiring their definitions yet.

## 1.6 Common errors

- **Treating modules like vector spaces.** Over a general ring, not every submodule has a complement, not every module is free, and division by nonunits is meaningless.
- **Checking exactness at only some positions.** Exactness is a local condition; verify it at every object.
- **Assuming every short exact sequence splits.** This holds for vector spaces but fails already for $\mathbb{Z}$-modules.
- **Confusing "generated by" with "free on."** The cyclic module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by one element but is not free on one generator because the universal property fails: the map sending the generator to $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ does not extend to a $\mathbb{Z}$-linear map $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$.

Annihilators in an exact sequence obey a product containment that an intersection cannot replace. If a kills A and c kills C in an exact sequence from A into B onto C, then cb maps to zero in C for every b. It therefore belongs to the copy of A, and applying a gives acb=0. Hence the product of the two annihilator ideals is contained in the annihilator of B. For the extension with middle module Z/4, submodule generated by two, and quotient Z/2, both endpoint annihilators are generated by two. Their intersection is still generated by two and does not kill the middle module. Their product is generated by four and does kill it, exactly as proved. This illustrates how an extension can retain a longer torsion exponent than either endpoint individually, without changing exactness.

### Exercise 1.1

For an integer $n \ne 0$, show that the only $\mathbb{Z}$-module homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is the zero map.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

The map is determined by the image of the residue of one.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

That image must be killed by n in Z.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Let $f: \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ be $\mathbb{Z}$-linear. Then $f(\overline{1})$ is an integer $m$ satisfying $nm = f(n \cdot \overline{1}) = f(\overline{0}) = 0$. Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free, $m = 0$. Thus $f(\overline{k}) = km = 0$ for all $k$. $\square$

</details>

### Exercise 1.2

Prove that a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

A finite generating set supplies a quotient of R^n.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Use Proposition 1.15 to prove R^n Noetherian, then pass to the quotient.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Let $M$ be generated by $m_{1}, \dots, m_{n}$. The map $R^{n} \to M$ sending the standard basis to the generators is surjective. Since $R$ is Noetherian, $R^{n}$ is Noetherian by induction using Proposition 1.15 applied to $0 \to R \to R^{n} \to R^{n-1} \to 0$. The quotient $M$ of a Noetherian module is Noetherian. $\square$

</details>

### Exercise 1.3

For the presentation matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, compute the Smith normal form and identify the cokernel.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Start with the entry 2 by swapping columns.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Use unimodular row and column subtraction; retain determinant magnitude 24.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Swap its columns to obtain [[2,4],[6,0]], replace the second row by itself minus three times the first to obtain [[2,4],[0,−12]], replace the second column by itself minus twice the first to obtain [[2,0],[0,−12]], and negate the second row. These unimodular operations give $\operatorname{diag}(2,12)$. The cokernel is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. The invariant factors are $2$ and $12$.

</details>

### Exercise 1.4

Show that $0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$, with first map $\overline{1} \mapsto \overline{2}$ and second map reduction modulo $2$, is exact but does not split.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Identify image and kernel explicitly before discussing splitting.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compare possible element orders in a direct sum.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Exactness: the first map is injective, the second is surjective, and the image of the first is $\{\overline{0}, \overline{2}\}$, which equals the kernel of the second. If the sequence split, $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ would be isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. But $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order $4$, while every element of the direct sum has order dividing $2$. Hence no splitting exists. $\square$

</details>

### Exercise 1.5

Let $R = k[x]$ where $k$ is a field. Show that the ideal $(x^{2}, x^{3})$ is finitely generated and find a minimal generating set.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

One listed generator is a multiple of the other.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A nonzero ideal needs at least one generator.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

We claim $(x^{2}, x^{3}) = (x^{2})$. Indeed $x^{3} = x \cdot x^{2} \in (x^{2})$, so $(x^{2}, x^{3}) \subseteq (x^{2})$. The reverse inclusion is immediate. The single generator is minimal in number because the ideal is nonzero and cannot be generated by the empty set. Also $(x^{2})/(x^{3})\cong k$ confirms its first nonzero degree is two, so no polynomial of higher degree alone generates it. $\square$

</details>

### CA-02-07 — A free quotient splits

Prove every exact sequence 0→A→B→R^n→0 splits.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Lift each standard basis vector separately.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The free universal property extends those lifts.

</details>
<details>

Choose b_i∈B mapping to e_i. The map s:R^n→B, Σr_ie_i↦Σr_ib_i, is R-linear and qs=id because this holds on the basis. The splitting theorem gives B≅A⊕R^n. The chosen b_i need not be unique, so the resulting direct-sum isomorphism is generally not canonical.

### CA-02-08 — Two sections differ by a kernel map

If s,t:C→B are sections of the same quotient q in a short exact sequence, show s−t factors uniquely through A.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Apply q to their difference.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Use ker q=im i and injectivity of i.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Since qs=qt=id, q(s−t)=0. The difference therefore lands in im i, and the inverse of i onto that image defines a unique R-linear f:C→A with s−t=if. </details>

### CA-02-09 — An ideal need not be free of rank one

For R=k[x,y], show the ideal (x,y) is generated by two elements but cannot be free of rank one.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

A free rank-one ideal would have a single generating basis element.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The ideal is not principal, as proved in the diagnostic.

</details>
<details>

### CA-02-10 — Exactness of a presentation

Let M=R^n/im A for a matrix A:R^m→R^n. Explain why R^m→R^n→M→0 is exact and why A need not be injective.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

The quotient map has kernel im A by construction.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Relations among the listed relations may remain.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

The quotient map is surjective and its kernel is precisely the submodule generated by A's columns, giving exactness at R^n and M. No condition has been imposed at R^m. For example A=[0]:R→R is zero, giving cokernel R and kernel R, so it is far from injective. Adding an initial zero to the sequence would assert an extra property not contained in a general presentation.

</details>

### CA-02-11 — A Noetherian counterexample

Show the ideal (x_1,x_2,…) in k[x_1,x_2,…] is not finitely generated.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Any finite list of proposed generators involves finitely many variables.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Set those variables to zero and retain a new variable.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Suppose generators f_1,…,f_r existed. Each has zero constant term and uses variables in a common finite set, say x_1,…,x_N after relabelling. Evaluate those variables at zero while retaining x_(N+1). Every f_i maps to zero, so their generated ideal maps to zero. But x_(N+1), which belongs to the claimed ideal, maps to itself and is nonzero. This contradiction proves nonfinite generation and failure of Noetherianity.

</details>

### CA-02-12 — Hom need not preserve surjectivity

Apply Hom_Z(Z/2Z,−) to Z→Z/2Z and show the induced map is not onto.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Maps from Z/2Z to Z are all zero.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The target Hom group contains its identity map.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

The source Hom group is zero because a possible image of one must be a two-torsion integer. The target Hom group Hom(Z/2Z,Z/2Z) has two elements, zero and identity. The induced map therefore has only zero in its image and misses identity. This shows why Theorem 2.2 stops before a terminal zero unless an additional lifting property holds.

</details>

## 1.8 Looking forward

Modules generalise linear algebra while exact sequences record kernels, images and quotient relations without assuming a splitting. Noetherian finiteness gives control of submodules, and free presentations make relation data explicit. The next chapter constructs [localisations](localisation.md) and proves that localising preserves exact sequences. Homological methods later organise the failures of exactness for other operations.
