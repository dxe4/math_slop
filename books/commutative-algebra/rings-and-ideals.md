
# Rings and Ideals

An ideal can encode equations, a quotient, a divisibility condition or an infinitesimal thickening. Prime ideals detect domain quotients, but they do not see every nilpotent element individually. The radical records exactly what survives if we ask only which primes contain an ideal. This chapter proves that relationship before introducing localisations and spectra.

Read the algebra book's [rings and ideals](#/books/algebra-patterns-structure/ring-homomorphisms-ideals-quotients) and [polynomial rings](#/books/algebra-patterns-structure/polynomial-rings-factorization). All rings here are commutative with identity; homomorphisms preserve identity. The zero ring is allowed, but fields and domains have 1≠0, and prime or maximal ideals are always proper.

## 1. Ideals and quotient criteria

An ideal I≤R is an additive subgroup satisfying rI⊆I for every r∈R. The ideal generated by a set A is the set of finite sums Σ_i r_i a_i with a_i∈A. An element of the generated ideal uses only finitely many generators even when A is infinite.

The quotient R/I has multiplication (a+I)(b+I)=ab+I. Replacing the representatives changes the product by an element of I because I absorbs multiplication. The quotient map has kernel I. Conversely every kernel of a unital ring homomorphism is an ideal, and the map R/kerφ→imφ sending a+kerφ to φ(a) is a well-defined bijective homomorphism.

**Proposition 1.1.** For a proper ideal I:
(1) I is prime exactly when R/I is a domain;
(2) I is maximal exactly when R/I is a field;
(3) a+I is a unit exactly when (a)+I=R.

*Proof.* Nonzero quotient classes multiply to zero exactly when ab∈I with neither a nor b in I. Excluding that possibility is the prime condition.

For maximality, ideals of R/I correspond to ideals of R containing I, by inverse image and quotient. A nonzero commutative ring has only the ideals zero and itself exactly when it is a field: every nonzero a then has (a)=R, providing an inverse; conversely every ideal containing a nonzero field element contains 1. This proves (2).

Finally a+I is invertible exactly when some b satisfies ab−1∈I, equivalently 1∈(a)+I. ∎

Every maximal ideal is prime because fields are domains. A prime ideal need not be maximal: (0) in Z has domain quotient Z, which is not a field.

An ideal is all R exactly when it contains 1. More generally any ideal containing a unit is all R. These observations are repeatedly useful when showing a constructed ideal remains proper.

## 2. Operations and the quotient correspondence

For ideals I,J define
$$I+J=\{i+j:i\in I,j\in J\},\qquad
IJ=\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^m i_\ell j_\ell\right\}.$$
These are ideals, as are arbitrary intersections. The product is the ideal generated by pairwise products and need not consist of single products. Always
$$IJ\subseteq I\cap J\subseteq I,J\subseteq I+J.$$
If I+J=R, choose i+j=1. For x∈I∩J, write x=xi+xj; both terms lie in IJ. Hence I∩J=IJ for comaximal ideals.

The colon ideal is (I:J)={r:rJ⊆I}. Additive closure and multiplication absorption follow directly. For a single element a we write (I:a). In particular Ann_R(a)=(0:a). Colon ideals record which multipliers force a membership condition.

For the quotient q:R→R/I, ideals K≥I correspond to ideals K/I≤R/I. In one direction inverse image preserves ideal operations; in the other direction q^(−1)(K/I)=K. Every quotient ideal L has q(q^(−1)L)=L because q is surjective. This proves bijectivity and order preservation. It also shows primes and maximal ideals correspond, using the isomorphic further quotients (R/I)/(K/I)≅R/K.

For a general map φ:R→S, contraction φ^(−1)(J) is always an ideal. The extension IS is the ideal generated by φ(I), not merely its set-theoretic image. Contracting a prime gives a prime, since ab mapping into the prime forces one factor there and 1 does not map into it. Contracting a maximal ideal need not give a maximal ideal: (0) is maximal in Q but contracts to nonmaximal (0) in Z. Extension can turn a proper ideal into the whole target: (2)Q=Q.

## 3. Radicals and nilpotents

**Definition 1.2.** The radical of I is
$$\sqrt I=\{a\in R:a^m\in I\text{ for some integer }m\geq1\}.$$
An ideal is radical when √I=I. The nilradical is √(0), the set of nilpotent elements. A ring is reduced when its nilradical is zero.

**Proposition 1.3.** √I is an ideal, √(√I)=√I, and
$$\sqrt{IJ}=\sqrt{I\cap J}=\sqrt I\cap\sqrt J.$$

*Proof.* If a^m∈I and b^n∈I, every term in (a+b)^(m+n−1) contains either a^m or b^n, so the sum lies in I. Negatives and scalar multiples satisfy the required power condition as well. Thus √I is an ideal. If a^r∈√I, some power (a^r)^s lies in I, proving a∈√I and idempotence.

The containments IJ⊆I∩J⊆I,J give √(IJ)⊆√(I∩J)⊆√I∩√J. Conversely if a^m∈I and a^n∈J, then a^(m+n)∈IJ. This closes the chain of containments. ∎

Prime ideals are radical: if a^m∈𝔭, repeated primality gives a∈𝔭. The radical quotient criterion is
$$R/I\text{ reduced}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I\text{ radical},$$
because a+I is nilpotent exactly when a∈√I.

A nilpotent element maps to zero under every map to a domain. Its image has a zero power, and domain cancellation rules out a nonzero nilpotent. The following theorem says that the prime quotients together detect every element outside the nilradical.

## 4. Prime intersection via a maximal-avoidance argument

**Lemma 1.4.** Let S⊆R be multiplicatively closed, with 1∈S. If an ideal I is disjoint from S, some prime 𝔭 contains I and is disjoint from S.

*Proof.* Order the ideals containing I and disjoint from S by inclusion. The family is nonempty. The union of a chain is an ideal: any two elements belong together to one chain member, which supplies addition and scalar closure. The union remains disjoint from S. Zorn's lemma therefore gives a maximal member J in this family. It is proper because 1∈S.

Suppose ab∈J but a,b∉J. The strictly larger ideals J+(a) and J+(b) must meet S by maximality. Choose s=j+ra and t=k+ub in those intersections. Then
st=jk+jub+rak+ruab∈J.
But st∈S by multiplicative closure, contradicting disjointness. Thus J is prime. ∎

The argument explicitly uses Zorn's lemma. No Noetherian hypothesis makes a finite ascending search available here.

**Theorem 1.5 (Radical as prime intersection).**
$$\sqrt I=\bigcap_{\substack{\mathfrak p\text{ prime}\\I\subseteq\mathfrak p}}\mathfrak p.$$

*Proof.* Every containing prime is radical, so contains √I. Conversely, if a∉√I, the set S={1,a,a²,…} is disjoint from I. The lemma supplies a prime containing I and avoiding S, so in particular avoiding a. Hence a does not lie in the displayed intersection. ∎

For I=R, no prime contains I; the intersection of the empty family of subsets of R is conventionally R, making the formula valid. In the zero ring the same convention applies.

Every proper ideal also lies in a maximal ideal: apply Zorn to proper containing ideals. A chain union remains proper because if it contained 1, one chain member would contain 1. A maximal proper ideal is maximal in the usual sense. This simpler existence theorem will support local-ring arguments.

## 5. Worked example: integer residues

In Z/12Z, ideals correspond to ideals (d) of Z containing (12), so d runs through positive divisors of twelve. The prime ideals are (2) and (3), with quotient fields F_2,F_3. Their intersection is the ideal (6), consisting of residues 0 and 6.

Thus the nilradical is (6). Directly, 6²=36=0 modulo twelve, while any nilpotent must vanish modulo both two and three and hence be divisible by six. The ring is not reduced.

Its units are 1,5,7,11, by the gcd criterion. The two nonunits 2 and 3 sum to the unit 5, so the nonunits do not form an ideal. Later this will distinguish a ring with multiple maximal ideals from a local ring.

For comparison Z/6Z has prime ideals (2),(3) whose intersection is zero, so it is reduced despite having zero divisors. Reduced means no nonzero nilpotents; it does not mean domain.

## 6. Worked example: two crossing coordinate axes

In k[x,y], let I=(x),J=(y). A polynomial lies in both exactly when every monomial is divisible by both x and y, so
$$I\cap J=(xy)=IJ.$$
Here I+J=(x,y) is proper, as evaluation at (0,0) kills it. Thus equality of product and intersection does not imply comaximality; the earlier implication has no converse.

The ideals (x),(y) are prime because their quotients are polynomial domains k[y],k[x]. Their intersection (xy) is therefore radical. But (x²y) is not radical: xy is not in it while (xy)²=x²y² is. Its radical is (xy), by the radical-product formula.

The quotient by (xy) has two nonzero classes x,y whose product is zero, so is not a domain. It is nevertheless reduced. This algebraic crossing is the basic example behind a reducible reduced geometric object.

## 7. Worked example: infinitesimal information

Let R=k[ε]/(ε²). Every element is uniquely a+bε. It is a unit exactly when a≠0; then
$$(a+b\varepsilon)^{-1}=a^{-1}-ba^{-2}\varepsilon.$$
If a=0, its square is zero and it cannot be a unit in the nonzero ring.

Every prime contains ε because ε²=0. The quotient by (ε) is k, so (ε) is the unique prime and unique maximal ideal. Its square is zero, while the ideal itself is nonzero. Therefore the prime set alone does not distinguish this ring from the field k; both have one prime. The nilpotent thickening is additional ring information.

This example will reappear when we discuss spectra: a topological space of primes must be supplemented by algebraic structure if it is to remember infinitesimals.

## 8. Jacobson radical and a unit criterion

The Jacobson radical Jac(R) is the intersection of all maximal ideals, with the same empty-intersection convention. It contains the nilradical, since maximal ideals are prime. Equality need not hold.

**Theorem 1.6.**
$$x\in\operatorname{Jac}(R)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
1-rx\text{ is a unit for every }r\in R.$$

*Proof.* Suppose x lies in every maximal ideal. If 1−rx were a nonunit, its generated proper ideal would lie in a maximal ideal 𝔪. But x∈𝔪 gives rx∈𝔪, so 1=(1−rx)+rx∈𝔪, a contradiction.

Conversely, if x∉𝔪 for a maximal ideal, its nonzero class in the field R/𝔪 has an inverse. Choose r lifting that inverse. Then 1−rx∈𝔪, so it cannot be a unit. This contradicts the proposed unit condition. ∎

**Worked example.** In k[[t]], the ring of formal power series, a series is a unit precisely when its constant term is nonzero: its inverse coefficients are solved recursively from the product equation. Thus the nonunits form (t), the unique maximal ideal, and Jac(R)=(t). The ring is a domain, since the first nonzero coefficients of two nonzero series have nonzero product. Hence its nilradical is zero. This proves the two radicals can differ even in a familiar domain.

## Comaximal quotients and idempotents

The Chinese remainder map gives another interpretation of ideal operations. If I+J=R, then
$$R/IJ\longrightarrow R/I\times R/J,\qquad
r+IJ\longmapsto(r+I,r+J)$$
is an isomorphism. Its kernel before quotienting is I∩J=IJ. For surjectivity, choose i+j=1 with i∈I,j∈J. The element aj+bi maps to any prescribed pair (a+I,b+J). This supplies the actual inverse rather than merely comparing cardinalities.

The two product factors correspond to orthogonal idempotents j+IJ and i+IJ: their product is zero and their sum is one. Thus a decomposition into comaximal quotient conditions also decomposes the ring into algebraic components.

For example, take I=(4),J=(3) in Z, with i=4,j=−3. The residue pair (a mod 4,b mod 3) is represented modulo twelve by −3a+4b. The nilpotent pair (2 mod 4,0 mod 3) corresponds to 6 mod 12. This recovers the earlier nilradical calculation through the product Z/4Z×Z/3Z, showing how quotient decomposition preserves rather than discards nilpotent information.

The Jacobson radical and nilradical can differ even in a familiar domain. Localise the integers at the prime p, allowing exactly denominators not divisible by p. Its unique maximal ideal consists of fractions whose numerator is divisible by p, so p belongs to its Jacobson radical. The ring is a domain, however, and p is nonzero, so p is not nilpotent and the nilradical is zero. The unit criterion also checks this directly: for any fraction a with denominator prime to p, the numerator of one minus ap remains nonzero modulo p, making that difference a unit in the local ring. Thus the property that every one minus a multiple is invertible does not imply that the element itself is nilpotent. The two radicals measure different kinds of obstruction, and they coincide only under additional hypotheses such as the zero-dimensional Noetherian situation developed later.

### CA-01-01 — Comaximal intersection

Prove I∩J=IJ when I+J=R.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Choose i+j=1.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Multiply that identity by an element in the intersection.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Always IJ⊆I∩J by ideal absorption. For x∈I∩J, write x=x(i+j)=xi+xj. Since x∈J and i∈I, xi∈IJ; since x∈I and j∈J, xj∈IJ. Thus x∈IJ and equality follows. The argument uses commutativity only to match the usual symmetric ideal product notation.

### CA-01-02 — A radical integer ideal

Find √(72) in Z.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Factor 72 into prime powers.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A power of a is divisible by 72 exactly when a is divisible by both prime factors.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Since 72=2³·3², a^m can be divisible by 72 only when both two and three divide a. Conversely, if six divides a, a³ is divisible by 2³·3³ and hence by 72. Thus √(72)=(6). The containing primes are (2),(3), whose intersection is also (6), agreeing with the prime-intersection theorem.

</details>

### CA-01-03 — A nonradical quotient

Show k[x]/(x³) is not reduced and identify its nilradical.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use the radical of (x³) upstairs.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A class with nonzero constant term cannot have a power in (x³).

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

The radical of (x³) is (x). A polynomial divisible by x has a sufficiently high power divisible by x³; a polynomial with nonzero constant term has every power with nonzero constant term and cannot. The quotient nilradical is therefore (x)/(x³). It is nonzero, containing the class of x, whose cube is zero. Hence the quotient is not reduced.

</details>

### CA-01-04 — A prime that is not maximal

Prove (y−x²) is prime but not maximal in k[x,y].

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Substitute y=x².

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Polynomial division by the monic polynomial y−x² identifies the kernel.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

The map k[x,y]→k[x], f↦f(x,x²), is surjective. Division in y by y−x² writes f=q(y−x²)+r(x), so its kernel is exactly (y−x²). The quotient is k[x], a domain but not a field. Therefore the ideal is prime and not maximal. The argument works over every field k.

</details>

### CA-01-05 — A colon computation

Compute ((x²,y):x) in k[x,y].

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Reduce modulo y.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

In k[x], the condition becomes xf divisible by x².

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

If xf∈(x²,y), reducing modulo y gives x f(x,0)∈(x²), so f(x,0) is divisible by x. Hence f lies in (x,y). Conversely, multiplying either generator x or y by x lands in (x²,y). Thus the colon ideal is (x,y). Working in the quotient k[x,y]/(y) made the divisibility condition precise.

</details>

### CA-01-06 — Contraction and extension

For Z→Q show extension of (3) is Q and contraction of (0) is (0). Explain the prime/maximal distinction.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Three is invertible in Q.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The inclusion map is injective.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

The extended ideal contains 3 and therefore (1/3)·3=1, so is all Q. The contraction of zero is the inclusion kernel, which is zero. The zero ideal is maximal in the field Q but only prime in Z, because Z is a domain rather than a field. Thus maximality need not survive contraction, and properness need not survive extension.

</details>

### CA-01-07 — Nilradical of a product

Show √(0) in R×S equals √(0_R)×√(0_S).

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Powers are computed componentwise.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

If the two components have different nilpotence exponents, take their maximum.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

If (r,s)^n=(0,0), both r and s are nilpotent. Conversely if r^a=0 and s^b=0, choosing n≥max(a,b) gives (r,s)^n=0. Thus the nilpotent pairs are exactly the product of the nilradicals. In particular a product of reduced rings is reduced, even though it has zero divisors when both factors are nonzero.

</details>

### CA-01-08 — Intersection versus sum of radicals

Prove √(I+J)=√(√I+√J).

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

One inclusion follows from I+J⊆√I+√J.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Both √I and √J lie in √(I+J).

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Monotonicity gives √(I+J)⊆√(√I+√J). Conversely, since I,J⊆I+J, both √I and √J are contained in the radical ideal √(I+J). Their sum is contained there, and taking radicals preserves that inclusion while leaving the target unchanged. This gives the reverse containment and equality.

</details>

### CA-01-09 — Primes avoid nonnilpotents

Let a be nonnilpotent in R. Prove some prime does not contain a, and identify the multiplicative set used.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use all nonnegative powers of a.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Nonnilpotence says that set avoids zero.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Set S={1,a,a²,…}. Since no positive power of a is zero and 1≠0 in this situation, S∩(0)=∅. The maximal-avoidance lemma gives a prime containing (0) and disjoint from S, so it excludes a. Thus primes collectively detect every element outside the nilradical. The proof uses Zorn's lemma and does not provide a finite search algorithm in an arbitrary ring.

</details>

### CA-01-10 — Nilpotents lie in the Jacobson radical

Prove directly that a nilpotent x satisfies the unit criterion for Jac(R).

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

If x^n=0, then (rx)^n=0.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Use a finite geometric sum to invert 1−rx.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

For every r, commutativity gives (rx)^n=r^nx^n=0. Therefore
(1−rx)[1+rx+⋯+(rx)^(n−1)]=1−(rx)^n=1.
So 1−rx is a unit for every r. The Jacobson criterion gives x∈Jac(R). This also proves the inclusion of the nilradical without invoking the fact that maximal ideals are prime.

</details>

### CA-01-11 — A unit lifts through a nilpotent ideal

Suppose I^n=0 and a+I is a unit in R/I. Prove a is a unit in R.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Choose b with ab=1−i, i∈I.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The element i is nilpotent, so 1−i has a geometric-series inverse.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

The quotient inverse gives ab=1−i for some i∈I. Since I^n=0, i^n=0, and c=1+i+⋯+i^(n−1) satisfies (1−i)c=1. Thus a(bc)=1. In a commutative ring this is a two-sided inverse, so a is a unit. Without a nilpotence or Jacobson-radical hypothesis, an invertible quotient class need not lift to a unit.

</details>

### CA-01-12 — Equal prime containment

For ideals I,J show the same primes contain them exactly when √I=√J.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Every prime containing an ideal contains its radical.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Take intersections of the containing primes.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

If the same primes contain I and J, the prime-intersection theorem gives equal radicals. Conversely a prime contains I exactly when it contains √I, since it is radical; the same holds for J. Equality of the radicals therefore gives equality of their containing-prime sets. This equivalence explains precisely which ideal information is retained by a set of prime vanishing conditions.

</details>

## 10. Summary and next connection

Ideal operations organise quotient conditions; radicals record powers, and the prime-intersection theorem identifies their exact relation to all prime quotients. Nilpotent and Jacobson radicals capture different phenomena, as the residue, crossing and formal-series examples show.
