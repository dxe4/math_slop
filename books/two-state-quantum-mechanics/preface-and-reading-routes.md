
# Preface and Reading Routes {#section-1-preface-and-reading-routes}

Mathematical quantum mechanics is not a collection of mysterious formulas. It is a disciplined relation among state spaces, observables, dynamics, probability laws, preparations, and experiments. This book begins with finite matrices, where every operation can be checked, then moves to wave mechanics, composite systems, open dynamics, approximation, and numerical evidence.

<a id="section-3-who-this-book-is-for"></a>
## Who this book is for {#section-5-who-this-book-is-for}

The L3 main route assumes [linear algebra](#/books/linear-algebra-structure-proofs), [real analysis](#/books/proof-safe-real-analysis), and [ordinary differential equations](#/books/ode-first-course), with elementary probability used locally. Complex inner products, adjoints, spectral projectors, tensor products, and domains are developed where needed. All twenty-two stable chapter identities carry substantive sources with at least three worked examples and twenty supported exercises.    A physics reader should use the diagnostic and treat unfamiliar domain language as part of the claim, rather than as decoration.

<a id="section-7-sequential-route"></a>
## Sequential route {#section-9-sequential-route}

Read Chapters 1–8 for the finite-dimensional core: complex state spaces, spectral measurement, two-state geometry, unitary evolution, pictures of motion, uncertainty, symmetry, and density operators. Chapters 9–13 build spatial wave mechanics from $L^2$, operator domains, bound states, oscillators, central potentials, and scattering. Chapters 14–18 develop composite systems, identical particles, generalized measurements, open dynamics, and Bell experiments. Chapters 19–22 treat approximation, computation, and foundations.

- **Evidence and interpretation:** Read each chapter’s boundary section, then Chapters 18 and 22.

<a id="section-20-how-to-work-a-chapter"></a>
## How to work a chapter {#section-22-how-to-work-a-chapter}

First identify the state space, domain, units, and named approximation. Reproduce one worked calculation without looking. Attempt exercises before solutions and maintain an error ledger: conjugation, normalization, basis, sign, units, domain, and physical scope. For a project, submit a text or numerical alternative to every visual and distinguish exact identities from floating-point evidence.

<a id="section-24-conventions-of-honesty"></a>
## Conventions of honesty {#section-26-conventions-of-honesty}

An ideal measurement is not a detector derivation. A unitary matrix is not a hardware pulse without calibration. A tiny numerical residual is not physical validation. A reduced density matrix does not identify its preparation mechanism. A Bell violation tests a defined local model under experimental assumptions. An interpretation label is not a mathematical proof.

<a id="section-28-suggested-course-designs"></a>
## Suggested course designs {#section-30-suggested-course-designs}

A one-semester course can cover Chapters 1–8, select either the spatial route 9–13 or composite route 14–18, and end with Chapters 19, 21, and a capstone. A two-semester course can complete all chapters, pairing each part with one computational or analytical project. Assessment should mix derivation, counterexample, dimensional audit, complete solutions, and evidence reporting rather than reward formula recall alone.

<a id="section-32-what-comes-next"></a>
## What comes next {#section-34-what-comes-next}

After this volume, continue to functional analysis and spectral theory for unbounded operators; representation theory for symmetry; PDE and scattering theory for spatial dynamics; operator algebras and quantum information for composite systems; and statistical mechanics or quantum field theory for many-body and relativistic structure. Use the final reference map to distinguish textbooks, mathematical references, and empirical standards.
