
# Unitary Dynamics and Driven Two-Level Systems

Closed-system quantum dynamics is linear, norm preserving, and generated by a Hamiltonian. In a two-state model the entire calculation can be displayed: matrix exponentials become rotations, detuning competes with drive strength, and measured oscillations expose both model power and model failure. We keep energy, angular frequency, approximation status, and observation protocol distinct throughout.

## 1. Schrödinger evolution

For a state \(\lvert\psi(t)\rangle\) and Hermitian Hamiltonian \(H(t)\),

\[
i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=H(t)\lvert\psi(t)\rangle.
\]

\(H\) has units of energy and \(H/\hbar\) inverse time. Differentiating the norm gives

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi\vert\psi\rangle
=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\psi\vert H\vert\psi\rangle
-\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\psi\vert H\vert\psi\rangle=0.
\]

Thus Hermiticity conserves normalization. This equation models a closed effective system. Environmental coupling, measurement records, and uncertain controls require additional structure.

**Theorem 4.1 (Finite-dimensional existence).** For a piecewise-continuous Hermitian matrix $H(t)$ on a compact time interval and $\hbar>0$, the initial-value equation has a unique propagator $U(t,s)$. It is continuous across coefficient switches, differentiable between them, and unitary. It obeys $U(t,s)U(s,r)=U(t,r)$ and $U(t,s)^{-1}=U(s,t)$.

*Proof.* Write $K(t)=-iH(t)/\hbar$ and solve the Volterra equation $U(t,s)=I+\int_s^tK(r)U(r,s)dr$ by iteration. Its $k$th ordered integral is bounded in operator norm by $[\int_s^t\|K(r)\|dr]^k/k!$. The resulting series converges uniformly, and substitution in the integral equation is justified by this bound. It is a solution. The difference of two solutions satisfies an integral inequality with integrable coefficient $\|K\|$; Gronwall makes it zero. Differentiating $U^\dagger U$ on each continuous piece gives zero because $K^\dagger=-K$. Continuity and $U(s,s)=I$ imply $U^\dagger U=I$ everywhere. In finite dimensions this also gives $UU^\dagger=I$. Composition follows by uniqueness with data at the intermediate time; the inverse follows from composition. The same construction backward in time defines the reversed propagators. Thus time ordering names a convergent, specified solution construction here, rather than an unexplained symbol.

Conversely, a differentiable unitary family has a Hermitian instantaneous generator $H=i\hbar\dot UU^\dagger$. Indeed differentiation of $UU^\dagger=I$ gives $U\dot U^\dagger=-\dot UU^\dagger$, so $H^\dagger=-i\hbar U\dot U^\dagger=H$. This converse concerns a chosen continuous unitary evolution, not every abstract transformation that preserves transition probabilities. Antiunitary symmetries have a different role and are not generated by this ordinary complex-linear Schrödinger equation.

## 2. Time-independent propagators

For constant \(H\),

\[
U(t)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{iHt}{\hbar}\right),
\qquad \lvert\psi(t)\rangle=U(t)\lvert\psi(0)\rangle.
\]

Spectral calculus gives \(U(t)=\sum_a \exp(-iE_at/\hbar)P_a\), immediately proving unitarity. Adding a constant energy \(cI\) multiplies every state by global phase \(\exp(-ict/\hbar)\) and changes no closed-system Born probability.

For \(H=a_0I+\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), let \(\omega=2\lVert\mathbf a\rVert/\hbar\) and \(\mathbf n=\mathbf a/\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\). Then

\[
U(t)=e^{-ia_0t/\hbar}\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)I
-i\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\right].
\]

The Bloch vector rotates by angle \(\omega t\) about \(\mathbf n\).

If $\mathbf a=0$, the axis formula is undefined, but the propagator is simply $e^{-ia_0t/\hbar}I$. The ray is stationary. If $\mathbf a\ne0$, the stated exponential follows by separating even and odd powers of $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, whose square is $I$. The scalar and traceless parts commute, allowing their factors to be separated. A real scalar shift depending on time likewise contributes $\exp[-i\int c(t)dt/\hbar]$ to every solution, even if the remaining Hamiltonian is time dependent. A relative shift between two levels instead changes their phase difference and can change interference probabilities.

## 3. Resonant two-state oscillation

For positive pulse durations in the formulas below take $\Omega>0$. A negative drive coefficient reverses the rotation direction; the population period uses $|\Omega|$. At zero drive the state does not transfer, so a finite $\pi$-pulse duration is unavailable.

Take \(H=(\hbar\Omega/2)X\). Since \(X^2=I\),

\[
U(t)=\cos\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right)I
-i\sin\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right)X.
\]

Starting in \(\lvert0\rangle\),

\[
\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=\cos\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right)\lvert0\rangle
-i\sin\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right)\lvert1\rangle,
\]

so \(p_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)\). A \(\pi\) pulse has duration \(\pi/\Omega\) and transfers the ideal state completely. If \(\Omega=2\pi\times1\,\mathrm{MHz}\), that duration is \(0.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}\).

The formula assumes a stable effective drive, correct rotating description, negligible leakage, and coherence across the pulse. It is not a universal description of a laboratory waveform.

## 4. Detuning and incomplete transfer

An effective driven Hamiltonian often has form

\[
H=\frac{\hbar}{2}(\Omega X+\Delta Z).
\]

Define \(\Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\). Starting in \(\lvert0\rangle\),

\[
p_1(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega_R^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2}\right).
\]

Detuning \(\Delta\) tilts the rotation axis away from the equator. The oscillation frequency increases to \(\Omega_R\), but the maximum transfer falls to \(\Omega^2/\Omega_R^2\). A faster oscillation does not imply a better population inversion.

For $\Omega_R>0$, the full state, not just its population, is

$$|\psi(t)\rangle=\left[\cos\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2}-i\frac\Delta{\Omega_R}\sin\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2}\right]|0\rangle
-i\frac\Omega{\Omega_R}\sin\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2}|1\rangle.$$

This follows by applying the constant-Hamiltonian exponential to $|0\rangle$, using $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$ and $Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$. Taking its second squared magnitude proves the transfer formula, while the first squared magnitude completes normalization. At $\Omega=\Delta=0$, use $U=I$ instead of a ratio with zero denominator. At $\Omega=0$, $\Delta\ne0$, the initial basis state only gains phase; a “first transition maximum” of a constantly zero population has no unique meaning.

### Worked audit

If \(\Delta=\Omega\), then \(\Omega_R=\sqrt2\,\Omega\) and the maximum transition probability is \(1/2\). The first maximum occurs at \(t=\pi/(\sqrt2\,\Omega)\). Both statements are dimensionally consistent only when \(\Omega\) and \(\Delta\) are angular frequencies.

## 5. Time ordering

The earlier Larmor example uses the opposite Hamiltonian sign, $H=-\hbar\omega Z/2$, with $\omega=\gamma B$ under a specified gyromagnetic convention. Starting from $|+\rangle$, its state is $(e^{i\omega t/2}|0\rangle+e^{-i\omega t/2}|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and its Bloch vector is $(\cos\omega t,-\sin\omega t,0)$. Its length remains one; the $X$ expectation alone does not reveal the precession orientation. This preserves that worked example and its corrected negative-$Y$ sign, while distinguishing it from the positive-$Z$ Hamiltonian used in the picture chapter. A quoted positive frequency magnitude without the Hamiltonian sign is insufficient to fix the rotation direction.

For piecewise-continuous finite Hermitian \(H(t)\), pairwise commutation \([H(t),H(s)]=0\) is sufficient for \(U(t,0)=\exp[-(i/\hbar)\int_0^t H(s)\,ds]\), not necessary for this fixed-start formula. For a counterexample choose \(T>0\), set \(\tau=t/T\) and \(H(t)=(\hbar/T)K(\tau)\), with \(K=X,-X,Z\) on \([0,1),[1,2),[2,3]\), respectively. The ordered propagator, with continuous matching at the switches, is \(e^{-i\tau X}\) for \(0\le\tau\le1\), \(e^{-i(2-\tau)X}\) for \(1\le\tau\le2\), and \(e^{-i(\tau-2)Z}\) for \(2\le\tau\le3\): the first two pulses cancel. These equal \(\exp[-i\int_0^\tau K(s)\,ds]\) at every prefix, although \([X,Z]\ne0\). Generally the propagator solves

\[
i\hbar\frac{dU}{dt}=H(t)U(t),\qquad U(0)=I,
\]

and is written as a time-ordered exponential. Piecewise-constant controls give an ordered product

\[
U=U_m\cdots U_2U_1.
\]

The rightmost pulse acts first. Swapping noncommuting pulses changes the result.

## 6. Conservation and the Ehrenfest identity

For a time-independent observable \(A\) and constant \(H\),

\[
\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,A]\rangle.
\]

Therefore \([H,A]=0\) is sufficient for conservation of its expectation and, more strongly, of its entire spectral distribution under the evolution. If \(A\) depends explicitly on time, add \(\langle\partial A/\partial t\rangle\).

For \(H=(\hbar\Omega/2)X\), the \(X\) distribution is conserved while \(Y\) and \(Z\) expectations rotate. Starting from \(\lvert0\rangle\), the Bloch vector is \((0,-\sin(\Omega t),\cos(\Omega t))\) under the stated sign convention.

To prove the expectation identity, differentiate $\langle\psi|A(t)|\psi\rangle$ in all three positions and insert the Schrödinger equation and its adjoint. The two state derivatives give $i\langle HA-AH\rangle/\hbar$, while the middle term gives $\langle\dot A\rangle$. The formula remains true for time-dependent Hermitian $H(t)$. If $A$ is fixed and commutes with every instantaneous $H(t)$, its spectral projectors commute too: for distinct finite eigenvalues each $P_a$ is the polynomial $\prod_{b\ne a}(A-bI)/(a-b)$. Differentiation of $U^\dagger P_aU$ gives zero. Hence every outcome probability, not merely its expectation, is conserved. By contrast a constant expectation on one particular initial state does not establish operator commutation: an energy eigenstate is a stationary ray and gives constant fixed-observable expectations even for observables that fail to commute with $H$.

## 7. Composition and control

A rotation is \(R_{\mathbf n}(\alpha)=\exp(-i\alpha\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2)\). Rotations about the same axis add angles. Rotations about different axes generally do not commute. For example,

\(R_x(\pi)R_z(\pi)\) and \(R_z(\pi)R_x(\pi)\) differ by a global sign in this special case, so they act identically on isolated rays; at other angles the difference changes the Bloch rotation. Global phase can become relative phase in a controlled composite operation, so scope matters.

Control design asks for a pulse sequence producing a target unitary within an error tolerance and a hardware model. A proof that an ideal matrix product equals the target is only the algebraic layer. Bandwidth, timing, calibration, leakage, decoherence, and robustness belong to the implementation layer.

### Worked example: pulse order that changes a ray

Start at the north-pole state $|0\rangle$. Apply $R_x(\pi/2)$ and then $R_z(\pi/2)$. The first gives $(|0\rangle-i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$; the second gives $e^{-i\pi/4}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, the plus $X$ ray. In the opposite order the $Z$ rotation initially supplies only $e^{-i\pi/4}$, and the final ray is $(|0\rangle-i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, the minus $Y$ ray. A final $X$ measurement is certain plus in the first sequence and gives equal signs in the second. These final rays cannot differ merely by global phase because their component ratios differ. The special global-sign cancellation for two $\pi$ rotations above must therefore not be generalized to all angles. Chronological order is part of the mathematical control specification.

### Worked example: a bounded Hamiltonian modeling error

Let two Hermitian Hamiltonians $H(t)$ and $G(t)$ act on the same finite space and start from the same unit vector. Differentiating $U_H(t,s)U_G(s,0)$ with respect to $s$ and integrating gives the Duhamel identity

$$U_H(t,0)-U_G(t,0)=-\frac i\hbar\int_0^tU_H(t,s)[H(s)-G(s)]U_G(s,0)\,ds.$$

Unitary factors have operator norm one, so

$$\|U_H(t,0)-U_G(t,0)\|\le\frac1\hbar\int_0^t\|H(s)-G(s)\|\,ds.$$

For a constant detuning error $H-G=\hbar\delta Z/2$, the bound is $|\delta|t/2$. Any fixed projector's probability discrepancy is at most twice this state-vector discrepancy, by writing $\langle x,Px\rangle-\langle y,Py\rangle=\langle x-y,Px\rangle+\langle y,P(x-y)\rangle$ and applying Cauchy–Schwarz. Thus a small frequency error needs a time scale before it becomes a small prediction error. This is an upper bound, not a claim of saturation: a scalar global-phase error can make the vector bound nonzero while changing no Born probability. The estimate compares two declared Hamiltonian models; it does not certify that either matches an apparatus or that a numerical approximation has achieved this bound.

## 8. Numerical propagation checks

A numerical integrator should be audited against exact properties:

- norm or unitarity residual;
- convergence under smaller time steps;
- agreement with a solvable constant-Hamiltonian case;
- dimensions of every parameter;
- population bounds and sum;
- sensitivity to detuning and pulse error.

Naive forward Euler applied to Schrödinger's equation is not unitary. For an eigenmode with real energy \(E\), a real step \(\Delta t\), and \(\hbar>0\), one step multiplies by \(1-iE\,\Delta t/\hbar\), whose squared modulus is \(1+(E\,\Delta t/\hbar)^2\ge1\), with strict growth exactly when \(E\,\Delta t\ne0\). Equality holds at zero energy or zero step. Crank–Nicolson or exact step exponentials preserve norm for Hermitian constant steps.

## 10. Failure clinic

- Mixing frequency \(f\) with angular frequency \(\Omega=2\pi f\).
- Dropping time ordering for noncommuting controls without verifying the resulting propagator.
- Interpreting a global energy shift as a change in isolated outcome probabilities.
- Assuming a Hermitian two-state Hamiltonian models decoherence.
- Inferring drive strength from oscillation frequency while ignoring detuning.
- Calling a numerical curve evidence without residual and step-size checks.
- Treating an ideal pulse identity as a hardware-fidelity guarantee.

### 17. Stationary probability
Why does an energy eigenstate have time-independent measurement probabilities for every fixed projector only when the state gains a global phase?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

An energy eigenstate acquires only one scalar phase.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

For the converse use the fixed projector onto the initial state, whose probability is initially one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Full solution</summary>

Evolution multiplies that eigenvector by \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\). Every amplitude against a fixed vector gains the same phase, so every squared magnitude is unchanged.

For the converse stationary-ray claim, include the fixed projector onto the initial normalized state among “every projector.” Its probability starts at one, so if all probabilities remain unchanged then $|\langle\psi(0)|\psi(t)\rangle|^2=1$. The equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz makes $\psi(t)$ a unit-modulus multiple of $\psi(0)$. For constant $H$, differentiating the Schrödinger equation at the initial time then requires $H\psi(0)$ to lie in that same ray, so the initial state is an energy eigenvector. One fixed population alone would not imply this conclusion.

</details>

Hermitian Hamiltonians generate unitary motion. Two-state exponentials are Bloch rotations, resonant driving produces complete oscillations, and detuning tilts the axis and limits transfer. Time-dependent noncommuting controls generally require order, conservation follows from commutators, and numerical or laboratory claims demand audits beyond exact matrix algebra.
