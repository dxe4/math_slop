
# Finite Fields and Their Galois Groups

Finite fields turn root permutations into a canonical map: taking a power. Once the base field has q elements, the q-power Frobenius generates every finite extension's Galois group. This makes subfields, intersections and irreducible factor degrees computable through divisors rather than an arbitrary subgroup search.

Read [separability](separability-primitive-element.md) and the [finite correspondence](fundamental-theorem-galois-theory.md) first. We fix a finite field F_q of q=p^r elements and an algebraic closure containing it. Every subfield and extension below is taken inside that closure unless a different placement is specified. Field size, characteristic, extension degree and multiplicative order are separate quantities.

## 1. Constructing a field from a complete root set

**Theorem 7.1 (Existence and uniqueness).** For every n≥1, the roots of
$$f_n(x)=x^{q^n}-x$$
in the chosen closure form a field with exactly q^n elements containing F_q. Denote it by F_(q^n). Every extension of F_q with q^n elements is F_q-isomorphic to it.

*Proof.* In characteristic p, the p-power map respects sums and products by the binomial theorem, since the intermediate binomial coefficients vanish. Iterating proves the same for the q^n-power map. Thus if a and b are roots, so are a−b and ab. Zero and one are roots. If a≠0 is a root, then a^(q^n−1)=1, and its inverse also satisfies the defining equation. Therefore the root set is a field.

The derivative of f_n is −1, so the polynomial has no repeated roots. In an algebraic closure it splits into exactly q^n distinct linear factors, proving the root set has that many elements. Every a∈F_q satisfies a^q=a: for nonzero a this follows from Lagrange in its multiplicative group of order q−1, and zero is immediate. Iteration gives a^(q^n)=a, so the root field contains the specified base.

Now let L/F_q have q^n elements. Every element of L satisfies x^(q^n)=x by the same multiplicative-group argument, so L is a splitting field of f_n and is generated by its roots. Splitting-field uniqueness supplies an F_q-isomorphism to the root field constructed above. ∎

The notation is an isomorphism-class statement when no common closure is fixed. Inside the fixed closure the full root set is a particular field, so later field intersections are literal intersections. Its degree over F_q is n: a finite vector space of dimension d over a q-element field has q^d elements, and q^d=q^n forces d=n.

Finite fields have prime characteristic because their prime subring is an integral domain of finite order. Their additive groups are finite-dimensional vector spaces over F_p, so their sizes are prime powers. In particular no field has six elements. A ring with six elements such as Z/6Z is not a counterexample: it has zero divisors.

## 2. Frobenius accounts for every automorphism

**Theorem 7.2.** The map
$$\phi:F_{q^n}\to F_{q^n},\qquad \phi(a)=a^q$$
is an F_q-automorphism of order exactly n. The extension F_(q^n)/F_q is Galois and
$$\operatorname{Gal}(F_{q^n}/F_q)=\langle\phi\rangle\cong C_n.$$

*Proof.* The map preserves addition and multiplication, fixes F_q and is injective because a^q=b^q implies (a−b)^q=0 and hence a=b. Finiteness makes it surjective. The defining equation of the field gives φ^n=id. If 0<d<n and φ^d=id, every q^n elements would be roots of x^(q^d)−x, a polynomial of degree q^d<q^n, impossible. Thus the order is n.

The field is the splitting field of f_n with distinct roots, so is finite normal separable. Its group has size equal to degree n. The n distinct powers of φ already supply that many automorphisms, so they exhaust the group. ∎

The base matters: on F_(p^(rn)), the p-power automorphism generates the group over F_p, while its r-th power generates the group over the fixed F_(p^r). Confusing these bases changes the group order from rn to n. Likewise, a multiplicative generator of the nonzero field elements is an element of a different group; it is not itself a field automorphism.

## 3. Subfields and divisibility

**Theorem 7.3 (Finite subfield theorem).** The intermediate fields between F_q and F_(q^n) are exactly F_(q^d) for divisors d of n, with one field for each divisor. For any positive integer k, the elements fixed by φ^k form F_(q^g), where g=gcd(n,k).

*Proof.* A cyclic group of order n has exactly one subgroup of each order dividing n. Specifically ⟨φ^k⟩ has order n/g for g=gcd(n,k). Artin’s theorem gives its fixed field degree g over F_q and therefore size q^g. The roots defining F_(q^g) lie in F_(q^n) when g divides n: iterating a^(q^g)=a exactly n/g times gives a^(q^n)=a. Its q^g elements are fixed by φ^k because g divides k, so they fill the fixed field completely.

The finite correspondence says every intermediate field is the fixed field of a subgroup. Each subgroup has the form just described, proving the full list and uniqueness. ∎

As a consequence, F_(q^a) is contained in F_(q^b) exactly when a divides b. The forward implication also follows from the tower law; the reverse follows from iterating the root equation. An integer comparison a≤b alone is insufficient: F_(q^2) is not a subfield of F_(q^3), despite its smaller size.

**Corollary 7.4.** Inside a common finite field containing both,
$$F_{q^a}\cap F_{q^b}=F_{q^{\gcd(a,b)}},\qquad
F_{q^a}F_{q^b}=F_{q^{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)}}.$$

*Proof.* A subfield of both has degree dividing a and b, and the unique field of degree gcd(a,b) is contained in both, proving the intersection statement. The compositum is an intermediate field of degree divisible by both a and b. The field of degree lcm(a,b) contains both and lies in any common F_(q^n), so the compositum's degree is exactly that least common multiple. Uniqueness identifies the field. ∎

## 4. Minimal polynomials are Frobenius orbits

**Theorem 7.5.** Let α∈F_(q^n), and let d be the least positive integer with α^(q^d)=α. Its minimal polynomial over F_q is
$$m_\alpha(x)=\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}(x-\alpha^{q^j}),$$
and its degree is d, a divisor of n.

*Proof.* The orbit length d divides the group order n by orbit–stabiliser. Frobenius cyclically permutes the d displayed factors, so fixes every coefficient of their product. Its fixed field is F_q, hence the product lies in F_q[x]. It vanishes at α, so the minimal polynomial divides it.

Conversely every orbit value is a root of the minimal polynomial: applying Frobenius preserves its coefficients and the equation m(α)=0. These d values are distinct by minimality of d. Thus the minimal polynomial has degree at least d, whereas divisibility gives degree at most d. Equality identifies the two monic polynomials. ∎

**Corollary 7.6 (Factorisation of the universal polynomial).** The polynomial x^(q^n)−x is the product, once each, of the monic irreducible polynomials over F_q whose degrees divide n.

*Proof.* If m is irreducible of degree d, its root field has q^d elements and every root is in F_(q^d). When d divides n this field is contained in F_(q^n), so m divides the displayed universal polynomial. Conversely a root α of that polynomial lies in F_(q^n), and the theorem makes its irreducible degree divide n. The universal polynomial has derivative −1, so none of its irreducible factors repeats. ∎

An irreducibility certificate can therefore combine x^(q^n)≡x modulo a candidate polynomial with gcd exclusions for smaller divisor degrees. The statement of such a certificate must include all required gcd conditions; a polynomial dividing x^(q^n)−x may be a product of several smaller irreducibles.

## 5. A finite irreducibility certificate

**Theorem 7.7 (Frobenius and gcd certificate).** Let $f\in F_q[x]$ be monic of degree $n>1$. It is irreducible if and only if both conditions hold:

1. $x^{q^n}\equiv x\pmod f$;
2. for every prime $\ell$ dividing $n$, $\gcd(f,x^{q^{n/\ell}}-x)=1$.

*Proof.* If $f$ is irreducible, its degree divides $n$, so the universal factorisation gives the congruence. It cannot divide any universal polynomial of degree index $n/\ell$, because its own degree $n$ does not divide that smaller index. Irreducibility then makes each gcd one.

Conversely, the congruence says $f$ divides $x^{q^n}-x$, so $f$ is squarefree and each irreducible factor has degree dividing $n$. Suppose a factor has degree $d<n$. Choose a prime $\ell$ dividing the integer $n/d>1$. Then $d$ divides $n/\ell$, so that factor also divides $x^{q^{n/\ell}}-x$, contradicting the required gcd. Therefore every irreducible factor has degree $n$. Since $f$ itself has degree $n$, it has just one such factor and is irreducible. ∎

This proof explains the complete list of certificate conditions. Checking only one arbitrary smaller exponent does not exclude every proper divisor degree. Checking every prime divisor of the degree is sufficient because every proper divisor fits beneath one of the resulting maximal proper divisor indices.

For $f=x^4+x+1$ over $F_2$, the only prime divisor of the degree is two. In the quotient ring, the relation $x^4=x+1$ gives $x^{16}=(x+1)^4=x^4+1=x$, so the first condition holds. Also $f-(x^4-x)=1$ in characteristic two, proving the gcd is one. This is a complete irreducibility certificate. The quotient calculation did not presuppose that the quotient was a field: polynomial reduction and Frobenius power identities work in that quotient ring before irreducibility is known.

The certificate uses finite exact operations. Modular powering can reduce after each multiplication, keeping polynomial degrees below $n$, and Euclid computes the gcds. The theorem proves what these equalities certify; a displayed calculation is separate from any claim that a particular software implementation was run.

## 6. Three explicit models

**Example 7.8 (Eight elements).** Over F₂ the polynomial x³+x+1 has no root, since its values at zero and one are both one. A reducible cubic has a linear factor, so it is irreducible. Write α for its class in E=F₂[x]/(x³+x+1). The basis is 1,α,α², giving eight elements, and α³=α+1.

Frobenius sends α to α², then to α⁴=α²+α. Squaring once more gives α⁸=α⁴+α²=α, so the orbit has length three, as the irreducible degree predicts. Its Galois group over F₂ is C₃. The only intermediate degrees are one and three, so there is no four-element subfield. The nonzero multiplicative group has order seven; since α is neither zero nor one, it has order seven. Its multiplicative powers and the powers of the Frobenius map are different lists with different lengths.

**Example 7.9 (A primitive multiplicative element in nine elements).** Over F₃ the polynomial x²+1 has no root, since the squares are zero and one. Let i²=−1 in E=F₃(i), of degree two and size nine. Frobenius is cubing and sends i to i³=−i, so a+bi is sent to a−bi. Its fixed field is exactly F₃ by coefficient comparison.

Set g=1+i. Then g²=2i, g⁴=−1 and g⁸=1. Since g⁴≠1, its order is eight and it generates the nonzero multiplicative group. Yet the Galois group has order two and is generated by cubing, not multiplication by g. Multiplication by g does not even fix one. This calculation keeps three notions separate: a field generator, a multiplicative generator and a group generator among automorphisms.

**Example 7.10 (The unique four-element subfield of sixteen elements).** The polynomial x⁴+x+1 is irreducible over F₂: it has no root, and the only irreducible quadratic is x²+x+1, whose square is x⁴+x²+1. Thus E=F₂(α) with α⁴=α+1 has sixteen elements. Put β=α⁵=α²+α. Then β²=α⁴+α²=α²+α+1=β+1, so β²+β+1=0. It is not zero or one, because either equation would give α a polynomial relation of degree at most two, contrary to its irreducible degree four.

Therefore F₂(β) has four elements and is the unique degree-two subfield. The Frobenius square fixes β, since β⁴=β, while Frobenius itself exchanges β and β+1. The complete subfield list is F₂, F₄ and F₁₆. There is no F₈ intermediate field because three does not divide four.

Frobenius can generate a separable Galois group while failing to be diagonalisable as a linear operator over the base. In the four-element field over the binary field, choose α with α²=α+1. Relative to the basis one,α, squaring fixes one and sends α to α+1, so its matrix has rows (1,1) and (0,1). It is not identity, but its square is identity because the characteristic is two. Its only eigenvalue over any extension is one, with a one-dimensional eigenspace, so it is not diagonalisable. Separability of the field extension concerns minimal polynomials of elements. It does not assert semisimplicity of every base-linear operator supplied by its automorphism group.

## Exercises

### Exercise 7.1 — An impossible size
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-1"></a>

Why is there no field with twelve elements?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

A finite field is a vector space over its prime subfield.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

A finite field has prime characteristic p and is finite-dimensional over F_p, so its size is p^d for an integer d≥1. Twelve is not a power of one prime, since it has distinct prime divisors two and three. Hence no such field exists. Z/12Z is merely a ring and has zero divisors, for example 3·4=0 with both factors nonzero.

</details>

### Exercise 7.2 — Roots are closed under inverses
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-2"></a>

Prove directly that if a≠0 satisfies a^(q^n)=a, its inverse satisfies the same equation.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Apply inversion to the defining equality in the ambient field.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Since a is nonzero, inversion is permitted and (a⁻¹)^(q^n)=(a^(q^n))⁻¹=a⁻¹. Thus the inverse is another root. Equivalently a^(q^n−1)=1 shows that a inverse is a positive power of a. The nonzero assumption is essential because zero has no multiplicative inverse.

</details>

### Exercise 7.3 — The relative Frobenius order
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-3"></a>

Find the order of a↦a⁸ on F_(2^12), and identify its fixed field.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

It is the third power of the 2-power Frobenius.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The 2-power Frobenius has order twelve. Its third power has order 12/gcd(12,3)=4. Its fixed field has degree gcd(12,3)=3 over F₂, hence is F₈. Equivalently it is the relative Frobenius for the degree-four extension F_(2^12)/F₈. The base choice explains why the automorphism order is four rather than twelve.

</details>

### Exercise 7.4 — All intermediate fields
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-4"></a>

List the intermediate fields of F_(q^12)/F_q and their degrees.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use every positive divisor of twelve exactly once.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The divisors are 1,2,3,4,6,12. The corresponding fields are F_q, F_(q²), F_(q³), F_(q⁴), F_(q⁶) and F_(q¹²), with precisely those degrees over F_q. The subgroup correspondence for the cyclic order-twelve group proves uniqueness for each divisor and excludes any additional field. Containment follows divisibility, so F_(q³) is contained in F_(q⁶) but not F_(q⁴).

</details>

### Exercise 7.5 — Intersection and compositum
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-5"></a>

Inside F_(q^24), find the intersection and compositum of F_(q^6) and F_(q^8).

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Take the gcd and lcm of the two relative degrees.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Their intersection is F_(q^gcd(6,8))=F_(q²), and their compositum is F_(q^lcm(6,8))=F_(q²⁴). Both original fields occur in the ambient field because six and eight divide twenty-four. The common placement matters: the formulas describe actual subfields in one closure, not arbitrary copies lacking specified embeddings.

</details>

### Exercise 7.6 — A minimal polynomial from an orbit
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-6"></a>

In F₈ with α³+α+1=0, list the conjugates of α over F₂ and determine its trace as their sum.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use α, α² and α⁴, with α⁴=α²+α.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The conjugates are α, α² and α⁴=α²+α, the length-three Frobenius orbit. Their sum is α+α²+(α²+α)=0 in characteristic two. This agrees with the negative quadratic coefficient of the monic minimal polynomial x³+x+1. The orbit length proves all three values are distinct, so none is being counted twice as a separate embedding.

</details>

### Exercise 7.7 — A reducible universal divisor
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-7"></a>

Give a reducible polynomial dividing x^(2^6)−x, showing why divisibility alone is not an irreducibility certificate of degree six.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use one irreducible quadratic and one irreducible cubic, or include linear factors as well.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The product (x²+x+1)(x³+x+1) divides x⁶⁴−x because its two irreducible degrees, two and three, divide six. It is visibly reducible, despite every root lying in F₆₄. If a degree-six example is desired, multiply also by x; the factors remain distinct and all their degrees divide six, so this reducible degree-six polynomial still divides the same universal polynomial. Gcd exclusions for smaller divisor degrees are needed to rule out such products.

</details>

### Exercise 7.8 — Factor x⁸−x over F₂
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-8"></a>

Use the universal factorisation theorem to factor x⁸−x completely into irreducibles over F₂.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Its irreducible degrees divide three; the irreducible cubics have no roots in F₂.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The linear factors are x and x+1. A monic cubic without zero as root has constant coefficient one. Testing one leaves exactly x³+x+1 and x³+x²+1 as the monic irreducible cubics. Therefore x⁸−x=x(x+1)(x³+x+1)(x³+x²+1). Their degrees sum to eight and every factor occurs once, as guaranteed by derivative −1. This also gives a direct multiplication identity over F₂.

</details>

### Exercise 7.9 — The nine-element fixed field
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-9"></a>

In F₉=F₃(i), prove a+bi is fixed by cubing exactly when b=0.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the characteristic-three binomial identity and i³=−i.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

For a,b∈F₃, (a+bi)³=a³+b³i³=a−bi. Equality with a+bi gives 2bi=0. Since 2 and i are nonzero in the field, b=0. Conversely any a∈F₃ is fixed by cubing. Thus the fixed field is precisely F₃, agreeing with the Frobenius theorem and the degree-two correspondence.

</details>

### Exercise 7.10 — Multiplication is not Frobenius
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-10"></a>

Why does multiplication by a primitive nonzero field element usually fail to be a field automorphism, even though it permutes the nonzero elements?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

A field automorphism must preserve one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The map a↦ga sends one to g. Unless g=1, it fails to preserve the identity and therefore is not a field homomorphism. It also sends a product to gab, whereas the product of the images is g²ab. Multiplication by g is a useful additive linear map and a permutation of the nonzero set, but those facts do not make it a field automorphism. Frobenius instead preserves both field operations and one.

</details>

### Exercise 7.11 — A four-element subfield
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-11"></a>

In the sixteen-element example, verify explicitly that β=α²+α satisfies β²+β+1=0, and list its generated subfield.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use α⁴=α+1 and characteristic two.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

β²=α⁴+α²=(α+1)+α²=β+1, so β²+β+1=0. The element β is not in F₂, since β=0 or β=1 would give α a relation of degree two. Its minimal polynomial is therefore x²+x+1 and the generated field is {0,1,β,β+1}. It has four elements and, by uniqueness of the degree-two intermediate field, is the only such subfield of F₁₆.

</details>

### Exercise 7.12 — Frobenius orbit length and subfields
<a id="galois-finite-fields-ex-12"></a>

If α∈F_(q^15) has minimal polynomial degree five over F_q, which is the smallest subfield containing it? Can α belong to F_(q³)?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The degree of F_q(α) equals its Frobenius orbit length.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Its generated field has q⁵ elements and is the unique F_(q⁵) subfield of F_(q¹⁵). Membership in F_(q³) would make [F_q(α):F_q]=5 divide three by the tower law, impossible. Equivalently Frobenius cubed cannot fix an element of orbit length five. The smallest subfield is determined by the generated degree, not merely by the size of the ambient field.

</details>

## Summary

The roots of x^(q^n)−x construct the unique finite extension of F_q with q^n elements. Its Galois group is cyclic, generated by q-power Frobenius; intermediate fields correspond to divisors of n. Frobenius orbits produce minimal polynomials and determine the universal irreducible factorisation. The next commissioned chapter turns from finite-field powers to roots of unity and cyclotomic extensions in characteristic zero.
