# Calculus: a course in change, accumulation, and geometry

This is an original written route through Calculus I, II, and III. The text develops definitions, calculations, theorem hypotheses, modeling choices, and proofs where they explain a method. Editable investigations make a mathematical relationship visible and changeable; written arguments and independent problems do the work that a picture cannot. The reader presents the three phases in prerequisite order. Within each phase, lessons lead to recitations, problem sets, and cumulative papers.

## Primary text and level

This is an alternative teaching route alongside Euler’s primary [Single-variable Calculus book](../../apps/calculus/reading/book-a.html) at **L2**. Its Calculus III continuation follows the primary [Multivariable and Vector Calculus book](#/books/calculus-multivariable) at **L2–L3**. Read the primary for a continuous chapter sequence; use Lucent’s investigations, recitations and independent papers for a second explanation and changed problems. Existing lesson identities and your notes stay attached to the same lessons.

The main single-variable sequence closes with its Calculus II papers. Complex exponentials follows Taylor series before those papers. The additional assessment studio remains a cumulative reference after the single-variable route, rather than an introductory lesson determined by its filename.

## Entering the course

Use the [readiness diagnostic](../lucent-refresher/diagnostic.md). You need algebraic manipulation without losing domains, functions and inverse functions, radians and elementary trigonometry, exponentials and logarithms, and basic graph reading. The [algebra](../lucent-refresher/algebra-functions.md) and [elementary functions](../lucent-refresher/elementary-functions.md) bridges are available before Calculus I. The [logic and proof bridge](../lucent-refresher/logic-proof.md) is useful when an argument moves from a plotted example to a statement about every input.

Calculus II assumes the Calculus I ideas of derivative, mean value, Riemann sum, and the Fundamental Theorem. Calculus III assumes single-variable differentiation and integration, parametric and polar coordinates, and familiarity with a two-dimensional vector's length and dot product. The necessary vector, cross-product, and plane geometry is taught inside [the first Calculus III lesson](multivariable-differentiation.md). There is no separate linear algebra course in this route.

A useful planning model is one teaching term for each phase, with 12–16 weeks per term and regular independent problem sessions. That is a suggested pace, not university credit or an estimate validated on learners. A reader returning to calculus may move faster; a first encounter with proofs, series, or surfaces may need longer. Do not advance because a chapter was opened or a plot looked familiar. Advance when you can explain a method's assumptions and solve a changed problem without the model answer.

The [150-hour single-variable route](single-variable-route.md) turns Calculus
I and II into a session-level schedule. Every block separates preparation,
developed study, independent problems, and delayed retrieval or assessment. It
also publishes the grading and reference policy so the workload claim can be
reviewed and, later, calibrated with actual learners.

## A measured first unit: limits through the FTC

The first sustained unit is designed for **33.5 hours**. The times describe
active work by a prepared learner, not video duration or time with a page open.
Record actual time and error categories; if a task takes much longer, use the
result to adjust the next week rather than skipping its independent work.

| Work | Hours | Evidence produced |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Readiness diagnostic and targeted refresher | 1.0 | Corrected algebra, function, and notation errors |
| Limits and continuity, including the five-stage epsilon–delta argument | 4.5 | Written tolerance proof and sequential/continuity distinctions |
| Derivative as local rate, including the retained secant-to-tangent argument | 3.5 | Difference-quotient derivation and controlled local error |
| Rules, implicit functions, and inverses | 4.0 | Derived rules with domain and inverse hypotheses |
| Riemann accumulation | 3.5 | Exact finite sum, signed model, and error comparison |
| Fundamental Theorem and moving accumulation | 4.0 | Proof mechanism, linked visual prediction, and endpoint calculations |
| Recitation A with a partner or written self-explanation | 2.5 | Rate/displacement/distance argument and corrected misconceptions |
| Problem Set A, independent first attempt | 3.0 | Six marked limit and differentiation solutions |
| Problem Set B, independent first attempt | 3.0 | Marked accumulation, FTC, and modeling solutions |
| Delayed retrieval from both problem sets | 1.0 | New-centre and changed-model solutions without method labels |
| [Limits-to-FTC unit examination](limits-ftc-unit-assessment.md) | 2.0 | Timed 60-mark paper |
| Marking conference, corrections, and error log | 1.5 | Rubric totals, repaired arguments, and a scheduled delayed paper |
| **Total** | **33.5** | A reviewable unit portfolio |

The examination covers only this unit and permits one handwritten A4 theorem
sheet. Its five solutions are complete enough for self-marking, but a proof
score becomes credible only when an instructor or capable peer checks the
argument. Three to seven days later, the 60-minute changed-case paper tests
whether the method can be reconstructed after the solution context has faded.

## How to study one section

The Calculus III optimization continuation, [global minima on a noncompact
set](noncompact-optimization.md), follows the boundary and constraint
lesson. It develops a separate existence question: how can a continuous
objective attain its minimum on an unbounded domain? Its two planned study
sessions and later retrieval are outside the published single-variable
150-hour route and existing timed examination budgets.

Start by writing an answer to the opening question before operating a control. Read a definition and one worked argument; then close the answer and reconstruct the step that makes the method valid. For an integral, this may be the region or small contribution. For an optimization problem, it is often the feasible set and boundary. For a theorem, mark every hypothesis and where it enters the proof.

The course's linked investigations connect several representations of one object. In [the polar patch](double-integrals.md), a parameter rectangle becomes a curved physical patch while its two derivative edges produce a local area factor. In [the surface and contour inquiry](gradient-chain-levels.md), the same selected point has a height, gradient, level tangent, and local plane. In [the circulation comparison](green-circulation-holes.md), changing orientation or enclosing a missing point changes the valid conclusion. Save a prediction, change one control, and explain the result with a formula or theorem. The diagram remains beside the problem text when printed.

Attempt a written exercise before opening a hint. Hints are staged so you can ask for only the next useful step. After reading a worked answer, put it away and solve the changed transfer task; return several days later to an older problem without a method label. The reader saves your attempts and revisions locally. Numeric and choice checks report what they checked. A written proof is self-reviewed with a rubric; an instructor or peer can give feedback that software cannot.

The mathematical notebook can bind computed results, retain bounded exact rationals, show approximate arithmetic honestly, iterate recurrence terms, and rerun a program when a declared parameter changes. The scene language lets you author diagrams and simulations. A finite plot, midpoint sum, or simulation is evidence about sampled cases. It does not prove continuity, convergence, integrability, or a numerical error bound. Use the text's exact argument for the scope of a claim.

## Assessment and standards

Work the recitations with notes, then attempt the problem sets independently. The cumulative papers deliberately mix topics so you must choose a theorem or model. Mark a solution by its setup, domain, method, justification, and interpretation, not only its final number. Keep an error log: write the incorrect claim, the assumption it missed, and a new example that exposes it. Revisit that example after a delay. An apparent 80% score is not proof of mastery if the missed item concerns a central hypothesis.
