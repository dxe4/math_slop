
# Ideal Factorisation and Splitting of Primes {#section-1-ideal-factorisation-and-splitting-of-primes}

The statement that ideals repair factorisation needs a proof and a precise
ring hypothesis. A nonmaximal order can have noninvertible ideals, so the
repair does not follow merely from adjoining an algebraic integer. This
chapter proves unique factorisation for the full ring of integers, develops
ideal norms from its local filtrations, and proves the polynomial splitting
rule with its indispensable index condition.

Read [Quadratic Integers and Ideals](quadratic-integers-ideals.md) and
[Number Fields and Rings of Integers](number-fields-norms-prime-ideals.md).
We use ordinary localization: R at a prime consists of fractions whose
denominator lies outside that prime. Ideals localize by the same rule.
The determinant trick and every valuation-ring step used for factorisation
are given below. The field K is a finite extension of the rationals,
and R is its full ring of algebraic integers, throughout.

<a id="section-16-why-the-integer-ring-has-the-right-three-properties"></a>
## Why the integer ring has the right three properties {#section-18-why-the-integer-ring-has-the-right-three-properties}

First R is a finite free abelian group of rank n, the field
degree. Recall a proof that also identifies the relevant lattice. Choose
an integral primitive element theta and the power-basis order A. The trace
pairing on K is nondegenerate because the extension is separable. Every
algebraic integer alpha has integral trace against every element of A.
Hence R lies in the trace-dual lattice of A and contains A.
The trace matrix is nonsingular with rational inverse, so that dual lattice
contains A with finite index. Every intermediate additive subgroup is therefore
free of rank n and finitely generated. An R-ideal, being an additive
subgroup of that finite-rank lattice, is finitely generated over the integers
and thus over R. This proves the Noetherian property.

Second R is integrally closed in K. If x is integral over R,
only finitely many coefficients of one monic relation are needed. Those
coefficients are integral over the integers, so adjoining them gives a finite
integer module. Adjoining x remains finite by its monic relation. Multiplication
by x on this finite module gives, by the determinant trick, a monic
integer equation for x. Thus x already belongs to R. The same
finite-module argument proves transitivity of integrality rather than assuming
it from the notation for an integer ring.

Third every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. Take a nonzero algebraic
integer a in that prime. Its monic minimal polynomial has nonzero integer
constant term, and its equation puts that constant in the principal ideal
generated by a. The prime therefore contains a nonzero rational integer
and hence a rational prime p. The quotient is a finite integral
domain, since R is a finite integer lattice. A finite domain is
a field: multiplication by any nonzero element is injective and hence
surjective, providing an inverse. This proves the claimed maximality and
the dimension-one property.

These properties survive localization at a nonzero prime. For integral closure,
clear a common denominator s from the coefficients of a monic relation
over the localization; then sx satisfies a monic relation over R.
It belongs to R, so x belongs to the localization. Noetherianity
follows by localizing a finite generating set of an ideal, and the
prime correspondence gives a unique nonzero prime in the local ring.

<a id="section-56-the-local-theorem-a-single-uniformizer"></a>
## The local theorem: a single uniformizer {#section-58-the-local-theorem-a-single-uniformizer}

**Lemma 9.1.** A Noetherian integrally closed local domain with a unique
nonzero prime ideal m is a discrete valuation ring, provided it is
not a field.

*Proof.* Choose a nonzero a in m. The radical of its ideal
is m, because every prime containing it is nonzero. A finite set
of generators of m each has a power in the ideal of a;
expanding a sufficiently large power of m shows that m to some
power is contained in that ideal. Let r be the smallest such
positive exponent. Choose b in m to r-minus-one but not in
the ideal of a. The fraction t equal to b divided by a
lies outside the ring and satisfies t m contained in the ring.

It cannot satisfy t m contained in m. If it did, write
the multiplication relations on a finite generating set of m. Taking
the determinant of the matrix tI minus their coefficient matrix gives
a monic polynomial in t annihilating every generator. Some generator is
nonzero, and the domain condition forces that polynomial to vanish. Thus
t would be integral and would belong to the ring, a contradiction.

There is consequently c in m for which tc lies outside m
and hence is a unit. For every x in m, the quotient
x divided by c equals tx divided by tc and belongs to
the ring. Therefore m is the principal ideal generated by c.
Call c a uniformizer pi. Every nonzero element is a unit times
a finite nonnegative power of pi. To check finiteness, suppose a
nonzero element were divisible by every power. The principal ideals generated
by its successive quotients by pi form an ascending chain. Noetherianity
makes two consecutive ones equal eventually, forcing pi to be a unit
after cancellation of a nonzero quotient. This contradicts pi in m.

The exponent is unique because pi is a nonunit. It adds under
multiplication, and an ideal is generated by an element of its least
occurring exponent. Every nonzero ideal is thus a power of m.
This proves the discrete valuation assertion and its ideal classification. ∎

For a nonzero prime p of R, write v-p for the exponent
in its localized ring, normalized so a uniformizer has value one.
It extends to K-star by subtracting denominator values. Products add values,
and sums have value at least the smaller input value when nonzero.
This valuation is an arithmetic order of divisibility; no completion has
yet been performed.

<a id="section-101-reassembling-local-powers-into-a-global-factorisation"></a>
## Reassembling local powers into a global factorisation {#section-103-reassembling-local-powers-into-a-global-factorisation}

We need the elementary local equality criterion. Two fractional ideals are
equal if their localizations are equal at every maximal ideal. After clearing
one common denominator, it is enough to treat integral ideals. If x
belongs to J but not I, the ideal of r with rx in
I is proper. Choose a maximal ideal containing it. In that localization,
x still cannot belong to I, since a denominator outside the maximal
ideal carrying x into I would contradict its choice. Thus local equality
would fail there. This proves the criterion without assuming a global
valuation representation in advance.

Let I be a nonzero integral ideal. Only finitely many primes contain
it: choose a nonzero a in I, and use the finite quotient
R/(a). Its finiteness follows from the nonzero determinant of multiplication
by a on the full integer lattice. At each prime p, the
local ideal is a power of the local maximal ideal; call the
nonnegative exponent e-p. It is zero away from that finite support.
The product of the global prime ideals to these exponents has the
same localization as I at every maximal ideal. A different maximal prime
becomes the unit ideal upon localization, while the selected one retains
its specified power. The equality criterion proves that this product is I.
Local uniqueness of valuation exponents proves global uniqueness.

Nonzero ideals are invertible as fractional ideals. Define I-inverse to be
the elements x of K with xI contained in R. Finite generation
of I lets this construction commute with localization: clear finitely many
denominators in the finitely many generator conditions. Locally it is the
inverse power of a uniformizer, so the product I times I-inverse
localizes to the unit ideal everywhere. The equality criterion makes that
product R globally. Fractional ideals therefore have finite factorizations with
integer exponents, including negative ones.

**Theorem 9.2 (Dedekind factorisation).** Every nonzero fractional ideal
of the ring of integers has a unique product expression in nonzero
prime ideals with integer exponents and finite support. Integral ideals are
exactly those with all exponents nonnegative. In particular cancellation of
nonzero fractional ideals is valid.

The zero ideal is excluded: it has neither an inverse nor finite
valuation exponents. The theorem is about ideals of the full integer
ring. The nonmaximal examples in the preceding primary show why its
normality hypothesis cannot be dropped and then silently restored by notation.

<a id="section-145-norms-filtrations-and-the-fundamental-equality"></a>
## Norms, filtrations and the fundamental equality {#section-147-norms-filtrations-and-the-fundamental-equality}

For a nonzero integral ideal I, define its norm to be the
finite cardinality of R/I. If p lies over the rational prime p-zero,
its residue field has p-zero to the f elements, where f is
its degree over the prime field. Each quotient of consecutive powers
p-to-j modulo p-to-j-plus-one is one-dimensional over that residue field.
Indeed it is annihilated by p, so localizing at p changes nothing:
every denominator outside p is already invertible on this vector space.
In the local valuation ring a uniformizer power is a basis of
that quotient. Thus each step of the ideal filtration multiplies index
by the same residue cardinality, proving N(p-to-e) equals N(p) to e.

Distinct prime powers are comaximal. The Chinese remainder theorem therefore
makes the quotient by their product the product of their quotients.
It follows from the unique factorisation that ideal norm is multiplicative.
For a principal ideal its norm is the absolute field norm of
the generator, by the integer-lattice determinant calculation. For a fractional
ideal, clear any nonzero integer denominator and divide its integral norm
by the corresponding integer norm. Multiplicativity proves that this is
independent of the chosen denominator and gives a positive rational value.

Apply this to the factorisation of a rational prime:

$$pR=\prod_{i=1}^g\mathfrak p_i^{e_i},\qquad
N(\mathfrak p_i)=p^{f_i}.$$

The norm of pR is p to the field degree n. Comparing
the product norms gives the fundamental equality, the sum of e-i f-i
equal to n. Split completely means n primes with both indices one;
inert means one unramified prime of residue degree n; ramified means
some exponent exceeds one. The equality does not require a Galois extension.
Only in a Galois extension may one replace the individual indices by
common values after establishing conjugacy of its prime ideals.

<a id="section-180-polynomial-factorisation-with-the-index-condition-prove"></a>
## Polynomial factorisation, with the index condition proved {#section-182-polynomial-factorisation-with-the-index-condition-prove}

Let theta be integral and primitive, with monic minimal polynomial f,
and suppose the rational prime p does not divide the finite index
of the power-basis order A in R. Reduction modulo p then gives
an isomorphism from A/pA to R/pR. One proof chooses integer bases:
the inclusion matrix has determinant prime to p and is invertible over
the prime field. Hence

$$R/pR\cong\mathbb F_p[T]/(\overline f).$$

Factor the reduced polynomial into distinct monic irreducibles g-i to
positive exponents a-i. Polynomial CRT identifies the quotient with the
product of the algebras F-p[T]/(g-i to a-i). Their maximal-ideal
kernels lift to the distinct primes generated by p and g-i(theta).
The residue field degree is the degree of g-i. The exponent
is a-i: its local quotient has the successive g-i-power filtration
of length a-i, while the discrete valuation filtration of R at
that prime has length e-i after reducing by p. These are the
same local algebra, so their lengths agree. This proves the whole
factor-to-prime rule, including multiplicities and residue degrees.

If the index is divisible by p, the inclusion matrix ceases to
be invertible modulo p and this identification need not hold. A repeated
factor in the smaller order is then not a valid ramification certificate.
It must be checked in the full integer ring or by an appropriate
local argument. Knowing that theta is integral is insufficient to remove
the index hypothesis.

The field discriminant detects exactly ramification. Its trace-pairing matrix
reduces modulo p to the trace pairing of the finite algebra R/pR.
If that algebra has a nonzero nilpotent, multiplication by its product
with any other element is nilpotent and has trace zero. The pairing
is degenerate. If it is reduced, CRT makes it a product of
finite fields; each field trace pairing is nondegenerate. To see the
latter directly, its trace polynomial is a nonzero polynomial of degree
less than the field cardinality, so some element has nonzero trace.
Pair any nonzero element with an appropriate quotient of that element
to obtain a nonzero trace pairing. The ideal-power description shows R/pR
is reduced exactly when every ramification exponent is one. Therefore p
divides the field discriminant exactly when p ramifies. An unverified order
discriminant may have extra index-square factors and does not have the
same exact interpretation.

<a id="section-224-three-worked-splitting-certificates"></a>
## Three worked splitting certificates {#section-226-three-worked-splitting-certificates}

**Worked example 1: the Gaussian prime five.** The full ring is
Z[i], with index-one power basis. Modulo five its polynomial is
(T minus two)(T plus two), with distinct linear factors. The
two prime ideals are generated by five and i minus two, and
by five and i plus two. Each has quotient the five-element field,
exponent one and norm five. Their product has norm twenty-five and
equals the ideal of five by the proved splitting rule. The sum
of the two index products is two, the field degree.

**Worked example 2: the index trap at two.** In the square-root-five
field the full basis is one and phi, where phi is one
plus square root five divided by two. The order generated by square
root five has index two. Its polynomial reduces to (T minus one)
squared modulo two, but that reduction is at an excluded index prime.
The full generator phi has polynomial T squared minus T minus one,
which has no root over the two-element field and is irreducible.
Thus two is inert, with residue degree two and exponent one.
Its ideal norm is four. The field discriminant five also confirms
that two is unramified; the smaller order discriminant twenty had introduced
the misleading extra factor of four.

**Worked example 3: ramification and index growth.** In Z[i], let q
be the ideal of one plus i. Its quotient has two elements
and its square is the ideal of two, up to the irrelevant
Gaussian unit in the generator identity. The filtration therefore gives norm
two to the k for its k-th power. At k equal to
four, the ideal is the ideal of four and its quotient has
sixteen elements. This agrees with the determinant of multiplication by
four on a rank-two lattice. The radical quotient has only two
elements, so replacing a ramified prime power by its radical would
lose the multiplicity information measured by the filtration.

<a id="section-258-exercises-with-two-hints-and-complete-solutions"></a>
## Exercises with two hints and complete solutions {#section-260-exercises-with-two-hints-and-complete-solutions}

### Exercise 9.1: finiteness of a principal quotient {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-1}

Why is R/(a) finite for every nonzero algebraic integer a?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Use multiplication by a on a full integral basis.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Its determinant is the nonzero field norm, so its image lattice has finite index.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Multiplication by a is an injective linear map on K.
In the integral basis it is an integer matrix with
nonzero determinant. The index of its image in the
integer lattice is the absolute determinant, a finite positive
integer. That image is exactly the ideal of a,
so it is the cardinality of the specified quotient.
</details>

### Exercise 9.2: the determinant trick {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-2}

In the local proof, explain why t m contained in m would force t integral.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Write t times each of finitely many generators as a ring-linear combination of the generators.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Multiply the resulting matrix equation by its adjugate.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The vector of generators is annihilated by tI minus
the coefficient matrix. The adjugate identity makes its monic
determinant annihilate every generator. Since the ideal is nonzero
in a domain, at least one generator is nonzero and
the scalar determinant must vanish. This is the required
monic relation for t. Integral closure then contradicts its
construction outside the ring.
</details>

### Exercise 9.3: a uniformizer generates m {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-3}

Suppose t m is contained in the local ring and tc is a unit for c in m. Prove m is generated by c.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
For x in m, compare x divided by c with tx divided by tc.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
The latter quotient belongs to the ring because its denominator is a unit.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Every x in m is c times the ring element
tx divided by tc, proving m contained in the ideal
of c. The reverse containment follows from c in m.
This proves equality and is the decisive step from a
fraction outside the ring to a single generator of the
maximal ideal. It does not assume that m was principal beforehand.
</details>

### Exercise 9.4: a local inequality {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-4}

If two nonzero elements have distinct finite valuations, show their sum has the smaller valuation.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Factor out the smaller uniformizer power.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
A unit plus an element of the maximal ideal is still a unit.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
After factoring out the lower power, one summand is a
unit and the other lies in the maximal ideal. Their
sum cannot lie in that ideal, since subtracting the
second term would put the unit there. It is a
unit, giving exactly the lower valuation. With equal valuations
cancellation may raise the result, so equality cannot be
asserted from the weak ultrametric inequality alone.
</details>

### Exercise 9.5: finite prime support {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-5}

Show that a nonzero integral ideal lies in only finitely many prime ideals.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Choose one nonzero element of the ideal.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Every containing prime descends to an ideal of the finite principal quotient.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Any containing prime also contains the selected nonzero principal
ideal. It therefore corresponds to a prime ideal of
that finite quotient. A finite ring has only finitely
many subsets and hence finitely many ideals. This proves
the support is finite before constructing its global factorisation.
The zero ideal would not permit this argument, since
its principal quotient is infinite.
</details>

### Exercise 9.6: a norm filtration {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-6}

If a prime ideal has residue field of cardinality nine, find the norms of its square and cube.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Each consecutive prime-power quotient is one-dimensional over that residue field.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Multiply the three successive quotient cardinalities for the cube.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The square has norm eighty-one and the cube norm seven
hundred twenty-nine. These follow from the filtration, not
from choosing an element generator globally. The prime ideal
may be nonprincipal while its local filtration has exactly
these dimensions. This is one reason ideal norms remain
effective even when ordinary element factorisation is not unique.
</details>

### Exercise 9.7: mixed cubic splitting {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-7}

A verified index-one cubic polynomial has one simple linear and one irreducible quadratic factor modulo p. Determine the indices and norms.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Both factor multiplicities are one.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Residue degrees equal the two factor degrees, which need not agree.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
There are two prime ideals, with pairs of indices
(one,one) and (one,two). Their norms are p and
p squared, and their product norm is p cubed.
The weighted degree sum is three. This demonstrates why
the general formula is a sum rather than a single
common product g e f outside hypotheses that make the
prime ideals conjugate with equal indices.
</details>

### Exercise 9.8: inert two in the five field {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-8}

Verify irreducibility of the full-generator polynomial modulo two in Worked example 2.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Test the two residues in the polynomial T squared plus T plus one.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
A quadratic over a field is reducible exactly when it has a root there.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
At both zero and one the value is one. There
is no root, so the quadratic is irreducible. Its
quotient is the field of four elements, producing one
prime above two with residue degree two. The repeated
root in the smaller order's polynomial has no authority
at its index prime and cannot override this full-ring computation.
</details>

### Exercise 9.9: norm of a fractional ideal {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-9}

Compute the fractional norm of the ideal generated by one half in a degree-three number field.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Multiplication by two clears the denominator to the unit ideal.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
The field norm of the rational integer two is two cubed.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The fractional ideal norm is one divided by eight.
It is a positive rational value, not a finite
quotient cardinality in the direction R divided by this
larger fractional ideal. The denominator-clearing definition and multiplicativity
are what extend the norm beyond integral ideals; treating
every fractional ideal as a subset of R would fail here.
</details>

### Exercise 9.10: nilpotents and traces {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-10}

Why does a nonzero nilpotent in a finite commutative algebra lie in the radical of its trace pairing?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Multiply the nilpotent by an arbitrary algebra element.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
The multiplication operator of their product is nilpotent and has trace zero.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Commutativity makes every such product nilpotent. A power of
its multiplication operator is multiplication by the corresponding
zero power, so that operator is nilpotent and its
trace vanishes. Pairing the fixed nonzero nilpotent with
every element consequently gives zero. The trace pairing
is degenerate. This connects ramified prime-power quotients with
the discriminant divisibility test.
</details>

### Exercise 9.11: cancellation's hypothesis {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-11}

Why can one cancel a nonzero ideal factor in R but not automatically in a nonmaximal order?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
The proof constructed inverses using the localized discrete valuation rings.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Integral closure was essential when the determinant trick produced an integral fraction.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
In the full ring every nonzero fractional ideal has
an inverse, so multiplying by that inverse proves cancellation.
A nonmaximal order need not be integrally closed and
its local maximal ideals need not be principal valuation
ideals. An ideal can then fail to be invertible.
Cancellation cannot be imported from the full-ring theorem
without checking those hypotheses.
</details>

### Exercise 9.12: what finite splitting does not prove {#ideal-factorisation-prime-splitting-ex-12}

Does determining the factorisation of every prime below a fixed bound prove an asymptotic density of split primes?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Separate exact arithmetic for finitely many inputs from a limiting statement.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
The factorisation theorem identifies each unexceptional reduction but does not estimate the distribution of the resulting factor types.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
It proves those finitely many splitting certificates only.
A density theorem requires control as the bound tends
to infinity. Reciprocity can turn some splitting conditions
into congruence conditions, while analytic theorems then address
their distribution; neither is supplied by a finite table.
The present chapter proves the arithmetic dictionary, not
a Chebotarev density theorem.
</details>

<a id="section-531-summary-and-next-connection"></a>
## Summary and next connection {#section-533-summary-and-next-connection}

The full integer ring is Noetherian, normal and one-dimensional. The local
determinant trick constructs a uniformizer, and local equality reassembles its
powers into unique global ideal factorisation. Filtrations prove norm multiplicativity
and the fundamental equality. Reduction at an index-good prime proves the
polynomial splitting rule; the trace pairing detects exactly ramification in
the field discriminant. Geometry of numbers next supplies the class-group
and unit finiteness that ideal factorisation alone does not imply.
