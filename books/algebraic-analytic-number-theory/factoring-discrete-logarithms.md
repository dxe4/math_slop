
# Factoring and Discrete Logarithms {#section-1-factoring-and-discrete-logarithms}

Exact arithmetic produces several kinds of evidence. A proper divisor proves
compositeness. A verified exponent proves one discrete logarithm. A complete
bounded search can prove absence of an exponent in a specified subgroup.
A heuristic running-time model proves none of those input-specific conclusions
until an actual arithmetic witness is supplied. This chapter develops algorithms
through their invariants and certificates, with explicit operation models.

Preparation is finite groups, CRT, modular arithmetic and the earlier
[prime-distribution sieve chapter](prime-distribution-sieves.md).
The [cyclotomic chapter](cyclotomic-fields.md) connects exponent orders to
splitting, but no deep analytic theorem is required for the finite algorithms
here. All numerical examples below are written mathematical derivations.
No program, benchmark or exhaustive experiment has been executed for this
manuscript. The goal is to prove what each procedure would establish under
its specified inputs, rather than infer security from arithmetic speed.

<a id="section-17-finite-inputs-and-the-cost-of-arithmetic"></a>
## Finite inputs and the cost of arithmetic {#section-19-finite-inputs-and-the-cost-of-arithmetic}

An integer N greater than one is prime if no integer from two
through its square root divides it. Indeed every composite has a factor
at most its square root, and hence a prime factor there. Trial
division is therefore a terminating deterministic factorisation algorithm:
divide by successive candidates, record each exact quotient while divisible,
and stop when the candidate square exceeds the remaining cofactor. If that
cofactor is greater than one, it is prime. The product of the
recorded prime powers and the final cofactor is the original integer,
maintained by every exact division.

This elementary proof also identifies a complexity boundary. An input with
L binary digits can have square root of order two to L-over-two.
Trial division is polynomial in the numerical size only in a loose
sense; it is exponential in the bit length. Binary modular exponentiation,
in contrast, uses a number of modular multiplications proportional to the
number of exponent bits. Repeated squaring maintains the invariant that the
accumulated result times the current base raised to the unprocessed exponent
equals the original power modulo N. Reading a low bit either contributes
that base or not; squaring the base and halving the remaining exponent
preserves the invariant. Termination gives the desired residue.

Under schoolbook integer multiplication and reduction, one modular multiplication
of L-bit residues costs a polynomial number of bit operations, with
a quadratic bound sufficient here. A b-bit exponent therefore costs at
most a constant times b modular multiplications, not a number proportional
to its numerical value. We distinguish group operations, integer bit operations
and storage throughout. A square-root group-operation bound need not be a
square-root bit-operation bound when the representation size also grows.

<a id="section-48-orders-and-certificates-before-logarithms"></a>
## Orders and certificates before logarithms {#section-50-orders-and-certificates-before-logarithms}

Suppose g is an element of a finite group and r a proposed
positive order whose complete prime factorisation is known. Its exact order
is r if and only if g-to-r is the identity and g-to-r-over-ell
is not the identity for every distinct prime ell dividing r. The
forward direction is immediate. Conversely its order divides r; if it
were a proper divisor, some prime ell would divide the quotient and
the order would divide r divided by ell, contradicting the corresponding
power certificate. This proves exactness once the factorisation of r is
itself established.

A discrete logarithm problem names g, a target y and an exponent
range modulo the exact order r. We seek the unique integer x
between zero inclusive and r exclusive with g-to-x equal to y,
or prove that no such exponent exists. The identity target has answer
zero in this convention. Target membership is not automatic in an arbitrary
ambient group. In a cyclic ambient group of known order, a subgroup
of a given divisor order is unique, so its membership can be
expressed by an appropriate power condition. Without that ambient hypothesis,
the condition y-to-r equal to the identity need not put y
in the cyclic subgroup generated by g.

Every returned logarithm has a short arithmetic certificate: check the range
and one exponentiation. Finding it may be difficult, but verifying that
particular answer is different from proving the search procedure was complete
or that the supplied group order was valid. A report should preserve
all three statements separately.

<a id="section-77-baby-step-giant-step-including-the-no-solution-conclusi"></a>
## Baby-step giant-step, including the no-solution conclusion {#section-79-baby-step-giant-step-including-the-no-solution-conclusi}

Put m equal to the least integer at least square root r.
Every exponent below r has a representation x equal to i m
plus j with both i,j between zero and m minus one.
The equation becomes y times g-to-minus-m raised to i equal
to g-to-j. Store the m baby values g-to-j and compare them
with the m successive giant values on the other side. If a
match occurs, recover x, discard it if outside the declared range,
and verify its power equality before returning it. Duplicate table entries
can be handled by retaining a matching exponent or a short list;
under the exact-order input and m at most r, the baby entries
are distinct except for the trivial single-element boundary, which is harmless.

**Theorem 16.1.** Under the exact group and order inputs, this procedure
returns the discrete logarithm when it exists. If the complete stated
table and giant range produce no valid match, the target is outside
the cyclic subgroup.

*Proof.* An existing exponent has the quotient-and-remainder representation
above and therefore yields a match at its i,j. Its reconstructed
range is valid, and the verification succeeds. Conversely every returned
candidate satisfies the equation by its explicit verification. With all
possible i,j represented, absence of a valid match rules out every
exponent in the full period. The proof also covers r equal to
one, where m is one and only the identity target matches. ∎

Baby and giant sequences each require order m group multiplications, plus
an inverse and a short exponentiation. Storage is order m group elements.
Dictionary access can make lookup efficient under an explicit hashing model;
a deterministic sorted table instead uses order m log m comparisons.
The group-operation bound is order square root r. It does not
claim a constant-cost representation-independent comparison, or that a memory
bound of that size is practical for a particular large input.

<a id="section-112-pohlig-hellman-recovers-prime-power-digits"></a>
## Pohlig–Hellman recovers prime-power digits {#section-114-pohlig-hellman-recovers-prime-power-digits}

Suppose r is the product of prime powers ell-to-e. Project the
problem to each component by replacing g,y with g and y raised
to r divided by ell-to-e. The new generator g-ell has exact
order ell-to-e, and the logarithm is the residue of x modulo
that prime power. CRT will reassemble these residues uniquely modulo r.

For one component, write x in base ell. Suppose the first j
digits have been determined and their value is x-j. Raise y-ell
times g-ell-to-minus-x-j to ell-to-e-minus-one-minus-j. Terms involving
unknown digits beyond j vanish because their exponent becomes divisible by
ell-to-e. The result is

$$\left(y_\ell g_\ell^{-x_j}\right)^{\ell^{e-1-j}}
=\left(g_\ell^{\ell^{e-1}}\right)^{d_j}.$$

The fixed base on the right has order ell, so the next digit
d-j is a small discrete logarithm. Find it by exhaustive search or
baby-step giant-step and update x-j by adding d-j times ell-to-j.
Induction determines all e digits. The invariant is that the currently
known exponent agrees with the true exponent modulo ell-to-j; the
displayed equality proves that each new digit is uniquely correct.

This is a complete algorithm and correctness proof given the exact factorisation
of r and target membership. It can also detect inconsistent projected
targets through their failed bounded logarithms. Its group work depends
mainly on the prime divisors ell, with e stages per component and
the exponentiation overhead made explicit. Smooth order can therefore make
the problem much easier than treating r as one large opaque order.
Factoring r and proving that it is the exact order remain input
obligations; the algorithm does not make those tasks disappear.

<a id="section-145-pollard-s-p-minus-one-method-and-what-smoothness-must-m"></a>
## Pollard's p-minus-one method and what smoothness must mean {#section-147-pollard-s-p-minus-one-method-and-what-smoothness-must-m}

For integer factorisation, choose a base a coprime to N and a
positive exponent M. If a prime p divides N and p minus
one divides M, Fermat's theorem makes a-to-M minus one divisible
by p. Thus the gcd with N detects at least that prime.
It is a proper factor precisely when not every prime-power component
of N divides the same difference. The possible gcd outputs are one,
a proper divisor, or all of N; only the middle case splits
the input immediately.

A standard exponent is the least common multiple of the integers through
a bound B. The precise sufficient condition is p minus one dividing
this exponent. It is guaranteed if every prime-power factor of p minus
one is at most B. Merely saying that all prime divisors are
at most B is weaker: a large power of a small prime may
still exceed the power present in that least common multiple. This
prime-power distinction belongs in the method's theorem, rather than being
hidden by an informal use of the word smooth.

Increasing the exponent can make a successful proper gcd become the entire
input, because additional components can start dividing the difference.
Changing bases or staging bounds can help an implementation, but the conditional
divisibility proof gives no universal polynomial running time for every N.
When a proper divisor d is returned, exact integer division and the
identity N equal to d times its cofactor certify that split. Further
factorisation and primality certificates are needed before calling the resulting
list a complete prime factorisation.

<a id="section-174-collision-methods-and-the-status-of-their-cost-models"></a>
## Collision methods and the status of their cost models {#section-176-collision-methods-and-the-status-of-their-cost-models}

Pollard rho factorisation iterates a chosen map modulo N and compares
two iterates through their difference gcd. If the iterates coincide modulo
one prime divisor but not the other relevant components, the gcd reveals
a proper factor. This implication is exact and follows by divisibility.
Cycle detection can compare appropriately spaced iterates with bounded storage;
any map on a finite set eventually enters a cycle. Neither observation
proves that a selected deterministic polynomial map gives a useful collision
quickly on every input.

The birthday heuristic models residues modulo an unknown prime p as independent
uniform samples. Under that model the probability of no collision after
t samples is the product of one minus j divided by p for
j below t, approximately exp(minus t squared divided by twice p)
when t is small relative to p. It suggests a square-root-p
collision scale. Actual polynomial iterates are correlated deterministic values,
so this model is a heuristic explanation unless supplemented by a suitable
proved random-map or algorithmic analysis. An observed fast factorization
would be input-specific evidence, not a proof of that model's assumptions.

The same discipline applies to discrete-log collision algorithms, which
track representations of iterates as powers of the generator and target.
A collision gives a linear congruence in the unknown exponent; solving
it may require checking a gcd and multiple candidates. A collision alone
does not always isolate one invertible coefficient. Bounded exhaustive
baby-step giant-step has a deterministic completeness proof; a randomized
walk trades its resource pattern against additional probability and stopping
questions. Those are different algorithmic contracts.

<a id="section-204-three-worked-finite-certificates"></a>
## Three worked finite certificates {#section-206-three-worked-finite-certificates}

**Worked example 1: p-minus-one finds and then loses a split.** Let
N be ninety-one, base two and M six, the least common multiple
through three. The value two-to-six minus one is sixty-three, and
its gcd with ninety-one is seven. Exact division leaves thirteen.
The order of two modulo seven divides six, while two-to-six is
minus one modulo thirteen, so the latter component has not vanished.
If M is increased to twelve, two-to-twelve minus one is four
thousand ninety-five, exactly forty-five times ninety-one. The gcd becomes
the whole input. Both are fully determined arithmetic certificates; the
second demonstrates why a larger bound need not preserve the useful split.

**Worked example 2: a complete baby-step giant-step match.** Work modulo
eleven with generator two of order ten and target seven. Choose m
four. The baby table at exponents zero through three is one,
two, four, eight. The giant multiplier is two-to-minus-four, which
is nine modulo eleven. The initial target seven is absent from the
table; the next giant value is seven times nine, equal to eight
modulo eleven. It matches baby exponent three, so x is four plus
three, namely seven. Verification gives two-to-seven equal to seven
modulo eleven. The range below ten is respected. The result is a
logarithm in the declared subgroup, not an assertion about a different base
or ambient multiplicative order.

**Worked example 3: the same logarithm through component digits.** For
order ten, project to the order-two component: two-to-five is minus
one modulo eleven, and seven-to-five is also minus one. Thus x
is one modulo two. In the order-five component, the generator is
two squared, or four, and target is seven squared, or five.
Four squared is five modulo eleven, giving x equal to two modulo
five. CRT combines these residues to seven modulo ten, agreeing with
the previous method. Agreement here is explained by two complete proofs,
not used as a substitute for either algorithm's correctness argument.

<a id="section-239-a-fourth-application-rsa-correctness-is-a-separate-stat"></a>
## A fourth application: RSA correctness is a separate statement {#section-241-a-fourth-application-rsa-correctness-is-a-separate-stat}

Let N equal p q for distinct primes. If positive e,d satisfy
e d congruent to one modulo the least common multiple of p minus
one and q minus one, then raising any residue to e and then
d returns the original residue modulo N. At each prime, a zero
residue stays zero; a nonzero residue satisfies the exponent identity by
Fermat. CRT combines the two congruences. This proof covers nonunits
too, rather than incorrectly applying Euler's theorem only to units and
then asserting the same result for every message.

For the small example N fifty-five, e three and d twenty-seven,
the product is eighty-one, one modulo forty and hence one modulo
twenty. Plaintext two encrypts to eight. For its decryption, modulo
five the power eight-to-twenty-seven is three-to-three, equal to two;
modulo eleven it is two-to-eighty-one, also equal to two by
the period ten. CRT gives the original residue two modulo fifty-five.
This proves arithmetic correctness of this illustrative map. It is not
a security claim, a recommended parameter choice or a complete encryption
protocol. Encoding, randomness and an adversarial definition would be separate
mathematical and engineering requirements.

<a id="section-261-a-report-that-exposes-exactly-what-is-known"></a>
## A report that exposes exactly what is known {#section-263-a-report-that-exposes-exactly-what-is-known}

A complete arithmetic record names the input domain, canonical representatives,
group operation, exact order and how that order was certified. It records
a returned factor or exponent with an independently checkable finite identity.
For a failed search, it specifies the full searched range and why that
range covers the desired possibilities. For a timing claim, it states
the operation model and whether comparisons, inversions, order factorisation
and memory were included. These quantities are mathematically different;
collapsing them into one speed label would hide which assertion is proved.

Likewise a claimed prime factorisation must establish that each listed factor
is prime, not only that their product is N. A list of probable
primes can be useful exploratory information, but it is a different
certificate. In a discrete-log problem a verified exponent may exist even
when the supplied order was wrong; it certifies that one equality, while
an exact-order proof is still needed for uniqueness and exhaustive failure.
The algorithms in this chapter keep these implications explicit. Deeper
index-calculus and number-field-sieve methods require additional arithmetic
and probabilistic analysis and are not assigned a running-time theorem
by resemblance to the elementary methods proved here.

## Exercises with two hints and complete solutions {#section-285-exercises-with-two-hints-and-complete-solutions}

### Exercise 16.1: a small complete factorisation {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-1}

Give a trial-division certificate for seventy-seven.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Its square root is less than nine.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Test the relevant small primes and inspect the cofactor after seven divides it.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Two, three and five do not divide
seventy-seven, while seven does with quotient
eleven. Both seven and eleven are prime,
as division by primes at most their
square roots shows. Thus seven times eleven
is the complete prime factorisation. The
product alone would certify a split, but
their primality supplies completeness.
</details>

### Exercise 16.2: a binary exponent invariant {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-2}

State an invariant proving repeated-squaring modular exponentiation.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Track the accumulated result, current base and remaining exponent together.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Their combined value should equal the original requested power after every low-bit step.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The accumulated value times current-base to
remaining-exponent stays congruent to the original
power. If the exponent is odd,
multiply the accumulator by the base;
then square the base and halve the
remaining exponent. The identity is preserved
in either parity case. At remaining exponent
zero the accumulator is the required answer.
</details>

### Exercise 16.3: smooth is not enough {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-3}

Why does two-smoothness of p minus one not guarantee that it divides the least common multiple through two?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Two-smooth means every prime divisor is two, without bounding its exponent.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Use p equal to seventeen, whose preceding integer is sixteen.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Sixteen has only prime divisor two,
but it does not divide the least
common multiple one and two, which
is two. The p-minus-one theorem
requires exact divisibility by the chosen
exponent. Bounding every prime-power factor
by the cutoff is sufficient, whereas
bounding only prime divisors is not.
</details>

### Exercise 16.4: the whole gcd {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-4}

Verify the unhelpful gcd when N is ninety-one and the base-two exponent is twelve.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Two-to-twelve minus one is four thousand ninety-five.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Divide that integer by ninety-one.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
It is exactly forty-five times ninety-one,
so the gcd is ninety-one. Both
prime components divide the difference and
no proper split is isolated. This
does not invalidate the divisibility method;
it identifies one of its legitimate
but uninformative outputs. A different exponent
or base would be another arithmetic choice.
</details>

### Exercise 16.5: exact order six {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-5}

Certify that three has order six modulo seven using the prime-factor criterion.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
The distinct prime divisors of six are two and three.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Check the sixth, third and second powers.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The sixth power is one by
Fermat. The third is twenty-seven,
or six modulo seven, and the
second is nine, or two. Neither
proper-divisor check is one, so the
order is exactly six. Checking just
the sixth power would have established
only that the order divides six.
</details>

### Exercise 16.6: the giant-step range {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-6}

Why do m giant indices suffice when m is the ceiling of square root r?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Every exponent below r is also below m squared.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Divide it by m with quotient and remainder.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Its quotient is at most m
minus one and its remainder is
below m. Thus all exponents are
covered by indices zero through m
minus one in both directions. Some
pairs may produce exponents beyond r;
range verification discards those without removing
the valid representation of any existing logarithm.
</details>

### Exercise 16.7: membership in a noncyclic ambient group {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-7}

Give two elements of an order-two-by-order-two product for which both square to the identity but one is not in the other's cyclic subgroup.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Use the two separate coordinate generators.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
The first cyclic subgroup has trivial second coordinate.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
In additive binary coordinates take g
equal to (one,zero) and y
equal to (zero,one). Both doubles
are zero, yet the subgroup generated
by g contains only (zero,zero)
and (one,zero). Thus a power
test alone is insufficient without the
cyclic-ambient uniqueness argument or another membership certificate.
</details>

### Exercise 16.8: isolating one digit {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-8}

Explain why digits beyond j disappear in the Pohlig–Hellman exponentiation.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
After subtracting the known prefix, every later term beyond digit j is divisible by ell-to-j-plus-one.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Multiplication by ell-to-e-minus-one-minus-j raises that divisibility to ell-to-e.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The later exponent terms become multiples
of the exact group order ell-to-e
and vanish. The j-th term
becomes d-j times ell-to-e-minus-one,
leaving a logarithm in a subgroup
of order ell. This is why
the algorithm determines one digit at
a time rather than guessing the whole prime-power exponent.
</details>

### Exercise 16.9: CRT reconstruction {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-9}

Reconstruct x modulo twenty from x congruent to three modulo four and two modulo five.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Write x as three plus four t.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
The second congruence gives four t equal to minus one modulo five.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
It gives t equal to one
modulo five, so x is seven
modulo twenty. The moduli are coprime,
which supplies existence and uniqueness through
CRT. These are precisely the properties
needed when reassembling prime-power logarithms;
inconsistent residues on noncoprime moduli would
require another compatibility condition.
</details>

### Exercise 16.10: numerical size versus input length {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-10}

Why is order square root N trial division not a polynomial bound in the binary input length?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
An L-bit integer can have magnitude near two-to-L.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
Its square root is then near two-to-L-over-two.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The candidate count grows exponentially in
L. Even if each division costs
only polynomially many bit operations,
multiplying by that exponential count does
not give polynomial bit complexity. Naming
the input encoding is therefore part
of a meaningful algorithmic cost theorem,
not a minor implementation convention.
</details>

### Exercise 16.11: the rho heuristic {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-11}

Which missing hypothesis prevents an arbitrary deterministic polynomial iteration from inheriting the independent-sample birthday calculation as a universal theorem?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Compare successive iterates with independently sampled residues.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
The iteration's outputs are related by one fixed map and may be strongly correlated.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
The birthday product assumes uniform independent
samples at each stage. A fixed
iteration provides neither assumption automatically.
It may enter an unhelpful cycle
or collide in all components together.
The gcd implication remains exact once
a suitable collision occurs, while its
speed model needs additional justified probabilistic analysis.
</details>

### Exercise 16.12: RSA on a nonunit {#factoring-discrete-logarithms-ex-12}

For N fifty-five,e three,d twenty-seven, explain why plaintext five is recovered despite not being a unit modulo N.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>
Check the exponent identity separately modulo five and eleven.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>
At five the residue is zero; at eleven use the nonzero residue and the period ten.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
Raising five to the combined exponent
eighty-one keeps it zero modulo five.
Modulo eleven, eighty-one is one
modulo ten, so Fermat gives the
original residue five. CRT returns five
modulo fifty-five. This proves correctness
for this nonunit without applying Euler's
unit-only statement to an input outside its domain.
</details>

<a id="section-573-summary"></a>
## Summary {#section-575-summary}

Exact orders and input domains make logarithm certificates meaningful.
Baby-step giant-step has a complete bounded-search proof, and Pohlig–Hellman
reduces a smooth-order problem to prime-power digits and CRT. Pollard's
p-minus-one method supplies conditional divisibility certificates, while rho
requires separating exact collision consequences from heuristic speed models.
Bit length, group operations and memory are distinct costs. The same
discipline separates RSA arithmetic correctness from protocol security and
finite computational evidence from a universal mathematical theorem.
