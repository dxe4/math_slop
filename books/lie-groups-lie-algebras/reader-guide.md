
# Reader Guide {#section-1-reader-guide}

This L4 book develops continuous symmetry through fourteen chapters, with worked examples and supported exercises. A personal reading mark is a reminder;
it is not a mathematical verdict on an exercise attempt.

The first five chapters form a matrix-first route. Begin with relative
closedness and examples, construct the manifold structure of a closed matrix
group, prove the one-parameter subgroup theorem, build brackets and integrate
homomorphisms. The fifth chapter also proves the quaternionic double cover
of SO(3). Do not skip its simply-connected hypothesis when later integrating
an abstract Lie-algebra homomorphism.

Chapters six through eight develop triangularization, Killing-form criteria
and sl(2) representations. The proofs use complex coefficients where Lie's
theorem requires them. Chapter seven's trace criterion includes its interpolation
argument, while chapter eight obtains complete reducibility through an explicit
SU(2) sphere average. This avoids depending on the later Haar chapter.

Chapters nine through twelve pass from root axioms to actual semisimple
root decomposition, classify finite crystallographic root systems and prove
the sl(3) Weyl character formula. The root-system classification is distinct
from reconstruction of Lie algebras from diagrams. The arbitrary-type Weyl
formula is stated; its general denominator theorem is not claimed as
a consequence of the sl(3) determinant proof.

The last two chapters return to compact groups and classical matrices.
Haar volume and Peter–Weyl use explicitly named integration and approximation
inputs. The capstone checks compactness, roots, covering kernels and descent
together. Its real symplectic examples show why equal dimensions and identical
complex diagrams need not identify real groups.

Preparation is the actual Representation Theory of Finite Groups book;
Linear Algebra chapters one through ten plus the linked spectral, Jordan
and trace chapters; and Smooth Manifolds through flows and Frobenius.
The later integration chapter is named when used, and Stone–Weierstrass
is additional analytic preparation for Peter–Weyl. Use the
[diagnostic](prerequisite-diagnostic.md) to locate gaps before starting.

For each theorem, record its field, finite-dimensional hypothesis and global
group assumptions. For each example, compute a tangent algebra, then ask
what topology that computation has forgotten. For each exercise, attempt
a proof before opening Hint 1, use Hint 2 to isolate the
missing mechanism, and compare the full solution only after making a
written attempt. An incorrect attempt with a precise failed step is
more useful than a copied proof with unexamined hypotheses.
