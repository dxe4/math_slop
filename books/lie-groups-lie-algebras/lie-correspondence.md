
# The Lie Correspondence {#section-1-the-lie-correspondence}

Differentiation forgets global topology. The real line and the circle have
the same one-dimensional abelian Lie algebra, while their periods differ.
An irrational winding in a torus has a perfectly good infinitesimal direction
but a nonclosed image. The Lie correspondence is therefore a collection
of precise statements with different hypotheses: connected immersed subgroups
correspond to subalgebras, homomorphisms are determined by their derivatives
on connected domains, and every Lie-algebra homomorphism integrates when the
domain group is simply connected. Removing one qualifier changes the conclusion.

Prepare with the preceding four chapters and the proved
[Frobenius theorem](#/books/smooth-manifolds/lie-bracket-frobenius).
Frobenius is an explicit geometric input: a smooth constant-rank involutive
distribution has unique maximal connected integral leaves, with their
immersed-manifold structures. The covering arguments needed below are supplied
here. Ado's theorem appears only in the final abstract-existence extension
and is clearly identified as an imported algebraic theorem.

<a id="section-18-differentiate-homomorphisms-without-losing-their-domain"></a>
## Differentiate homomorphisms without losing their domains {#section-20-differentiate-homomorphisms-without-losing-their-domain}

**Theorem 5.1 (Automatic smoothness and exponential naturality).** A
continuous homomorphism F between closed matrix groups is smooth. Its
identity derivative is a real Lie-algebra homomorphism and satisfies

$$F(\exp X)=\exp(dF_eX).$$

*Proof.* Choose a basis X-one through X-d of the domain Lie algebra.
The map sending real coordinates to the ordered product of exp(t-i X-i)
has identity derivative at zero, so the inverse-function theorem makes
it a coordinate chart near the identity. Each curve F(exp(t X-i)) is
a continuous one-parameter subgroup of the target. Chapter3 therefore
writes it as exp(tY-i) for one fixed target tangent matrix Y-i.
In the product chart, F is the ordered product of these smooth target
exponentials. It is smooth near the identity and hence everywhere by
left translation and the homomorphism law. Its derivative sends X-i to
Y-i and is linear.

For arbitrary X, the image of exp(tX) is again a one-parameter subgroup.
Its derivative at zero is dF-e X, so uniqueness of generators proves
the exponential formula, also with any real time parameter. Differentiating
the homomorphism identity shows that a left-invariant field with identity
value X is F-related to the left-invariant field with value dF-e X.
Related fields have related brackets: apply their commutators to every
smooth target function and pull back along F; the two derivation terms
agree separately. Evaluate at the identity and use Chapter4's identification
to obtain dF-e [X,Y] equal to [dF-e X,dF-e Y]. ∎

This proof does not assume a continuous homomorphism is differentiable
before constructing its local formula. Nor does exponential naturality
assert that every domain element is a single exponential. The finite
product chart is enough for smoothness.

**Corollary 5.2 (Connected uniqueness).** Two smooth homomorphisms out
of a connected Lie group with the same identity derivative are equal.

*Proof.* Naturality makes them agree on an exponential identity
neighborhood. The connected-generation argument of Chapter3 expresses
every domain element as a finite product from that neighborhood, and
the homomorphism law then makes the two values agree. ∎

For a disconnected domain, the derivative says nothing about the images
of additional components. A homomorphism on a finite discrete group has
zero identity derivative regardless of its values. Connectedness belongs
in the uniqueness assertion, not merely in a preparatory remark.

<a id="section-65-subalgebras-integrate-to-immersed-subgroups"></a>
## Subalgebras integrate to immersed subgroups {#section-67-subalgebras-integrate-to-immersed-subgroups}

**Definition 5.3 (Immersed Lie subgroup).** This is a subgroup equipped
with a smooth manifold and Lie-group structure whose inclusion in the
ambient Lie group is an injective immersion. Its manifold topology need
not be the inherited subspace topology. An embedded subgroup has that
additional local subspace compatibility.

**Theorem 5.4 (Connected subgroup correspondence).** Every Lie subalgebra
h of the Lie algebra of G is the tangent algebra of a unique connected
immersed Lie subgroup H. As a set, H is generated by the exponentials
of h. Closedness of H is not asserted.

*Proof.* Left-translate h to a distribution D on G, with value A h
at a matrix A. A basis of h gives a smooth frame of constant rank.
Brackets of the frame fields stay in the distribution by Chapter4 and
the subalgebra condition. Brackets of arbitrary local combinations do too,
by the product rule for vector fields. Frobenius therefore gives a maximal
connected integral leaf through I.

Every exponential curve of a vector in h is tangent to D, so it stays
in that leaf. A product of sufficiently small such exponential motions
gives a local chart on the leaf: its derivative in a basis is an
isomorphism onto h, and the inverse-function theorem on the leaf applies.
Left translation preserves D and sends leaves to leaves. Thus multiplying
any point of the identity leaf by an element obtained from these motions
stays on the identity leaf. Conversely, any path in the leaf can be
covered by finitely many such translated leaf charts, so its endpoint
is a finite product of the exponential motions. The leaf is connected
and locally path connected, hence path connected, and this describes
every point. The leaf as a set is exactly the subgroup generated by
exp h; inverses are included by changing signs of the generators.

Its multiplication and inversion are smooth for the leaf structure.
To justify this rather than relying on the subspace topology, observe
first that every element of the generated subgroup preserves h under
adjoint action: each exponential does by exp(ad X), and finite products
do by composition. Derivatives of multiplication and inversion therefore
carry leaf tangents into D. In a Frobenius coordinate neighborhood,
a smooth map with those tangent directions has constant transverse
coordinates on a connected small domain. Its image consequently lies
in one plaque and its leaf coordinates are smooth. Apply this locally
to multiplication and inversion. The inclusion is an injective immersion
because it is an integral leaf.

Finally a connected immersed subgroup with algebra h is generated by
its own small exponential neighborhood, hence has the same underlying
subgroup. Its local exponential charts are the same leaf charts, so its
smooth structure agrees. This proves uniqueness in the immersed sense. ∎

The usual manifold conventions include Hausdorffness and second countability.
They hold for this leaf structure: the ambient Hausdorff topology separates
distinct leaf points, and a small exponential coordinate neighborhood generates
the connected subgroup by its countably many finite products. These products
are covered by countable chart families, giving a countable base.

<a id="section-121-a-covering-lemma-with-its-global-hypothesis-exposed"></a>
## A covering lemma with its global hypothesis exposed {#section-123-a-covering-lemma-with-its-global-hypothesis-exposed}

**Lemma 5.5 (A surjective local-isomorphism homomorphism is a covering).**
If a homomorphism p from L onto G has an isomorphism as identity
differential, then it is a covering map with discrete kernel.

*Proof.* The inverse-function theorem makes p a diffeomorphism from
some identity neighborhood U onto an identity neighborhood V. Shrink U
so that U U-inverse meets the discrete kernel only at I. Kernel
discreteness itself follows from local injectivity. Every point of
p-inverse(V) equals ku for some kernel element k and u in U: choose
u with the same projection and then take their quotient. The sets kU
are disjoint, because an intersection would produce a nonidentity kernel
element in U U-inverse. On each, p is a diffeomorphism to V.
Translation gives the same evenly covered description at every point
of G. This is exactly a covering map. ∎

For clarity, an evenly covered neighborhood has full preimage a disjoint
union of open sets, each mapped homeomorphically onto it. Such neighborhoods
give unique path lifting after the initial lift point is chosen: subdivide
a path's compact parameter interval into pieces lying in these neighborhoods
and choose the uniquely matching inverse branch successively. Uniqueness
also follows locally and then on the whole interval by continuation.

Homotopies lift as well. Cover the image of a continuous homotopy square
by evenly covered neighborhoods, choose a sufficiently fine finite
subdivision of the square, and use the same local inverse branch on
each small rectangle. The branch is fixed by an already lifted corner;
uniqueness of path lifts makes the constructions agree on common edges.
This elementary grid argument is the covering homotopy property for the
compact square. When the base loop is deformed while keeping endpoints
fixed, its lifted endpoint lies in a discrete fiber and varies continuously,
so remains constant.

**Corollary 5.6.** A connected covering L of a simply connected Lie
group G is a diffeomorphism when its projection is a local diffeomorphism.

*Proof.* Every point of L can be reached by a path from a chosen identity
lift, because L is a connected manifold. If a path ends over the identity,
its projection is a loop. Simple connectedness contracts that loop with
endpoints fixed. Lift the contraction: the lifted endpoint equals that
of the constant loop, namely the identity. Thus the identity fiber has
one point. Translation gives one point in every fiber. The covering is
bijective and its smooth local inverses combine into a smooth global inverse. ∎

<a id="section-166-integration-of-a-lie-algebra-homomorphism"></a>
## Integration of a Lie-algebra homomorphism {#section-168-integration-of-a-lie-algebra-homomorphism}

**Theorem 5.7 (Simply connected integration).** Let G be connected and
simply connected, and H a Lie group. Every Lie-algebra homomorphism
phi from g to h is the derivative of a unique smooth group homomorphism
F from G to H.

*Proof.* In the product algebra g direct sum h, the graph of phi is
a Lie subalgebra: bracket preservation makes the bracket of two graph
pairs another graph pair. Integrate it by Theorem5.4 to a connected
immersed subgroup L of G times H. Projection p from L to G has
identity differential sending the graph pair (X,phi X) to X, an
isomorphism. It is a local diffeomorphism. Its image is an open subgroup
of connected G and therefore all of G.

Lemma5.5 makes p a covering, and Corollary5.6 makes it a diffeomorphism
because G is simply connected. Compose its inverse with the second
projection q to define F. These are smooth group homomorphisms, and
the identity differential of the resulting F is phi by the description
of the graph tangent space. Uniqueness is Corollary5.2. ∎

The argument applies to abstract finite-dimensional Lie groups as well
as matrix groups. To see what is required, define their Lie algebra
by left-invariant vector fields. Local integral curves of such a field
exist by the smooth ODE theorem. Left translation makes their local
time law additive and extends each curve in equal small time increments
to all real time. The resulting exponential has identity derivative I
and hence local inverse-function coordinates. These facts reproduce the
local generation, invariant distribution and graph arguments above.
No ambient matrix multiplication was essential to Frobenius or the
covering step. The smooth ODE existence and dependence theorem is the
explicit analytic input to this abstract extension.

<a id="section-199-worked-example-1-when-a-circle-map-descends"></a>
## Worked example 1: when a circle map descends {#section-201-worked-example-1-when-a-circle-map-descends}

Identify the tangent algebra of the circle with the real line by its
rotation generator. Any linear map multiplying this line by a real
number a is a Lie homomorphism, since the bracket is zero. Its unique
integration from the simply connected real line into the circle is
t to exp(iat).

It descends to a map from the circle itself exactly when adding two
pi to t does not change the image. That requires exp(two pi i a)
equal to one, or a an integer. For those integers the descended map
is z to its ath power, including negative integers by inversion and
zero by the constant identity map. The infinitesimal map with a equal
to one half therefore integrates on the universal covering line but
not on the circle domain.

The obstruction is global period compatibility. Local smoothness near
one does not repair it, and choosing an angle discontinuously cannot
define a continuous group homomorphism. This example supplies the exact
necessity of the simply connected domain hypothesis in Theorem5.7.

<a id="section-220-worked-example-2-one-line-a-nonclosed-torus-subgroup"></a>
## Worked example 2: one line, a nonclosed torus subgroup {#section-222-worked-example-2-one-line-a-nonclosed-torus-subgroup}

In the torus Lie algebra, take the real line spanned by the pair one,
alpha for irrational alpha. Its bracket is zero, so it is a subalgebra.
The corresponding immersed subgroup is parametrized by the pair of
phases t and alpha t, as in Chapter3. The map from the real line is
injective and its image is dense in the torus. It is not an embedding
onto the inherited topology, because return sequences in the torus do
not correspond to parameter sequences approaching zero.

The correspondence nevertheless works: its Lie-group structure is the
real-line structure supplied by the parameter and its identity tangent
is the specified line. Closing the image instead produces the entire
torus and its two-dimensional tangent algebra. Thus taking closure
changes the infinitesimal object; it is not a harmless completion step
inside the same subalgebra correspondence. A subalgebra need not integrate
to a closed subgroup, even inside a compact ambient group.

<a id="section-238-worked-example-3-kernels-and-infinitesimal-invisibility"></a>
## Worked example 3: kernels and infinitesimal invisibility {#section-240-worked-example-3-kernels-and-infinitesimal-invisibility}

For a smooth homomorphism F, its kernel is closed, as the inverse image
of the closed identity singleton. Its Lie algebra is exactly the kernel
of dF-e. One inclusion follows by differentiating curves lying in the
group kernel. Conversely, if dF-e X is zero, exponential naturality
makes every exp(tX) map to the identity, so X is tangent to that kernel.

Apply this to the power map z to z-to-the-m on the circle, with m
a positive integer. Its group kernel has m points, while its derivative
multiplies the tangent line by m and has zero kernel. The group kernel
is discrete and hence has zero tangent algebra, agreeing with the
general statement. Distinct positive integers produce different finite
kernels despite the same zero-dimensional infinitesimal kernel.

Rank is likewise transported by translations: differentiating F after
left translation shows its differential has the same rank everywhere.
This controls local image dimension, but does not imply that the
image is embedded or closed, as the torus example demonstrates.

<a id="section-258-worked-example-4-su-2-covers-so-3-twice"></a>
## Worked example 4: SU(2) covers SO(3) twice {#section-260-worked-example-4-su-2-covers-so-3-twice}

Realize the quaternion algebra inside complex two-by-two matrices by

$$Q(a,b)=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-\overline b&\overline a\end{pmatrix}
\quad(a,b\in\mathbb C).$$

Multiplication preserves this real four-dimensional space. Its real basis
is I together with

$$\mathbf i=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix},\quad
\mathbf j=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\mathbf k=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

Direct multiplication gives squares minus I and the oriented products
ij equal to k, jk equal to i and ki equal to j; reversing two
different imaginary basis factors negates the result. Associativity
is inherited from matrix multiplication. The determinant and squared
norm of Q(a,b) are both modulus of a squared plus modulus of b
squared, and its conjugate transpose times itself is that scalar
times I. Unit quaternions are therefore in SU(2). Conversely, for
a special unitary matrix with first row a,b, orthonormality forces
the second row to be a unit scalar multiple of minus conjugate b,
conjugate a. Determinant one forces that scalar to be one. Thus
these unit quaternions are exactly SU(2), with underlying manifold
the unit three-sphere in four real coordinates.

Identify the purely imaginary span of i,j,k with oriented Euclidean
three-space. The multiplication rules give, for imaginary vectors u,v,

$$uv=-(u\cdot v)I+u\times v.$$

For a unit quaternion q, conjugation u to quq-inverse preserves the
imaginary subspace, because it preserves the skew-Hermitian trace-zero
condition. It preserves the Euclidean norm by multiplicativity of the
quaternion norm, and is real linear. It therefore defines an orthogonal
transformation R-q. Its determinant is one: the unit three-sphere is
path connected and the determinant sign is continuous and equals one
at I. Path connectedness can be seen by writing a nonreal unit q as
cosine theta plus u sine theta for a unit imaginary u; the same formula
joins I to it, and any such u also gives a path to minus I.
Conjugation composition makes q to R-q a smooth homomorphism into SO(3).

Its kernel is exactly I and minus I. An imaginary part commuting with
every imaginary vector would have cross product zero with every vector,
so must vanish. Thus a kernel quaternion is real, and its unit norm
forces one of those two values. Both plainly act trivially.

To prove surjectivity, first take any R in SO(3). It has a real
unit eigenvector u of eigenvalue one. A real odd-dimensional orthogonal
matrix has a real eigenvalue plus or minus one; nonreal eigenvalues
occur in conjugate pairs with product one, and determinant one forces
at least one positive real eigenvalue. The perpendicular plane to u
is invariant, and the restriction has determinant one, so it is a
planar rotation through some angle theta in the orientation determined
by u. Put q equal to cosine of theta over two plus u times sine
of theta over two. It commutes with u and hence fixes that axis.
For w perpendicular to u, the identities uw equal to minus wu,
u squared equal to minus I and uwu equal to w give

$$qwq^{-1}=(\cos\theta)w+(\sin\theta)(u\times w).$$

This is exactly the specified planar rotation, proving R-q equals R.
The half-angle is forced by the conjugation calculation, not inserted
as a pictorial convention.

Differentiate q(t) equal to exp(tu) at zero. Its action on an imaginary
w has derivative uw minus wu, equal to twice u cross w. The
identity differential is therefore the linear isomorphism sending u
to twice its cross-product matrix. Lemma5.5 now makes the surjective
homomorphism a covering. Every fiber is a coset of its two-element
kernel, so the covering has exactly two sheets.

It is also universal, because the three-sphere is simply connected.
Here is a direct topological justification for that last input. A
loop can be approximated, using uniform continuity and a fine subdivision,
by finitely many short great-circle arcs with the same basepoint. The
original and polygonal loops are uniformly close and therefore homotopic
by normalizing their pointwise straight interpolation; closeness excludes
antipodal pairs, so its denominator never vanishes. The polygonal loop
misses some sphere point, since finitely many great-circle arcs lie
in finitely many proper two-dimensional linear planes and cannot cover
the three-sphere. Stereographic projection from a missing point identifies
its complement with Euclidean three-space, where the polygonal loop
contracts to its basepoint by straight interpolation. The two homotopies
combine to contract the original loop while fixing its basepoint.

This proves the familiar double cover with its algebraic kernel,
surjectivity, local differential and global topology all accounted for.
An isomorphism of the three-dimensional tangent algebras does not turn
the two groups into the same global group. Later representation theory
will distinguish which representations of SU(2) kill this central kernel
and hence descend to SO(3).

<a id="section-352-the-abstract-existence-theorem-and-its-named-algebraic-"></a>
## The abstract existence theorem and its named algebraic input {#section-354-the-abstract-existence-theorem-and-its-named-algebraic-}

**Theorem 5.8 (Ado, characteristic zero; imported).** Every finite-dimensional
real or complex Lie algebra admits a faithful finite-dimensional linear
representation. Faithful means an injective Lie homomorphism into a
matrix commutator algebra. This theorem is not proved in this book.

Given this algebraic input, the real existence part of Lie's third theorem
follows from our geometric construction. A complex algebra may first be
regarded as a real algebra here; a compatible holomorphic group structure
is an additional conclusion and is not being inferred from real Frobenius.
Represent the real algebra faithfully,
integrate its image to the connected immersed subgroup of GL, and take
the universal covering group of that connected Lie group. Its Lie
algebra is unchanged by the covering local diffeomorphism.

For completeness, the group structure on the universal cover is concrete.
Represent its elements by homotopy classes of identity-based paths, with
endpoints free to vary between classes but fixed during a representing
homotopy. Multiply two classes by pointwise multiplication of their paths;
invert by pointwise inversion. These operations respect endpoint-fixed
homotopies because group multiplication and inversion are continuous.
They have the group laws pointwise. Lift small identity charts from
the base and translate them to obtain smooth charts; the projection
is a local diffeomorphism and the lifted operations are smooth. Standard
path subdivision constructs the covering from these classes, and
endpoint-fixed contractions make it simply connected.

Uniqueness of a connected simply connected integration follows by integrating
an algebra isomorphism in both directions using Theorem5.7. Their composites
have identity derivative and hence are identity maps by connected
uniqueness. This proves geometric existence and uniqueness conditional on
the explicitly imported Ado theorem. It does not assert that the simply
connected group has a faithful closed matrix realization; a faithful
algebra representation can acquire a discrete group kernel after integration.

## Exercises with staged help {#section-390-exercises-with-staged-help}

### Exercise 5.1: derivatives determine connected maps {#lie-correspondence-ex-1}

Prove Corollary5.2 using products of exponentials. Explain the failure
for a two-element discrete domain.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

First compare the maps on exp(tX) for every tangent X.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A connected group is generated by an identity exponential neighborhood;
a discrete group's tangent algebra is zero.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Equal derivatives give equal images of each exponential by naturality.
Finite products then have equal images by the homomorphism laws, and
connected generation exhausts the domain. For the two-element group,
the constant map into the circle and the map sending its nonidentity
element to minus one are different homomorphisms. Both have the
unique zero derivative on a zero-dimensional tangent space. Thus
the connectedness hypothesis does real work: derivatives cannot
recover choices on disconnected components.

</details>

### Exercise 5.2: a graph subalgebra {#lie-correspondence-ex-2}

For a linear map phi, prove its graph is a subalgebra of the direct
sum exactly when phi preserves brackets.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Bracket the pairs (X,phi X) and (Y,phi Y) componentwise.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The first component already specifies which graph element could equal
the result.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Their bracket is the pair ([X,Y],[phi X,phi Y]). It lies on
the graph exactly when its second component equals phi([X,Y]).
Requiring this for all X,Y is the Lie-homomorphism identity.
Linearity makes the graph a vector subspace, so no additional
subalgebra requirement remains. This equivalence is the reason
integration can be reduced to an integral-leaf problem on a product
group. A merely nonlinear bracket-preserving-looking assignment
would not automatically give a linear graph distribution.

</details>

### Exercise 5.3: circle degree condition {#lie-correspondence-ex-3}

Which real tangent multipliers integrate to circle-to-circle homomorphisms?
Find the kernel for a nonzero integer multiplier.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Compare the images assigned to angles t and t plus two pi.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

For integer m, solve z-to-the-m equal to one on the unit circle.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Descent requires exp(two pi i a) equal to one, so a must be
an integer. Every integer m does work through the power map.
For nonzero m its kernel consists of the absolute value of m
equally spaced roots of unity. Negative m has the same kernel
as its positive opposite, because inversion does not change the
identity condition. The derivative has zero kernel for every
nonzero m, so the finite kernel is global information that tangent
dimension alone cannot count.

</details>

### Exercise 5.4: no nonzero circle-to-line derivative {#lie-correspondence-ex-4}

Show that a continuous homomorphism from the circle to the additive
real line is zero. Give both a compactness and a period argument.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Its image is a compact subgroup of the real line.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compose with the covering angle map and use additive one-parameter
subgroups in the target.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

A nonzero real number in a subgroup supplies all its integer
multiples, an unbounded set. A compact subgroup can therefore
contain only zero, proving the claim. Alternatively, composing
with the real angle line gives a continuous additive map t to
at. Periodicity under adding two pi forces a times two pi
equal to zero, so a is zero. Both proofs identify a global
obstruction. The nonzero linear tangent maps between the two
one-dimensional abelian algebras exist but cannot descend to
this compact domain.

</details>

### Exercise 5.5: ideal normalizer directions {#lie-correspondence-ex-5}

If a is an ideal in g, show that exp(ad X) preserves a for every
X in g. Deduce that the integrated connected subgroup of a is normal
in connected G.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Every power of ad X preserves the ideal, so its convergent exponential does.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Use conjugation of exponential generators and connected generation of G.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The ideal property makes ad X carry a into itself. Its powers
and their exponential preserve a, with inverse exp(-ad X)
giving equality. Thus Ad of exp X preserves a. Connected
generation extends this to every g in G. Conjugation by g
sends exp A for A in a to exp(Ad-g A), another generator
of the integrated subgroup. It therefore normalizes that subgroup.
This proves normality as an immersed subgroup. It does not
prove closedness; the irrational torus line is an ideal in an
abelian algebra but integrates to a nonclosed normal subgroup.

</details>

### Exercise 5.6: kernel algebra {#lie-correspondence-ex-6}

Prove that the Lie algebra of the group kernel of F is the kernel
of its identity derivative, without presuming that every kernel element
is an exponential.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Differentiate a kernel-valued curve for one inclusion.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

For the reverse inclusion, use all times of the exponential curve
generated by a derivative-kernel vector.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

A tangent vector to the closed group kernel is represented by
a curve that F sends constantly to the identity, so its image
under the derivative is zero. Conversely, if dF-e X is zero,
then F(exp(tX)) equals exp(0), the identity, for every real
t. The full exponential curve therefore lies in the group
kernel and has derivative X. This proves the reverse inclusion.
Neither direction requires all disconnected kernel elements to
be exponentials. The statement concerns the identity tangent
space, which is exactly the locally visible part.

</details>

### Exercise 5.7: covering sheets {#lie-correspondence-ex-7}

In Lemma5.5, show that two translates kU and lU cannot intersect
when k and l are distinct kernel elements and U U-inverse contains
no nonidentity kernel element.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Write one intersection point in the two ways ku and lv.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Rearrange to put a quotient of kernel elements in U U-inverse.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Equality ku equal to lv gives l-inverse k equal to v u-inverse.
The left side is in the kernel, while the right side is in
U U-inverse. The neighborhood condition forces that element
to be the identity, so k equals l. Thus the translated sheets
are disjoint. A local inverse near one point alone would not
give this full preimage description. The homomorphism structure
and discrete kernel supply the additional information needed
to conclude that the map is a covering.

</details>

### Exercise 5.8: why simple connectedness appears {#lie-correspondence-ex-8}

Explain exactly where simple connectedness is used in the graph-leaf
integration proof, and why connectedness alone is insufficient.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

The graph projection becomes a surjective local diffeomorphism before
simple connectedness is invoked.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A connected covering can still have more than one sheet over a base circle.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Connectedness of G makes the open subgroup image of the graph
projection equal to G. The local differential then makes that
projection a covering, possibly with a nontrivial discrete
kernel. Simple connectedness is used only to contract projected
loops and show every kernel endpoint is the identity. This
turns the covering into a diffeomorphism and allows a globally
single-valued inverse. A circle admits connected nontrivial
coverings, so connectedness alone cannot justify that final
inverse or the integration of an arbitrary tangent multiplier.

</details>

### Exercise 5.9: dense subgroup closure {#lie-correspondence-ex-9}

For the irrational torus line, compare its immersed dimension with
the dimension of its closure. Why does this not contradict the
subgroup correspondence?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Its parameter map is injective from one real dimension, while its
closure is the whole two-dimensional torus.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The theorem does not identify a connected immersed subgroup with its closure.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The immersed group has the real-line topology and one-dimensional
tangent algebra. Its closure is the two-dimensional torus,
whose tangent algebra contains all pairs of real directions.
The correspondence associates the original line algebra with
the immersed image, not with its closure. Taking closure
therefore changes the associated algebra and is an additional
operation outside the bijection. Compactness of the ambient
group ensures its closure is compact, but does not make the
original immersed subgroup closed or give it the inherited topology.

</details>

### Exercise 5.10: constant rank of a homomorphism {#lie-correspondence-ex-10}

Derive the constant-rank statement for a smooth homomorphism by
differentiating its compatibility with left translations.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Write F after left multiplication by g as left multiplication by F(g)
after F.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The differentials of both translations are linear isomorphisms.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The homomorphism identity is F composed with L-g equal to
L-F(g) composed with F. Differentiate at the identity. The
differential at g, composed with the invertible differential
of L-g, equals the invertible target translation differential
composed with dF-e. Their ranks are therefore equal. This
holds at every g, including every component. Constant rank
gives the same local image dimension throughout, but does
not guarantee that the global image has its subspace topology
or is closed. Those are separate global properties.

</details>

### Exercise 5.11: a covering kernel is central {#lie-correspondence-ex-11}

Let p be a covering homomorphism with connected domain and discrete
kernel. Show that its kernel is central.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Conjugating a fixed kernel element keeps it in the kernel.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

A continuous map from the connected domain into the discrete kernel
is constant.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Fix k in the kernel. The map sending g to gkg-inverse is
continuous and takes values in the kernel because applying
p gives the identity. Its image is connected, so it is a
single point in the discrete kernel. At the identity that
point is k, hence gkg-inverse equals k for every g. Thus
k commutes with the whole domain. Connectedness is essential
to this argument: a disconnected group can act nontrivially
by conjugation on a discrete normal subgroup.

</details>

### Exercise 5.12: uniqueness of simply connected integrations {#lie-correspondence-ex-12}

Given two connected simply connected Lie groups with isomorphic
Lie algebras, prove they are isomorphic as Lie groups.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Integrate the algebra isomorphism and its inverse using Theorem5.7.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The composites have identity differential, so apply connected uniqueness.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Integrate the given algebra isomorphism to a homomorphism F
from the first group to the second, using simple connectedness
of the first. Integrate its inverse to H using simple
connectedness of the second. The derivative of HF is the
identity, so connected uniqueness identifies HF with the
identity homomorphism. Likewise FH is the identity. Thus
F and H are inverse smooth maps and inverse group
homomorphisms. This is uniqueness up to isomorphism, not
a preferred equality between two differently realized groups.

</details>

## Summary and next connection {#section-783-summary-and-next-connection}

Continuous matrix homomorphisms are smooth and differentiate to bracket
maps. Connectedness supplies uniqueness from the derivative; simple
connectedness removes the period obstruction to existence. Subalgebras
integrate uniquely to connected immersed subgroups, whose images need
not be closed. Covering kernels are discrete global data invisible to
the local algebra. Abstract existence follows from the geometric
construction with Ado explicitly imported. The next chapter studies
solvable and nilpotent algebras, proving the triangularization theorems
that turn structural definitions into concrete matrix forms.
