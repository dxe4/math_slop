# Real analysis: proof, counterexample, and approximation

This course develops a substantial undergraduate analysis sequence on the real line and then continues into metric spaces, fixed points, and polynomial approximation. Its main activity is proving a statement with the right quantifiers and hypotheses. Visual experiments and the mathematical notebook help you form and test conjectures; they cannot certify a theorem about every point or every index.

The reader follows three **parts**: foundations, real functions and function sequences, and metric continuation. These are teaching parts of this course, not claims of three accredited university courses. Each substantial theme has a lesson, guided recitation or worked decision, independent problems, and later cumulative work. The order in the sidebar is the intended prerequisite order.

The [180-hour proof route](proof-course-route.md) assigns every lesson, recitation, problem set, paper, correction, oral defence, and delayed return. It separates 22.5 hours of preparation, 42 hours of developed study, 85.5 hours of independent proof work, and 30 hours of retrieval or assessment. The totals are explicit so an instructor can replace estimates with observed cohort timings.

## Primary text and level

This **L3** proof course follows Euler’s primary [Real Analysis book](#/books/proof-safe-real-analysis). Its own readings, investigations and proof papers offer an alternative teaching sequence. The primary extends the course with bounded variation, approximation, category and local multivariable analysis; those extensions are linked below rather than repeated here.

After the uniform-convergence and metric continuation lessons, use the primary chapters on [bounded variation and Riemann–Stieltjes integration](../proof-safe-real-analysis/bounded-variation-stieltjes.md), [Weierstrass and Stone–Weierstrass approximation](../proof-safe-real-analysis/stone-weierstrass-approximation.md), [Baire category](../proof-safe-real-analysis/baire-category.md), [total differentiation](../proof-safe-real-analysis/differentiation-in-r-n.md), and [inverse and implicit function theorems](../proof-safe-real-analysis/inverse-implicit-function-theorems.md). The Arzelà–Ascoli proof is in [equicontinuity](../proof-safe-real-analysis/function-spaces-and-equicontinuity.md). [Measure and Integration](#/books/measure-and-integration) is the complete sixteen-chapter L4 draft continuation. Its transferred [outer-measure](../measure-and-integration/outer-measure-and-measurability.md), [Lebesgue-integration](../measure-and-integration/lebesgue-integration.md), and [convergence-theorem](../measure-and-integration/measure-convergence-theorems.md) readings remain available with their existing identities; chapters 8–16 develop convergence modes, extension, products, densities, differentiation, representation, convolution, probability and synthesis.

## Preparation and entry check

You should already understand single-variable limits, derivatives, Riemann integrals, and infinite series at a computational level. You will need comfortable algebra with absolute values and inequalities. The [readiness proof bridge](../lucent-refresher/logic-proof.md) and [analysis proof chapter](proof-bridge.md) develop quantifiers, induction, and proof methods, but a proof course asks you to practice those methods repeatedly.

Before beginning, try these without a graph: negate “for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists \(N\) such that all \(n\ge N\) satisfy \(P(n,\varepsilon)\)”; give a bounded divergent sequence; explain why \(x^2-a^2=(x-a)(x+a)\) needs a bound on the second factor in a limit proof; and say which theorem makes a bounded monotone sequence converge. If the definitions or quantifier order are unfamiliar, start with the opening chapters and write out the missing steps.

## A repeatable proof session

Read a definition and rewrite it with quantifiers. Make a small example and a plausible false variant. Before opening a proof, identify the obstacle: Is the problem to extract a subsequence, choose one tolerance for a whole set, control a tail, or show that an expression has a limit independent of choices? Try to write the needed estimate first. Then read the developed argument and mark the exact use of each hypothesis. Close the page and reconstruct that step without looking.

For independent work, submit your own argument before taking the first hint. If a hint is needed, use one at a time. Compare your proof with the rubric and worked solution, then revise the argument itself rather than only its conclusion. A changed transfer task follows many problems. Return after several days to a problem from an earlier part; deciding *which* theorem applies is part of analysis. The reader saves attempts and proof revisions locally, but it cannot certify a written proof. Have an instructor, teaching assistant, or peer review important proofs when possible.

The linked visual investigations ask for a prediction before a control changes. In the [sequence chapter](sequences.md), actual recursive terms are separate dots and a proved error envelope is a different object; changing the rule reveals a fixed point whose orbit still oscillates. In [uniform convergence](uniform-convergence.md), a moving witness for \(x^n\), a restricted domain \([0,r]\), and a different moving-peak family make the order of quantifiers consequential. Record what the finite display shows, then prove the claim using a witness or a uniform bound. A fixed sampling grid may miss a narrow peak; the [exchange lesson](exchanging-limits.md) asks you to reason beyond it.

The notebook language supports exact bounded rational arithmetic, explicit approximate conversion, finite computations, and actual recurrence data. It is not a proof assistant. In particular, a successful midpoint calculation cannot establish an improper integral, and a formal symbolic derivative may be undefined where the mathematical derivative still exists. Use these limitations as prompts to return to definitions.

## Scope and external standards

The real-line development is informed by the theorem and assignment progression of [MIT 18.100A](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-100a-real-analysis-fall-2020/pages/calendar/) and the sustained proof treatment in [Jiří Lebl's Basic Analysis](https://www.jirka.org/ra/html/ra.html). The continuation uses topics common in a later undergraduate analysis unit. These sources set questions for comparison, not an affiliation, copied text, or evidence of equal course quality. We do not teach measure theory or Lebesgue integration here. Independent instructor review, learner studies, and assistive-technology testing remain necessary before claiming that this course meets a particular department's standard or works well for its students.
