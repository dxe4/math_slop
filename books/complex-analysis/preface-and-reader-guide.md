
# Preface and Reader Guide {#section-1-preface-and-reader-guide}

A complex derivative is one rotation-and-scale approximation valid in every direction. From that local restriction comes an unexpectedly global theory. A boundary circle determines all derivatives inside; a cluster of zeros determines a whole function; a simply connected proper domain has a disk map even when no formula is apparent.

This book is a complete eighteen-chapter course with an L3 core and an L4 continuation. Its purpose is to prove the mechanisms behind those statements and make their hypotheses usable in calculations. The advanced chapters retain full existence arguments rather than replacing them with names of theorems.

<a id="section-5-three-ways-through-the-course"></a>
## Three ways through the course {#section-7-three-ways-through-the-course}

Read Chapters 1–11 in order for the first complex-analysis course. The progression is deliberate: power series give examples before Cauchy theory, but arbitrary holomorphic functions are not declared analytic until their Taylor expansions have been proved.

For a stronger continuation, read Chapters 12–18 after that core. Argument principle and Rouché support zero counting and Hurwitz. Harmonic and conformal chapters prepare disk maps. Normal families then supply compactness for the Riemann mapping theorem. Continuation, infinite products, and gamma–zeta examples complete the route.

A reader repairing one topic can use the theorem and subject indexes, but should still follow its stated preparation links. A residue calculation may depend on an orientation theorem; a normal-family proof may depend on a compactness theorem; a power requires a chosen logarithm.

<a id="section-13-what-the-proofs-assume"></a>
## What the proofs assume {#section-15-what-the-proofs-assume}

The core preparation is the first fifteen Real Analysis chapters, linked in the diagnostic guide. We use completeness and compactness, real integration, uniform convergence, and power-series tools. Real two-dimensional first-order approximation is explained when the Cauchy–Riemann criterion is introduced, rather than assuming a later multivariable analysis chapter.

The finite-region version of the residue theorem uses [Green's theorem, Calculus Chapter 26](../../apps/calculus/index.html#chapter/26). This is explicitly additional preparation at that point. The chapter derives the complex boundary identity by applying the real theorem separately to real and imaginary parts, with its neighborhood and whole-boundary hypotheses.

For Montel, the compactness input is the proved [Arzelà–Ascoli theorem](../proof-safe-real-analysis/function-spaces-and-equicontinuity.md). Complex-valued functions are handled by successive real and imaginary subsequences. A diagonal exhaustion extends compact convergence to the whole domain.

Every theorem may use previously proved results, but an imported result is named where it enters. General Picard value distribution, arbitrary-domain Mittag-Leffler, the zeta functional equation, and the Riemann hypothesis are not claimed as conclusions of this course.

<a id="section-23-working-with-a-chapter"></a>
## Working with a chapter {#section-25-working-with-a-chapter}

Before reading a proof, state what would count as its conclusion. A primitive theorem produces a function with a derivative; an identity theorem proves equality; Rouché counts zeros with multiplicity; a Poisson theorem solves a boundary problem in a specified regularity class.

During the proof, find where each hypothesis is used. Compactness can give a common margin or a subsequence. Nonvanishing permits a reciprocal or logarithm. Connectedness propagates a local equality. A boundary condition excludes poles on the integration path. If a condition seems unused, test whether it is implicit in a later step.

For examples, do the domain audit before the algebra. For exercises, attempt the problem before opening help, and compare the complete reasoning after using the reveals. A correct final number with an invalid contour or an unstated branch is not a complete solution.

<a id="section-31-current-edition"></a>
## Current edition {#section-33-current-edition}


The current book is additive. It rewrites and expands mathematical ideas from named local seeds without replacing those resources or transferring their learner records. The references explain that lineage. The reading route and semantic exercise anchors identify this new book independently of any earlier collection order.

<a id="section-37-summary"></a>
## Summary {#section-39-summary}

The first course builds local analytic rigidity and contour methods; the continuation uses them for global existence and construction. Reading preparation and hypotheses as part of the argument is the central habit that connects the two.
