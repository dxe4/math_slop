
# Notation and Topology Conventions

Topology has several convention-sensitive words and many constructions whose notation suppresses an ambient space. This guide fixes the defaults used throughout the volume. A chapter's explicit local declaration outranks a default here.

## Sets, families, and ambient spaces

- $X,Y,Z$ denote topological spaces. A phrase such as $A \subseteq X$ supplies the ambient space for closure, interior, boundary, and relative topology.
- $\varnothing$ is the empty set; $X\setminus A$ is the complement of $A$ in $X$; $A\mathbin{\triangle}B$ is symmetric difference when used.
- $\bigcup_{i} A_{i}$ and $\bigcap_{i} A_{i}$ denote unions and intersections over the displayed index set. An arbitrary union may be infinite; “finite intersection” includes the empty intersection $X$.
- $|A|$ denotes cardinality, not diameter or absolute value. $\mathcal{P}(A)$ denotes the power set.
- $\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$ unless a local formula explicitly includes $0$; $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ have their usual meanings.
- A family $\{U_{\alpha}\}$ may contain repeated sets. A cover is a family, not merely its union, so a subcover means a subfamily retaining the relevant labels.

A set is **countable** if it is finite or countably infinite. “Uncountable” means not countable. No countability claim silently invokes the continuum hypothesis.

## Open-set operations

For $A \subseteq X$:

- $\operatorname{int}_{X}(A)$ is the largest open subset of $X$ contained in $A$;
- $\operatorname{cl}_{X}(A)$ is the smallest closed subset of $X$ containing $A$;
- $\partial_{X}(A)=\operatorname{cl}_{X}(A)\setminus\operatorname{int}_{X}(A)$ is the boundary;
- $A'$ denotes the set of accumulation points only when declared locally, because a prime may also denote a derived object.

The subscript is dropped when the ambient space is unambiguous. Relative and ambient closure are not interchangeable: for $A \subseteq Y \subseteq X$,

$\operatorname{cl}_{Y}(A)=Y \cap \operatorname{cl}_{X}(A)$.

A **neighborhood** of $x$ is a set containing an open set that contains $x$; it need not itself be open. The text says **open neighborhood** whenever openness of the neighborhood is required. A **local base at $x$** is a family of neighborhoods of $x$ refining every neighborhood of $x$.

The specialization preorder is written $x \le_{spec} y$ when $x \in \operatorname{cl}(\{y\})$, equivalently every open neighborhood of $x$ contains $y$. Some sources reverse this order; arguments here use the displayed definition.

## Maps and constructions

- A map is written with its type, $f:X\to Y$, whenever the domain or codomain matters.
- $g \circ f$ applies $f$ first. Plain juxtaposition of maps is avoided when order could be confused.
- $f|_{A}$ is restriction to $A$; $f^{-1}(B)$ is inverse image and never means an inverse function unless $f$ is declared bijective.
- $i:A\to X$ usually denotes inclusion. A map $e:A\to X$ is an embedding when it is a homeomorphism onto its image with the subspace topology.
- $\pi_{i}:\prod_{i} X_{i}\to X_{i}$ is a coordinate projection. Product-basic sets restrict only finitely many coordinates. A box-basic set may restrict every coordinate.
- $q:X\to X/{\sim}$ denotes the quotient projection. A subset $S \subseteq X$ is saturated when $S=q^{-1}(q(S))$.
- $\Delta_{X}=\{(x,x):x \in X\}$ is the diagonal. $\Gamma_f=\{(x,f(x)):x \in X\}$ is the graph of $f$.

An identification or gluing instruction is not considered complete until its equivalence relation or quotient map is specified. A formula on equivalence classes carries a well-definedness obligation. A continuous bijection is not called a homeomorphism until continuity of the inverse is proved, often by a compact-to-Hausdorff argument.

## Bases, subbases, and generated topologies

A **basis** is a family of open sets satisfying the point and intersection refinement tests. Every open set is then a union of basis elements. A **subbasis** generates a basis by finite intersections and then a topology by arbitrary unions.

“The topology generated by” means the smallest topology containing the declared family. For maps $f_{i}:X\to Y_{i}$, the initial topology on $X$ is the coarsest topology making all $f_{i}$ continuous. For maps $q_{i}:X_{i}\to Y$ that jointly cover $Y$, the final topology is the finest topology detected by their inverse images. Product topology is initial with respect to projections; quotient topology is final with respect to its quotient map.

When two topologies $\tau_1,\tau_2$ lie on the same set, $\tau_1\subseteq\tau_2$ means $\tau_2$ is finer and $\tau_1$ is coarser. Finer topologies have more open sets, make convergence harder, and make continuity into the space harder; these directional consequences should be checked rather than guessed.

## Separation conventions

The separation ladder uses these definitions:

- $T_{0}$: distinct points have distinct neighborhood systems, equivalently an open set contains one but not the other.
- $T_{1}$: every singleton is closed.
- Hausdorff or $T_{2}$: distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods.
- regular: a point and a disjoint closed set have disjoint open neighborhoods; $T_{1}$ is **not** built into the word.
- normal: disjoint closed sets have disjoint open neighborhoods; $T_{1}$ is **not** built into the word.
- $T_{3}$: regular plus $T_{1}$.
- $T_{4}$: normal plus $T_{1}$.
- completely regular: a point and a disjoint closed set are separated by a continuous $[0,1]$-valued function.
- Tychonoff: completely regular plus $T_{1}$.

This convention permits an indiscrete space to be regular or normal without being $T_{0}$. Whenever a cited source folds $T_{1}$ into regularity or normality, translate its statement before use.

## Compactness and local compactness

Compactness means the open-cover property, including for non-Hausdorff spaces. A compact subset is not assumed closed unless the ambient space is Hausdorff. The empty space and finite spaces are compact under the ordinary definitions.

The default phrase **locally compact Hausdorff** means Hausdorff and every point has a neighborhood with compact closure. When “locally compact” appears without Hausdorffness, the chapter states its convention explicitly. This caution matters because several inequivalent local compactness definitions coincide only under separation hypotheses.

$K,L$ usually denote compact subsets. A space is **sigma-compact** when it is a countable union of compact subspaces. **Countably compact** and **sequentially compact** retain their own definitions and are not treated as synonyms for compactness outside metric or otherwise stated settings.

## Countability conventions

- first-countable: each point has a countable local base;
- second-countable: the topology has a countable basis;
- separable: there is a countable dense subset;
- Lindelof: every open cover has a countable subcover;
- sigma-compact: a countable union of compact subsets.

The manuscript writes “Lindelof” in stable titles and plain source; the mathematical term is the usual Lindelöf property. Second-countability implies first-countability, separability, and Lindelofness. Converses require hypotheses or fail by counterexample. A finite family is countable, so a finite subcover also qualifies as a countable subcover.

## Convergence, nets, and filters

A sequence is indexed by $\mathbb{N}$. A net $(x_{d})_{d \in D}$ is indexed by a nonempty directed set: for any $d_{1},d_{2}$, some $d_{3}$ satisfies $d_{3}\ge d_{1},d_{2}$. “Eventually” means beyond one index; “frequently” means beyond every index there is a later occurrence.

A subnet uses the manuscript's order-preserving cofinal reindexing convention, not merely a subsequence-like injection. A filter $F$ is a nonempty family of subsets closed upward and under finite intersections and excluding $\varnothing$. An ultrafilter is maximal among proper filters. A filter converges to $x$ when it contains every neighborhood of $x$.

$x_{n}\to x$ and $x_{d}\to x$ always refer to the named topology. In a uniform or metric section, Cauchy is a uniform notion and does not by itself name a limit. Hausdorffness guarantees uniqueness of net limits; uniqueness of sequential limits alone does not characterize Hausdorffness without extra hypotheses.

## Metrics and uniform spaces

$d(x,y)$ is a metric, $B_{d}(x,\varepsilon)$ its open ball, and $\operatorname{diam}_{d}(A)$ its diameter. Metrics are **compatible** when they induce the same topology. Compatible metrics need not be uniformly equivalent and need not have the same Cauchy sequences.

For a uniform space, $U,V,W$ may denote entourages when explicitly typed as subsets of $X \times X$; otherwise these letters denote open sets. $U[x]=\{y:(x,y) \in U\}$ is the entourage ball. Completion is unique only up to the stated uniform isomorphism and density property.

Total boundedness means that every entourage admits a finite family of entourage balls covering the space; in metric language, finitely many $\varepsilon$-balls cover for every $\varepsilon>0$. It is stronger than boundedness.

## Connectedness and homotopy

A separation is a decomposition into two disjoint nonempty relatively open sets. Connected components are maximal connected subspaces; path components are equivalence classes under existence of a path. Components need not be open, and connected need not imply path connected.

For paths $\alpha,\beta:I\to X$, $\alpha*\beta$ means traverse $\alpha$ first and then $\beta$. $\overline{\alpha}(t)=\alpha(1-t)$. A path homotopy fixes both endpoints throughout. $\pi_{1}(X,x_{0})$ uses endpoint-relative homotopy classes of loops based at $x_{0}$; moving the basepoint along a path changes the comparison by conjugation.

A deformation retract fixes the retract pointwise throughout the deformation; this is sometimes called a strong deformation retract. An unqualified homotopy need not preserve basepoints or a subspace.

## Function spaces

- $C_{p}(X,Y)$ has the pointwise topology inherited from $Y^X$.
- $C_{k}(X,Y)$ has the compact-open topology generated by $[K,U]=\{f:f(K) \subseteq U\}$.
- $C_{u}(X,Y)$ has the uniform topology supplied by the declared target metric or uniformity.

These topologies are not silently identified. Joint evaluation for $C_{k}(X,Y)$ and the reverse direction of the exponential law use local compactness hypotheses on $X$. “Uniform convergence on compacta” is translated into compact-open convergence only under the hypotheses stated in the relevant theorem.

## Proof, counterexample, and choice conventions

A definition fixes language; a theorem includes its hypotheses; an example verifies a case; a counterexample refutes a universal statement. A picture motivates a construction but is not evidence that a quotient is Hausdorff, a loop is null-homotopic, or an infinite cover has a finite subcover.

Sequence arguments are not promoted to arbitrary topological statements without first-countability, metrizability, or a separate proof. Finite-product arguments are not promoted to arbitrary products. Full Tychonoff and ultrafilter-extension statements may use a choice principle; the chapter flags where choice enters rather than hiding it inside “select one point from each set.”

All visual distinctions have a text or numerical alternative. Motion is never essential to a homotopy argument: the endpoints, formula, fixed subset, and continuity or pasting check carry the proof.

## Conventions for the expanded point-set route

New chapters use their semantic filenames as chapter identities. Historical `TOP-CHnn-Ennn` and project identifiers remain unchanged even where the main route inserts new chapters. The optional compactly generated chapter retains compact Hausdorff probes. Homotopy, covering spaces, and the old capstone atlas remain retained readings.

The compactness chapter states the axiom of choice and Zorn's lemma before extending filters and selecting coordinate limits. The full Tychonoff proof does not assume Hausdorff factors; uniqueness of coordinate limits is therefore not silently assumed. Complete-metric Baire requires completeness of the stated metric. Normality in Urysohn–Tietze does not include T1, and closedness of the extension subset is always explicit. The new approximation chapter proves the real unital separating-algebra form of Stone–Weierstrass; a complex version requires additional conjugation hypotheses.
