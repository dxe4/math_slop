
# Uniform Spaces, Completion, and Total Boundedness

A topology says which points are locally near a chosen point. A uniformity says when two points are near each other by one test that works across the whole space. That extra coordination makes uniform continuity, Cauchy convergence, completion, and total boundedness possible without assigning numerical distances.

Every metric generates a uniformity, but a topology can support many compatible uniformities. Consequently completeness and total boundedness are not properties of a metrizable topology until a particular uniformity—or at least a completely metrizable structure—has been specified. This chapter develops the entourage language, translates it into nets and filters, constructs metric completion in full, states the general completion theorem, and locates compactness at the boundary “complete plus totally bounded.”

## 1. Entourages and the uniformity axioms

Let $X$ be a set and let $\Delta_{X}=\{(x,x):x \in X\}$ be its diagonal. For relations $U,V \subseteq X \times X$, define

$U^{-1}=\{(y,x):(x,y) \in U\}$

and

$U \circ V=\{(x,z):\text{there exists }y\text{ with }(x,y)\in V\text{ and }(y,z)\in U\}$.

Thus $(x,z) \in U \circ V$ means one can travel first through $V$, then through $U$. A **uniformity** on $X$ is a nonempty family $\mathcal{U}$ of subsets of $X \times X$, called **entourages**, satisfying:

1. every $U \in \mathcal{U}$ contains $\Delta_{X}$;
2. if $U \in \mathcal{U}$ and $U \subseteq W$, then $W \in \mathcal{U}$;
3. if $U,V \in \mathcal{U}$, then $U \cap V \in \mathcal{U}$;
4. if $U \in \mathcal{U}$, then $U^{-1} \in \mathcal{U}$;
5. for every $U \in \mathcal{U}$, some $V \in \mathcal{U}$ satisfies $V \circ V \subseteq U$.

When $X\ne\varnothing$, the first three conditions make the entourages a proper filter on $X \times X$, and every member must additionally contain the diagonal. For the empty space we use the unique uniformity $\{\varnothing\}$. Inversion formalizes symmetry of “uniformly near,” even though an individual entourage need not be symmetric. The composition axiom is a qualitative triangle inequality.

Given any entourage $U$, one may choose a symmetric entourage $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$: first choose $W$ with $W \circ W \subseteq U$, then use $V=W \cap W^{-1}$. Repeating the argument gives $V \circ V \circ V \subseteq U$ or any prescribed finite depth after enough shrinkings. Such controlled shrinkage replaces choosing $\varepsilon/2$ in metric proofs.

A nonempty family $\mathcal{B}$ is a **base for a uniformity** if finite intersections can be refined by members of $\mathcal{B}$, every basic set contains the diagonal, inverses can be refined, and every basic set admits a compositional square refinement. Its upward closure is then the generated uniformity. As with topological bases, checking the base conditions is usually easier than listing every entourage.

## 2. Metric and pseudometric uniformities

If $(X,d)$ is a metric space, set

$U_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y):d(x,y)<\varepsilon\}$ for $\varepsilon>0$.

The sets $U_{\varepsilon}$ form a uniformity base. The diagonal condition and inverse condition follow from $d(x,x)=0$ and symmetry. Intersections are refined by $U_{\min(\varepsilon,\delta)}$. The triangle inequality gives

$U_{\varepsilon/2} \circ U_{\varepsilon/2} \subseteq U_{\varepsilon}$.

The same construction works for a pseudometric, which may have $d(x,y)=0$ for distinct points. A family of pseudometrics $\{d_{i}\}$ generates a uniformity from finite intersections of sets $\{(x,y):d_{i}(x,y)<\varepsilon\}$. This is the uniform analogue of an initial topology generated by coordinate functions.

Two metrics can generate the same topology but different uniformities. On $\mathbb{R}$, compare

$d(x,y)=|x-y|$

and

$\rho(x,y)=|\arctan x-\arctan y|$.

Because $\arctan:\mathbb{R}\to(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ is a homeomorphism, the metrics induce the same open sets. The identity from $(\mathbb{R},d)$ to $(\mathbb{R},\rho)$ is uniformly continuous because $\arctan$ is $1$-Lipschitz. Its inverse identity is not uniformly continuous: with $x_{n}=n$ and $y_{n}=n+1$, one has $\rho(x_{n},y_{n})\to 0$ while $d(x_{n},y_{n})=1$. The sequence $(n)$ is $\rho$-Cauchy because $\arctan n$ converges, but it is not $d$-Cauchy. Thus compatible topology does not determine uniform continuity or Cauchy behavior.

## 3. The induced topology and separated uniformities

For an entourage $U$ and a point $x$, write

$U[x]=\{y:(x,y) \in U\}$.

The topology induced by $\mathcal{U}$ declares $O \subseteq X$ open when, for every $x \in O$, some entourage $U$ satisfies $U[x] \subseteq O$. The sets $U[x]$, as $U$ varies, form a neighborhood base at $x$. The composition axiom proves the neighborhood axiom: if $V \circ V \subseteq U$ and $y \in V[x]$, then, after taking $V$ symmetric, $V[y] \subseteq U[x]$.

For the metric uniformity, $U_{\varepsilon}[x]=B_{d}(x,\varepsilon)$, so the induced topology is the metric topology.

A uniformity is **separated** when

$\bigcap_{U\in \mathcal{U}} U=\Delta_{X}$.

This is equivalent to the induced topology being Hausdorff. If $x \ne y$, choose an entourage $U$ omitting $(x,y)$, then choose symmetric $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$. The neighborhoods $V[x]$ and $V[y]$ are disjoint: a common $z$ would put $(x,y)$ in $V \circ V$. Conversely, if $(x,y)$ lies in every entourage, every basic neighborhood of $x$ contains $y$, so Hausdorff separation is impossible.

Without separatedness, limits need not be unique and the canonical map into a completion may identify points. A zero pseudometric gives the indiscrete uniformity: its only basic entourage is $X \times X$, every net is Cauchy, and every net converges to every point.

A topology is **uniformizable** if some uniformity induces it. A Hausdorff topology admits a compatible separated uniformity exactly when it is completely regular. Every metrizable space is therefore uniformizable. Not every topological space is; the uniformity axioms impose global separation by continuous pseudometrics. When multiple compatible uniformities exist, the topology remembers continuous maps but forgets which of them are uniformly continuous.

## 4. Uniform continuity

Let $(X,\mathcal{U})$ and $(Y,\mathcal{V})$ be uniform spaces. A function $f:X\to Y$ is **uniformly continuous** if for every $W \in \mathcal{V}$ there exists $U \in \mathcal{U}$ such that

$(x,x') \in U \implies (f(x),f(x')) \in W$.

Equivalently, $(f \times f)(U) \subseteq W$. For metric uniformities this is exactly the epsilon–delta definition with one $\delta$ valid at every point of $X$.

Uniform continuity implies continuity. Given a neighborhood $W[f(x)]$ in $Y$, choose $U$ whose image pairs lie in $W$; then $f(U[x]) \subseteq W[f(x)]$. The converse fails: $f(x)=x^{2}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ but not uniformly continuous, since the pairs $(n,n+1/n)$ get arbitrarily close while their squared values stay more than $2$ apart.

Uniformly continuous maps preserve Cauchy nets and Cauchy filters. They also compose. A bijection whose map and inverse are uniformly continuous is a **uniform isomorphism**. Uniform isomorphism is stronger than homeomorphism: the identity between the two uniform real lines in Section 2 is a homeomorphism but not a uniform isomorphism.

Uniform continuity on compact domains has the familiar repair.

**Theorem 14.1 (Compact-domain).** Every continuous map from a compact uniform space to a uniform space is uniformly continuous.

**Proof.** Given a target entourage $W$, choose symmetric $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq W$. For each $x \in X$, continuity supplies an open neighborhood $O_{x}$ such that $f(O_{x}) \subseteq V[f(x)]$. Choose a symmetric source entourage $E_{x}$ with

$(E_{x} \circ E_{x})[x] \subseteq O_{x}$,

and choose an open neighborhood $N_x$ with $x\in N_x\subseteq E_x[x]$. The sets $N_x$ cover compact $X$; choose a finite subcover indexed by $x_{1},...,x_{n}$ and put $E=\bigcap_{i} E_{x_{i}}$. If $(z,z') \in E$, choose $i$ with $z \in N_{x_i}\subseteq E_{x_{i}}[x_{i}]$. Since $E \subseteq E_{x_{i}}$, both $z$ and $z'$ lie in $(E_{x_{i}} \circ E_{x_{i}})[x_{i}] \subseteq O_{x_{i}}$. Therefore $(f(x_{i}),f(z))$ and $(f(x_{i}),f(z'))$ lie in symmetric $V$, so $(f(z),f(z')) \in V \circ V \subseteq W$. Thus one source entourage $E$ works across all of $X$. $\square$

As a consequence, a compact Hausdorff space carries at most one compatible uniformity. If $\mathcal{U}$ and $\mathcal{U}'$ induce the same compact Hausdorff topology, the identity in each direction is continuous and hence uniformly continuous, so the entourage filters coincide. On noncompact spaces, the usual and arctangent uniformities on $\mathbb{R}$ show uniqueness fails.

## 5. Cauchy nets

A net $(x_{a})_{a \in A}$ in a uniform space is **Cauchy** if for every entourage $U$ there exists $a_{0}$ such that

$a,b\ge a_{0} \implies (x_{a},x_{b}) \in U$.

This uses pairs of late terms, not distance from a proposed limit. In a metric space it is the usual condition $d(x_{a},x_{b})<\varepsilon$ eventually.

Every convergent net is Cauchy. Suppose $x_{a}\to x$ and let $U$ be an entourage. Choose symmetric $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$. Eventually $(x,x_{a})$ and $(x,x_{b})$ lie in $V$; symmetry and composition then give $(x_{a},x_{b}) \in U$.

The converse is the definition of completeness, not a theorem. The sequence $1/(n+1)$ is Cauchy in the Euclidean uniformity on $(0,1)$ but has no limit there. A Cauchy net with a cluster point does converge: if $x$ is a cluster point, choose symmetric $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$; take a late term inside $V[x]$, and compare all later terms to it using the Cauchy condition. This forces the entire tail into $U[x]$.

In a separated uniform space a Cauchy net has at most one limit, because the induced topology is Hausdorff. In a nonseparated uniform space, completion is best understood after identifying points related by every entourage.

## 6. Cauchy filters

A filter $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$ is **Cauchy** if for every entourage $U$, some $A \in \mathcal{F}$ satisfies

$A \times A \subseteq U$.

Equivalently, the filter eventually concentrates inside a set uniformly small for each requested scale. A filter converges to $x$ when it contains every neighborhood of $x$.

Every convergent filter is Cauchy. Given $U$, choose symmetric $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$. Since $V[x]$ is a neighborhood, it belongs to the filter, and $V[x] \times V[x] \subseteq U$.

The tail filter of a net is Cauchy exactly when the net is Cauchy. Conversely, a Cauchy filter can be represented by a Cauchy net: direct pairs $(A,a)$ with $A \in \mathcal{F}$ and $a \in A$ so that later indices use smaller filter sets. Filters remove the irrelevant choice of indexing and are often cleaner in completion proofs.

An ultrafilter on a totally bounded uniform space is Cauchy. Given symmetric entourage $U$, take $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$ and a finite cover $X=V[x_{1}] \cup ... \cup V[x_{n}]$. An ultrafilter contains one member of every finite cover. If it contains $V[x_{i}]$, that set has square contained in $U$, proving the Cauchy condition.

This observation is the bridge from total boundedness to compactness.

## 7. Completeness

A uniform space is **complete** if every Cauchy filter converges. Equivalently, every Cauchy net converges; passing between tail filters and representing nets proves the equivalence. Under either formulation, separatedness is not part of the definition, though it is normally included when discussing a completion with unique limits.

Closed subspaces of complete uniform spaces are complete. A Cauchy filter on a closed subspace is also Cauchy in the ambient space, hence converges ambiently; closedness keeps its limit in the subspace. The converse boundary is useful: in a separated uniform space, every complete subspace is closed. If $x$ lies in the closure of complete $A$, the neighborhood filter at $x$, traced on $A$, is Cauchy and converges to some $a \in A$; separatedness forces $a=x$.

Completeness is a uniform property, not a topological one. The usual metric on $(0,1)$ is incomplete, while the compatible metric

$\rho(x,y)=|\log(x/(1-x))-\log(y/(1-y))|$

is complete because it transports the complete Euclidean metric from $\mathbb{R}$. The topology is unchanged, but its uniform structure is different.

Uniformly continuous maps do not always send complete spaces to complete images. A constant map does, but a general uniformly continuous map can have a nonclosed image: $\arctan:(\mathbb{R},d)\to((-\pi/2,\pi/2),|\cdot|)$ is uniformly continuous, the domain is complete, and the image is incomplete. A uniform isomorphism, however, preserves completeness because it and its inverse preserve Cauchy filters and convergence.

## 8. Metric completion by Cauchy sequences

For a metric space $(X,d)$, let $C$ be the set of all Cauchy sequences in $X$. Define

$(x_{n})\sim(y_{n}) \iff d(x_{n},y_{n})\to 0$.

This is an equivalence relation. Transitivity follows from

$d(x_{n},z_{n})\le d(x_{n},y_{n})+d(y_{n},z_{n})$.

Let $\widehat{X}=C/{\sim}$ and define

$\widehat{d}([(x_{n})],[(y_{n})])=\lim_{n\to\infty} d(x_{n},y_{n})$.

The limit exists because

$|d(x_{n},y_{n})-d(x_{m},y_{m})|\le d(x_{n},x_{m})+d(y_{n},y_{m})$,

so the real sequence of distances is Cauchy. Changing representatives does not change the limit, by another triangle-inequality estimate. The metric axioms descend from $d$; distance zero is exactly the equivalence relation.

Embed $X$ by $i(x)=[(x,x,x,...)]$. Then $\widehat{d}(i(x),i(y))=d(x,y)$, so $i$ is an isometry. Its image is dense: for a class $\xi=[(x_{n})]$, the points $i(x_{n})$ converge to $\xi$, since

$\widehat{d}(i(x_{n}),\xi)=\lim_{m\to\infty}d(x_{n},x_{m})$,

which tends to zero as $n\to \infty$ by the Cauchy property.

To prove $\widehat{X}$ complete, take a Cauchy sequence $(\xi_{k})$ in $\widehat{X}$. Choose a subsequence with $\widehat{d}(\xi_{k_{j}},\xi_{k_{j+1}})<2^{-j}$, and choose $a_{j} \in X$ with $\widehat{d}(i(a_{j}),\xi_{k_{j}})<2^{-j}$, possible by density. Triangle inequalities show $(a_{j})$ is Cauchy in $X$; let $\xi=[(a_{j})]$. The summable estimates give $\xi_{k_{j}}\to \xi$, and because the original sequence is Cauchy, convergence of this subsequence forces the whole sequence to converge to $\xi$. Thus $\widehat{X}$ is sequentially complete. In metric spaces this is equivalent to uniform completeness. Indeed, for a Cauchy filter choose $A_n$ in the filter with diameter below $2^{-n}$, put $B_n=A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_n$, and choose $x_n\in B_n$. The sequence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy. If it converges to $x$, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ some $B_n$ lies in $B(x,\varepsilon)$, so that ball belongs to the filter and the filter converges to $x$.

The pair $(\widehat{X},i)$ is a **completion** of $X$: a complete metric space containing an isometric dense copy of $X$.

## 9. General completion and its universal property

Every separated uniform space has a completion. One construction uses equivalence classes of Cauchy filters. Two Cauchy filters represent the same ideal point when their common **coarsening** $\mathcal F\cap\mathcal G$ is Cauchy. Equivalently, for every entourage $U$ there are $A\in\mathcal F$ and $B\in\mathcal G$ with $A\times B\subseteq U$. The principal ultrafilter at $x$ represents the original point $x$; its neighborhood filter is generally not principal but represents the same class. Entourages on the classes say that two filters contain sets related by a chosen original entourage, with a smaller entourage used to make the definition independent of representatives. The existence and well-definedness of this general construction are imported here; the preceding section supplies the complete metric proof.

The metric sequence construction is the countable-scale special case. In a uniformity without a countable base, Cauchy sequences may miss ideal points, so filters or nets are essential.

**Theorem 14.2 (Completion).** For every separated uniform space $X$, there are a complete separated uniform space $\widehat{X}$ and a uniform embedding $i:X\to\widehat X$ with dense image. If $(Y,j)$ is another such pair, there is a unique uniform isomorphism $T:\widehat{X}\to Y$ satisfying $T\circ i=j$.

The uniqueness proof contains the reusable idea. For $\xi \in \widehat{X}$, choose a net $(x_{a})$ in $X$ with $i(x_{a})\to \xi$. The net $j(x_{a})$ is Cauchy because the correspondence between the two dense copies is uniformly continuous, and it converges in complete $Y$; define $T(\xi)$ to be that limit. If another approximating net is chosen, interleave the two nets or use their product directed set: both approach $\xi$, hence become uniformly close, so their images have the same limit in separated $Y$. This proves well-definedness. Entourage estimates prove uniform continuity, symmetry constructs the inverse, and density proves uniqueness.

More generally, every uniformly continuous $f:X\to Y$ from a dense subspace $X$ of a uniform space $Z$ into a complete separated uniform space $Y$ extends uniquely to $\operatorname{cl}_{Z}(X)$, provided $f$ sends ambient-Cauchy nets to Cauchy nets—as it does when the subspace carries the restricted uniformity. For completions, one writes

$\widehat{f}:\widehat{X}\to Y$, with $\widehat{f} \circ i=f$.

Ordinary continuity is insufficient. The continuous map $f:(0,1)\to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x)=1/x$, has no continuous extension to the Euclidean completion $[0,1]$; it sends the Cauchy sequence $1/(n+1)$ to the unbounded, non-Cauchy sequence $n+1$.

## 10. Total boundedness

A uniform space $X$ is **totally bounded** if for every entourage $U$ there are finitely many points $x_{1},...,x_{n}$ such that

$X=U[x_{1}] \cup ... \cup U[x_{n}]$.

For a metric uniformity, this says that for every $\varepsilon>0$, finitely many $\varepsilon$-balls cover $X$. The term **precompact** is often used synonymously, especially because the completion theorem below characterizes it.

Total boundedness implies boundedness for a metric space. Choose a finite cover by unit balls and fix one center; the finitely many distances between centers give a global bound. The converse fails dramatically. In the closed unit ball of infinite-dimensional $\ell^{2}$, the standard unit vectors have pairwise distance $\sqrt{2}$. Balls of radius less than $\sqrt{2}/2$ can contain at most one of them, so no finite such cover exists. The ball is bounded but not totally bounded.

Total boundedness depends on the uniformity. Under $d(x,y)=|x-y|$, the real line is not totally bounded. Under $\rho(x,y)=|\arctan x-\arctan y|$, it is isometric to the bounded interval $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, which is totally bounded. Both metrics induce the usual topology on $\mathbb{R}$.

Subspaces and finite products of totally bounded uniform spaces are totally bounded. Uniformly continuous images are totally bounded: pull a target entourage back to a source entourage, cover the source by finitely many source balls, and use the images of their centers. The closure of a totally bounded subspace remains totally bounded; after shrinking an entourage, a finite cover of the dense subset expands to cover its closure.

## 11. Compactness as complete total boundedness

**Theorem 14.3 (Uniform compactness).** A separated uniform space is compact if and only if it is complete and totally bounded.

**Compact implies total bounded.** For any entourage $U$, choose for each $x$ an open neighborhood $N_x$ with $x\in N_x\subseteq U[x]$. The $N_x$ cover $X$, so compactness selects finitely many. The corresponding finitely many $U[x]$ still cover $X$, giving total boundedness.

**Compact implies complete.** Every Cauchy filter extends to an ultrafilter. Compactness makes that ultrafilter converge. Since the original filter is Cauchy, the existence of this cluster point forces the original filter to converge to the same point.

**Complete and totally bounded implies compact.** Every ultrafilter is Cauchy by the finite-cover argument of Section 6. Completeness makes every ultrafilter converge. The ultrafilter characterization of compactness then gives compactness.

Separatedness ensures Hausdorff topology and unique limits; versions without separatedness require passing to the separated quotient or weakening uniqueness claims.

For metric spaces this recovers the familiar theorem:

$\text{compact} \iff \text{complete and totally bounded}$.

Each adjective is necessary. The space $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$ is totally bounded but incomplete and noncompact. The infinite discrete space with the $0$–$1$ metric is complete but not totally bounded and noncompact. The closed unit ball of $\ell^{2}$ is complete and bounded but not totally bounded, hence not compact.

There is also a completion characterization:

**Theorem 14.4 (Precompactness).** A separated uniform space is totally bounded if and only if its completion is compact.

If $X$ is totally bounded, its dense image remains totally bounded after closure; its completion is complete, hence compact. Conversely, a subspace of a compact uniform space is totally bounded. Thus $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$ is precompact precisely because its completion is $[0,1]$, while usual $\mathbb{R}$ is not precompact because its completion is still noncompact $\mathbb{R}$.

## 12. Proof and counterexample audit

Before accepting a uniform-space argument, ask:

1. Is the relation an entourage, or merely a neighborhood depending on one point?
2. Was a symmetric entourage chosen before reversing an ordered pair?
3. Was compositional shrinking used where a triangle inequality would appear metrically?
4. Does the claim depend only on topology, or on the chosen compatible uniformity?
5. Is continuity being mistaken for uniform continuity on a noncompact domain?
6. Does a Cauchy object have a cluster point, or was convergence simply assumed?
7. Is the space separated when uniqueness or injectivity is claimed?
8. Does a sequence construction suffice, or might the uniformity lack a countable base?
9. Is boundedness being substituted for total boundedness?
10. In a compactness proof, were both completeness and total boundedness established?

The reliable substitutions are: $\varepsilon/2$ becomes a symmetric compositional refinement; “eventually pairwise close” becomes Cauchy; finite epsilon-nets become finite entourage-ball covers; and metric completion becomes completion by Cauchy filters.

## Worked examples: choosing the uniform structure

### Worked example 1 — A different completion of the same underlying line

Put $d(s,t)=|s-t|$ and $\rho(s,t)=|\arctan s-\arctan t|$ on $\mathbb R$. The map $\arctan$ is a homeomorphism onto $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, so these metrics give exactly the same open sets. They nevertheless give different Cauchy sequences. The sequence $n$ is not $d$-Cauchy, since successive terms remain distance one apart. It is $\rho$-Cauchy, since $\arctan n$ converges to $\pi/2$.

The completion for $\rho$ is the closed interval $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$: embed $t$ as $\arctan t$, whose image is dense there. The ambient interval is complete, and every point is a limit of the embedded image. The two added endpoints represent escape in the two directions of the real line. The Euclidean completion is simply $\mathbb R$, with no added points. Thus a completion belongs to a uniform structure, not just to an underlying topology.

The same computation distinguishes boundedness from total boundedness. The arctangent metric is totally bounded because its image lies in a bounded interval, which can be divided into finitely many intervals of any prescribed positive length. The Euclidean line is not totally bounded: finitely many balls of radius one have bounded union and cannot cover it. A homeomorphism does not have to preserve either of these uniform properties.

### Worked example 2 — A pseudometric identifies an entire direction

On $\mathbb R^2$ define $p((x,y),(u,v))=|x-u|$. The triangle inequality follows from the real triangle inequality, but distinct points with the same first coordinate have distance zero. The induced topology is therefore not Hausdorff. Every neighborhood of $(x,y)$ contains every point $(x,v)$.

To obtain the separated quotient, declare $(x,y)\sim(u,v)$ when $p((x,y),(u,v))=0$. Equivalence classes are precisely vertical lines. Their quotient distance is $|x-u|$, independent of the chosen representatives. Sending a vertical line to its first coordinate is an isometry onto the usual real line. This quotient is already complete.

A sequence $(x_n,y_n)$ is pseudometric-Cauchy exactly when $(x_n)$ is a real Cauchy sequence. No control whatever is imposed on $(y_n)$. For example $(1/n,n^2)$ converges in this pseudometric topology to every point $(0,y)$. This is why separatedness appears in uniqueness assertions about limits and extensions. Completing without first recording the zero-distance relation can hide the distinction between existence and uniqueness.

### Worked example 3 — Rational points have finite nets but missing limits

Let $A=\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]$ with its Euclidean metric. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose an integer $m$ with $1/m<\varepsilon$. The rational grid
$\{0,1/m,\ldots,m/m\}$ is a finite $\varepsilon$-net in $A$. Every point of $A$ is within $1/m$ of a grid point, so $A$ is totally bounded.

It is not complete. Choose rationals converging to the irrational number $\sqrt2/2$. They form a Cauchy sequence in $A$, but their only possible Euclidean limit is absent. Its completion is $[0,1]$, not the whole real line: every Cauchy sequence stays in this closed interval, and density supplies a rational approximating sequence for every point of the interval. Consequently the completion is compact.

Compare an infinite set with discrete metric $d(x,y)=1$ for unequal points. It is complete because a Cauchy sequence is eventually constant. It is not totally bounded, because a ball of radius $1/2$ contains only its center. These two examples separate the two ingredients in compactness: finite nets do not supply missing limits, and completeness does not supply finite nets.

## A uniform proof ledger

Uniform arguments repeatedly require a smaller entourage before they require a smaller neighborhood. If an estimate will use two successive comparisons, choose a symmetric $V$ with $V\circ V\subseteq U$ first. If it uses three, shrink once more so that $V^3\subseteq U$. This replaces the familiar metric habit of dividing an error bound into halves or thirds. It is a mathematical step, not merely notation: an arbitrary entourage need not compose with itself into a prescribed one.

For a uniformly continuous map, the controlling input entourage is chosen independently of the point. Ordinary continuity permits that choice to vary with the point. The distinction explains why a continuous map can send a Cauchy sequence to a non-Cauchy sequence: $t\mapsto 1/t$ on $(0,1)$ is the basic warning. Conversely a uniformly continuous map transports Cauchy filters because one input entourage simultaneously controls every pair of sufficiently late terms.

Completion also has a universal aspect. A uniformly continuous map from a dense subspace into a complete separated uniform space has at most one continuous extension; existence requires proving that image Cauchy data have limits and that equivalent approximations give the same limit. Separatedness supplies uniqueness, completeness supplies existence, and uniform continuity supplies independence of approximation. Leaving out any of these roles makes a completion proof difficult to audit.

For a metric completion, the equivalence class of a Cauchy sequence records approximation rather than a newly chosen name for its limit. Constant sequences embed the old space. A representative sequence itself proves density of that embedding. Taking limits of distances is legitimate because the reverse triangle inequality makes the scalar distance sequence Cauchy; changing representatives alters those distances by a quantity tending to zero. These are the checks that turn a formal quotient into a genuine metric space.

The relation between completeness and topology is developed further in [Complete Metric Spaces and Baire Category](complete-metric-spaces-baire-category.md). A complete compatible metric can exist even when the originally displayed metric is incomplete. Arbitrary products used in uniform compactness arguments rely on the choice-dependent product theorem proved in [Compactness: Covers and Finite Control](compactness-covers-and-finite-control.md).

For every $x$, $U_{\varepsilon}[x]=\{y:d(x,y)<\varepsilon\}=B_{d}(x,\varepsilon)$. Thus the uniform neighborhoods at $x$ include precisely a base of metric balls. A set contains a uniform ball about each of its points exactly when it contains a metric ball about each point, which is the definition of metric openness.


</details>

### TOP-CH13-E004 — Exercise 4

Prove that a uniformity is separated if and only if its induced topology is Hausdorff.


<details data-euler-reveal="hint">

</details>
<details data-euler-reveal="hint">
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

</details>
<details data-euler-reveal="solution">
<summary>Full solution</summary>

### TOP-CH13-E004 — Solution 4

If the intersection of all entourages is the diagonal and $x\ne y$, choose $U$ with $(x,y) \notin U$. Choose symmetric $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$. Conversely, if $(x,y)$ belongs to every entourage, then $y \in U[x]$ for every basic neighborhood $U[x]$ of $x$. Every neighborhood of $x$ contains $y$, preventing Hausdorff separation unless $x=y$.


</details>

### TOP-CH13-E005 — Exercise 5

### TOP-CH13-E013 — Exercise 13

### TOP-CH13-E020 — Exercise 20

Prove that a separated uniform space is totally bounded if and only if its completion is compact.


<details data-euler-reveal="hint">

</details>
<details data-euler-reveal="hint">
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

</details>
<details data-euler-reveal="solution">
<summary>Full solution</summary>

### TOP-CH13-E020 — Solution 20

Let $i:X\to\widehat{X}$ be the dense uniform embedding. If $X$ is totally bounded, then $i(X)$ is totally bounded. Given an entourage $U$ of $\widehat{X}$, choose a symmetric $V$ with $V \circ V \subseteq U$ and finitely many $V$-balls covering $i(X)$. Conversely, if $\widehat{X}$ is compact, it is totally bounded. Therefore its subspace $i(X)$, and hence the uniformly isomorphic space $X$, is totally bounded.

## Chapter checkpoint

Uniformities replace numerical radii by entourages and the triangle inequality by compositional shrinking. They induce topologies but retain more global information: compatible uniformities can disagree about uniform continuity, Cauchy objects, completeness, and total boundedness. Cauchy filters provide the scale-free language needed for general completions; in metric spaces, equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences give a concrete model. A separated uniform space is compact exactly when it is complete and totally bounded, and it is precompact exactly when its completion is compact. Boundedness alone carries neither conclusion.

The worked examples distinguish topology, uniformity, separation, finite nets, and completeness. Record which of these structures a claimed invariant actually uses.
