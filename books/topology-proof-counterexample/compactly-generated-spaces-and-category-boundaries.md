
# Compactly Generated Spaces and Category Boundaries

Ordinary topological spaces support products and quotient maps, but function spaces do not always satisfy the exponential law one expects from continuous families of maps. Compactly generated spaces repair this by making compact probes authoritative. The repair is useful precisely because it changes some constructions; it must not be treated as invisible notation.

## 1. Compactly detected closed sets

A subset $A$ of $X$ is **k-closed** when $f^{-1}(A)$ is closed in every compact Hausdorff $K$ for every continuous $f:K\to X$. A space is a **k-space** when every k-closed subset is closed.

Every closed set is k-closed by continuity. The converse is the defining content. Equivalent formulations use intersections $A \cap K$ for compact subspaces when $X$ has suitable separation; the map-based definition avoids hiding those conditions.

Locally compact Hausdorff spaces and first-countable spaces are k-spaces. If $x \in \operatorname{cl}(A)\setminus A$ in a first-countable space, choose $a_n\in A$ with $a_n\to x$. The compact Hausdorff convergent-sequence space $K=\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\ge1\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ maps continuously to $X$ by $0\mapsto x$ and $1/n\mapsto a_n$. The inverse image of $A$ contains every $1/n$ but not $0$, so it is not closed. This compact **domain** detects nonclosedness without assuming the sequence image is Hausdorff.

## 2. Kelleyfication

For a space $X$, its **k-ification** $kX$ has the same underlying set and declares k-open sets open; equivalently its closed sets are the k-closed sets of $X$. This topology is generally finer than the original because every original closed set is k-closed.

The identity $kX\to X$ is continuous. Compact Hausdorff probes into $X$ are exactly those into $kX$, so k-ification changes no compactly parameterized observations. Applying it twice changes nothing: $k(kX)=kX$.

A function from a k-space $X$ to $Y$ is continuous if its composites with all compact Hausdorff probes into $X$ are continuous. This final-topology property is the main testing rule.

## 3. Weak Hausdorffness

A space is **weak Hausdorff** when the image of every continuous map from a compact Hausdorff space is closed. Every Hausdorff space is weak Hausdorff because compact images are compact and compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed.

Weak Hausdorffness is weaker than Hausdorffness but strong enough to control compact probes and many quotient constructions. A common convenient category uses compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces. The chosen category must be stated; “compactly generated” alone does not silently add weak Hausdorffness.

## 4. Products in the convenient category

The ordinary product of two k-spaces need not be a k-space. Their categorical product is therefore

$X\times_kY=k(X\times Y)$.

For locally compact Hausdorff factors, ordinary and k-product agree. In general, forgetting the final k-ification can invalidate adjunction claims.

Subspaces are delicate as well. Closed subspaces of compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces behave well, while arbitrary subspaces may leave the convenient category unless k-ified.

## 5. Mapping spaces and the exponential law

For compactly generated $X$ and $Y$, start with the set $C(X,Y)$ of continuous maps. Given a compact Hausdorff space $K$, a continuous probe $u:K\to X$, and open $U\subseteq Y$, put

$$W(u,K,U)=\{f\in C(X,Y):f(u(K))\subseteq U\}.$$

Give $C(X,Y)$ the topology generated by these sets, then k-ify; call the result $\operatorname{Map}(X,Y)$. If $X$ is weak Hausdorff, every probe image $u(K)$ is a compact Hausdorff subspace, so this agrees with using the corresponding compact-Hausdorff-subspace sets. Without that hypothesis, replacing probes by an unqualified collection of compact subspaces hides a real separation condition.

For compactly generated weak Hausdorff $X,Y,Z$, the imported cartesian-closedness theorem gives a natural correspondence

$\operatorname{Map}(X\times_kY,Z)\cong\operatorname{Map}(X,\operatorname{Map}(Y,Z))$

at the level of continuous maps. This is the **exponential law** or cartesian closedness statement.

Set-theoretic currying always exists: $F(x,y)$ corresponds to $\widehat{F}(x)(y)$. The theorem concerns continuity in both directions, including continuity of

$\operatorname{ev}:\operatorname{Map}(Y,Z)\times_kY\to Z.$

The probe-generated topology supplies compact Hausdorff control; k-ification repairs the remaining final-topology issues. Strickland, *The Category of CGWH Spaces*, Definition 2.8, pp. 3--4, gives this mapping-space topology; Propositions 2.11--2.12 prove continuity of evaluation and the exponential law for compactly generated spaces, and Proposition 2.24 verifies that the mapping object is weak Hausdorff when its target is. Together these results give cartesian closedness for compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces. We use this theorem as the categorical boundary of the earlier locally compact exponential-law proof.

## 6. Quotients, colimits, and gluing

The ordinary topological quotient of a k-space is already a k-space. If $q:X\to Y$ is quotient and $A\subseteq Y$ pulls back closed under every compact Hausdorff probe into $Y$, then $q^{-1}(A)$ does so under every probe into $X$; k-generation of $X$ makes $q^{-1}(A)$ closed, and the quotient criterion makes $A$ closed. Disjoint unions of k-spaces are k-spaces by the same componentwise test. Since a topological colimit is a quotient of a disjoint union, ordinary colimits of k-spaces remain k-spaces; no extra k-ification is needed for that closure statement.

Weak Hausdorffness is separate. An ordinary colimit of compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces can fail to be weak Hausdorff, and k-ification alone cannot repair that separation failure. A construction inside a chosen weak-Hausdorff convenient category must use that category's colimit or verify the resulting weak Hausdorff condition. CW cell attachments satisfy the standard hypotheses and produce compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces.

The weak topology of a CW complex says a subset is closed when its intersection with every finite subcomplex (or characteristic disk, in an appropriate formulation) is closed. This is a compact-detection principle, not the claim that the complex is compact.

## 7. Category boundary audit

There are three distinct claims to track:

1. the underlying set construction;
2. the ordinary topology produced by product, quotient, or compact-open rules;
3. the k-ified object used in the convenient category.

A homeomorphism in ordinary spaces implies one after k-ification, but a convenient-category isomorphism may compare k-ifications rather than the original raw topologies. Similarly, a compact-open mapping space may require k-ification before evaluation or currying is continuous under the intended hypotheses.

## Proofs behind compact detection

**Proposition 21.1 (why k-ification is a topology and is idempotent).** The k-closed subsets of $X$ are the closed sets of a topology. The compact Hausdorff probes into this topology are exactly the original probes into $X$.

**Proof.** Inverse images commute with arbitrary intersections and finite unions. Therefore arbitrary intersections and finite unions of k-closed sets are again k-closed, and the empty and whole sets are k-closed. This gives the closed-set axioms. Every originally closed set is k-closed, so the identity $kX\to X$ is continuous.

Suppose $u:K\to X$ is an original continuous compact Hausdorff probe. For every closed set $A$ of $kX$, the definition of k-closedness says precisely that $u^{-1}(A)$ is closed in $K$. Thus the same function $u:K\to kX$ is continuous. Conversely a probe into $kX$ composed with the continuous identity is a probe into $X$. The two collections of probes coincide. Since k-closedness is specified entirely by this collection, applying the construction again gives exactly the same closed sets. $\square$

**Proposition 21.2 (the final testing rule).** Let $X$ be a k-space. A function $g:X\to Y$ is continuous if $g\circ u$ is continuous for every compact Hausdorff probe $u:K\to X$.

**Proof.** For a closed subset $F$ of $Y$, the set $g^{-1}(F)$ pulls back under $u$ to $(g\circ u)^{-1}(F)$, which is closed. Thus $g^{-1}(F)$ is k-closed in $X$, hence closed. This is continuity by the closed-set criterion. The converse follows from composition. No separation hypothesis on $Y$ is needed. $\square$

**Proposition 21.3 (locally compact Hausdorff spaces are k-spaces).** Suppose $X$ is locally compact Hausdorff and $A$ is not closed. Choose $x\in\overline A\setminus A$. A compact neighborhood $K$ of $x$ is Hausdorff as a subspace. Every relatively open neighborhood of $x$ in $K$ meets $A\cap K$: intersect its ambient representative with an open neighborhood of $x$ contained in $K$, then use $x\in\overline A$. Hence $A\cap K$ is not closed in $K$. The inclusion probe $K\to X$ detects this failure. Therefore k-closed sets in $X$ are closed. $\square$

The proof uses a compact neighborhood rather than an arbitrary compact set through $x$. It also uses Hausdorffness to make the domain $K$ an allowed probe. For first-countable spaces, the convergent-sequence domain used earlier avoids needing the image to be Hausdorff.

## Worked examples of the convention

### Worked example 1 — A k-space need not close its points

Let $S=\{0,1\}$ have open sets $\varnothing,\{1\},S$. It is first-countable: at zero the single neighborhood $S$ is a local base, and at one the singleton is a local base. Consequently it is a k-space by the sequential-probe argument.

It is not weak Hausdorff. The map from the one-point compact Hausdorff space to $S$ taking its point to one is continuous, but its image $\{1\}$ is not closed. In particular the compactly generated condition and the weak Hausdorff condition are independent pieces of the chosen category definition. Merely applying k-ification does not repair this example, because $kS=S$ already.

There are also probes whose image is the whole non-Hausdorff space. On $[0,1]$, let $u(t)=1$ for $t>0$ and $u(0)=0$. The inverse image of $\{1\}$ is $(0,1]$, open in the relative topology, so $u$ is continuous. Its image is $S$, not a Hausdorff subspace. This directly demonstrates why the domain condition cannot simply be moved to the image.

### Worked example 2 — Countable discrete domains recover a familiar mapping space

Take $X=\mathbb N$ discrete and $Y=\mathbb R$. Every compact subset of $X$ is finite, by its singleton cover. Every compact Hausdorff probe into $X$ has finite image, since its image is compact. Conversely each finite subset is the image of a finite discrete compact probe.

The raw probe-generated mapping topology is therefore exactly the product topology on $\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}$. Its basic conditions control finitely many coordinates. This product is metrizable, for example by
$D(a,b)=\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}\min(1,|a_n-b_n|).$
The usual finite-head and small-tail estimates verify the product topology. Hence it is first-countable and already a k-space: the final k-ification changes nothing in this example.

Joint evaluation is continuous even for the ordinary product with $\mathbb N$. At a pair $(f,n)$, the domain coordinate can be restricted to the open singleton $\{n\}$, leaving only one continuous coordinate evaluation. This special calculation does not establish ordinary evaluation for arbitrary non-locally-compact domains.

### Worked example 3 — A quotient preserves generation but may lose separation

Start with the disjoint union of two ordinary real lines and identify corresponding nonzero points while leaving the two origins distinct. The source is locally compact Hausdorff, hence a k-space. Its ordinary quotient is therefore a k-space by the quotient theorem proved above.

The two origins cannot be separated by disjoint open neighborhoods: both neighborhoods contain sufficiently small shared nonzero points. More strongly the quotient fails weak Hausdorffness. Map the compact convergent-sequence space $\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\ge1\}$ to the quotient by sending zero to the first origin and $1/n$ to the shared point $1/n$. This is continuous. Its image excludes the second origin, but that origin is in its closure, so the image is not closed.

Thus an ordinary quotient of a Hausdorff k-space can remain compactly generated while losing weak Hausdorffness. The extra separation step in a convenient-category colimit is doing actual work. It cannot be replaced by k-ification, because the ordinary quotient here is already k-generated.

## What the categorical theorem adds

These elementary propositions establish the compact testing mechanism, its idempotence, and important examples. They do not by themselves prove cartesian closedness. That theorem must simultaneously identify the correct product, mapping topology, and continuous evaluation. It is an imported theorem at the stated categorical boundary, with its precise source retained above.

A reliable use of the exponential law begins by specifying the category of objects and the topology on the mapping set. It then checks that each object belongs to that category. Only then does it identify a continuously parameterized family with its curried map. Set-theoretic formulas alone verify none of those topological requirements. This distinction is especially useful in homotopy theory, where parameter spaces such as intervals and spheres are compact Hausdorff but the spaces being parameterized need not be locally compact.

## Chapter checkpoint

Compactly generated spaces make compact Hausdorff probes determine topology. In the compactly generated weak Hausdorff category, the probe-generated mapping topology and k-ification make currying and evaluation behave categorically; ordinary quotients and colimits of k-spaces are already k-spaces. Weak Hausdorffness is an additional separation condition and can fail under ordinary colimits. The reward is a convenient setting for homotopy and cell complexes; the cost is an obligation to name which topology and which category each construction uses.

Compact Hausdorff domains remain the probes throughout. The elementary testing proofs do not erase the distinction between compact generation and weak Hausdorffness, or between an ordinary construction and the construction in the convenient category.
