
# Lesson-study facilitation and public professional knowledge

Chapter 4 presented lesson study as collaborative inquiry: study, plan, teach, observe, discuss, revise. This chapter focuses on facilitation—how a team sustains the inquiry mechanism through norms, roles, and structured conversation—and on how lesson-study products can become public professional knowledge without overstating their warrant. Facilitation is not performance coaching. Its object is a testable conjecture about mathematics learning.

## 1. Facilitation is the stewardship of inquiry

A facilitator does not own the correct answer. They steward a process in which the team studies mathematics, anticipates learner reasoning, collects low-inference evidence, and revises conjectures. Facilitation skills include:

- keeping the mathematical goal precise and visible;
- separating record from interpretation;
- surfacing rival explanations before consensus;
- protecting participants from evaluative pressure;
- linking discussion to the conjecture map;
- documenting decisions and their evidence basis.

**Definition 8.1.** A *facilitation move* is an intervention in the team conversation that advances the inquiry function without substituting the facilitator's judgment for the team's evidence.

## 2. Establishing norms before content

Before selecting a mathematical focus, agree on how the team will work:

- observation follows the research question, not personnel appraisal;
- records are low-inference and time-linked;
- interpretations are offered as hypotheses, not verdicts;
- disagreement is expected and examined, not smoothed over;
- revisions change one feature at a time with a new prediction;
- external research is appraised and tested locally, not imported as script.

These norms are teaching conjectures about productive collaboration. They are not research findings that every team adopting them will succeed.

## 3. The facilitator's pre-cycle work

Before the first meeting, the facilitator:

1. identifies a mathematical focus with the team, linked to a curriculum gap or learner pattern;
2. ensures the team has access to relevant research, curriculum materials, and prior learner work;
3. schedules enough time for study, planning, observation, and two discussion sessions;
4. clarifies ethics, consent, recording, and data handling with institutional authorities;
5. prepares a template for the conjecture map, observation notes, and revision record.

## 4. Mathematical study: the foundation

The team must understand the mathematics deeply before writing tasks. For a focus on proportional reasoning, the team should:

- define proportionality and distinguish it from additive reasoning;
- identify valid strategies: unit rate, scale factor, cross-multiplication with justification, table construction;
- list common responses: additive error, same-difference error, guess-and-check without structure;
- map forward and backward connections: fractions, ratios, slope, percent, similarity;
- anticipate which representations may help and which may mislead.

**Mathematical result:** for positive quantities, $a/b = c/d$ exactly when $ad = bc$. Cross-multiplication is valid but can obscure the multiplicative structure. A unit-rate argument may be more transparent for some learners.

## 5. Anticipation without labeling

Anticipating learner responses prepares observers to notice. It does not assign responses to children in advance.

### Worked anticipation for proportionality

Task: "A recipe uses 3 cups of flour for 4 cups of milk. How much flour for 10 cups of milk?"

| Anticipated response | Mathematical status | Follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Add 6 to 3 because 10 is 6 more than 4" | additive error | ask what stays the same in the recipe |
| "Two and a half batches, so 7.5 cups" | valid scale-factor strategy | ask for a table showing the pattern |
| "3/4 = x/10, so x = 7.5" | valid cross-multiplication | ask what 3/4 means in the context |
| "I guessed 8" | unsupported | ask for a check using the ratio |

Observers prepare to record which strategy appears, not to judge the learner.

## 6. Observation structures and templates

Assign observers to evidence zones: selected pairs, representation changes, teacher moves, or transfer responses. A timestamped template supports low-inference recording:

```text
time | task state | exact words/action | artifact | observer hypothesis | follow-up needed
```

**Common error:** observers write evaluative summaries instead of records. "The group was stuck" is an interpretation. "From 10:15 to 10:22, no writing; one learner read the problem aloud three times" is a record.

## 7. The evidence-first discussion protocol

Use this order, enforced by the facilitator:

1. **Teacher narrative:** intended plan, actual pivots, and reasons.
2. **Observer records:** time-linked, low-inference, with artifacts.
3. **Comparison:** anticipated responses versus observed responses.
4. **Missing evidence:** what was not recorded, what failed, what was ambiguous.
5. **Mathematical revisit:** does the evidence support, contradict, or leave open the conjecture?
6. **Rival explanations:** what else could account for the pattern?
7. **One revision:** change one feature, state a new prediction, and specify next evidence.

The facilitator interrupts premature interpretation, redirects to records, and ensures minority views are heard.

### Focus

Proportionality: does a double-number-line representation help learners distinguish multiplicative from additive reasoning in missing-value problems?

### Conjecture map excerpt

| Lesson moment | Mathematical purpose | Anticipated activity | Evidence | Contingent response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| present recipe problem without diagram | elicit initial strategy | additive or multiplicative attempt | written strategy label | offer double number line if additive |
| introduce double number line | make coordinated units visible | mark flour and milk alignments | accuracy of alignment and explanation | ask what stays equal |

### Low-inference record

| Pair | Initial strategy | Number-line episode | Transfer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | additive: 3+6=9 | aligned 3-4 and 6-8, then adjusted to 7.5-10 | multiplicative table, no number line |
| B | multiplicative: 3/4 times 10 | confirmed alignment, explained "same ratio" | cross-multiplication correctly |
| C | additive: 3+6=9 | watched partner align, no independent work | additive again, 9 |
| D | guessed 8 | aligned 3-4 and 9-10 incorrectly | no transfer artifact |

### Facilitated discussion

Facilitator: "Begin with records. What did you see?"

Observer 1: "Pair A used additive initially, then the number line, then a table at transfer."

Observer 2: "Pair B was already multiplicative. The number line confirmed what they knew."

Facilitator: "What is missing?"

Observer 1: "We don't know if Pair A would have used a table without the number line."

Observer 2: "Pair C repeated additive after watching. Was the number line too brief?"

Facilitator: "Rival explanations for Pair C?"

Observer 3: "They may need more time. They may need a different representation. They may not yet see why additive fails."

Facilitator: "What one revision and prediction follows?"

Team: "Keep the number line but add an individual prediction before pair work. Prediction: initial reasoning becomes visible, and we can compare partner influence with independent strategy."

## 9. Public professional knowledge: what to share

A lesson-study product can leave the team if it includes:

- the mathematical map with definitions, strategies, and boundary cases;
- the conjecture map with predictions and evidence criteria;
- anonymized response categories, not individual portraits;
- the revision logic, including what was discarded and why;
- the access plan and its rationale;
- limitations: sample, setting, missing evidence, and what remains untested.

Do not share a "best lesson" stripped of context. A useful product helps another team test and adapt the conjecture.

## 10. Failure modes in facilitation

| Failure | Why it fails | Facilitator repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| facilitator answers mathematical questions | team does not develop independent mathematical authority | ask the team to prove or justify |
| discussion jumps to "what went wrong" | records are skipped, interpretations dominate | enforce evidence-first protocol |
| one voice dominates | minority hypotheses are lost | call on quiet members, use written reflection first |
| revision changes five features | mechanism is uninterpretable | insist on one change and a new prediction |
| team treats external research as script | local testing is abandoned | ask how the study's sample and design differ |
| products omit limitations | next team overgeneralizes | require a limitations paragraph before sharing |

## 11. Scaling and sustainability

Lesson study can spread through:

- open sharing of conjecture maps and revisions;
- cross-school observation with shared mathematical focus;
- facilitator development programs;
- integration with curriculum materials.

**Research finding, bounded.** A randomized trial by Lewis and Perry studied lesson study supported by fractions resource kits across 39 educator teams and reported improvements on named educator and learner outcomes relative to controls. That intervention included particular resources and support; it does not isolate a context-free "lesson study effect." Conditions matter: scheduled time, mathematical and research expertise, psychological safety, stable participation, and authority to revise.

## 12. Equity in facilitation

- Ensure quiet members and early-career teachers have speaking space.
- Use the team's language resources; do not privilege one dialect or register.
- Make mathematical authority distributed, not concentrated in the facilitator or a senior member.
- Interpret unfamiliar contributions generously before diagnosing error.
- Collect only records needed for the research question.
- Share products only with consent and under agreed terms.

## 13. Second worked facilitation episode: early-number place value

The following dataset is invented for professional rehearsal. It contains no learner data.

### Focus

Place value: does bundling ten loose objects into a container help learners connect the count "fourteen" to the notation $14 = 1 \times 10 + 4$?

### Mathematical map

**Definition 8.2.** A *positional numeral system* with base ten represents each whole number as a sum of powers of ten. The notation $14$ means $1 \times 10 + 4 \times 1$. A *bundle* is a physical or drawn grouping of ten individual units. The numeral $14$ is not the same object as fourteen loose counters; it encodes a structure.

Common responses to anticipate:

| Anticipated response | Mathematical status | Follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| counts fourteen loose objects correctly, writes 14 | may be rote symbol matching | ask what the 1 means |
| makes one bundle of ten and four loose, writes 14 | valid structural connection | ask what would happen with twenty-four |
| makes one bundle of ten and four loose, writes 104 | place-value confusion | ask what each digit represents |
| counts by ones to fourteen, resists bundling | additive strategy, not yet structural | ask whether the bundle helps keep track |

### Conjecture map excerpt

| Lesson moment | Mathematical purpose | Anticipated activity | Evidence | Contingent response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| count fourteen loose counters | establish cardinality | accurate count, one-to-one correspondence | verbal count, final number | offer a container if counting is accurate |
| bundle ten and leave four loose | make the decade structure visible | place ten in container, count remaining | physical arrangement, verbal description | ask what the bundle is worth |
| write the numeral | connect physical structure to notation | write 14, explain digits | written numeral, explanation | probe meaning of each digit |
| transfer to twenty-three | test independence from initial example | make bundles, write numeral | written work, explanation | collect before summary |

### Low-inference record

| Pair | Counting episode | Bundling episode | Notation | Transfer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E | counted 14 accurately by ones | placed 10 in container, 4 outside; said "ten and four more" | wrote 14; said "one ten and four" | made 2 bundles, 3 loose; wrote 23; said "two tens and three" |
| F | counted 14 accurately by ones | placed 10 in container; left 4 loose but did not mention the container | wrote 14; could not explain the 1 | made 2 bundles, 3 loose; wrote 203 |
| G | counted 14 accurately by ones | refused container; counted all by ones again | wrote 14; said "I counted" | counted 23 by ones; wrote 23; no bundles |
| H | counted 14 accurately by ones | placed 10 in container, 4 outside; said "this is ten" | wrote 14; said "one group of ten and four left" | made 2 bundles, 3 loose; wrote 23; said "two groups of ten and three" |

### Facilitated discussion

Facilitator: "Begin with records. What did you see?"

Observer 1: "Pairs E and H connected the bundle to the digits. Pair F wrote 14 but could not explain the 1. Pair G never used the bundle."

Facilitator: "What is missing?"

Observer 2: "We don't know if Pair G would have used bundles if the container had been introduced differently."

Observer 3: "Pair F wrote 203 at transfer. We need to know whether they think there are two hundreds or whether they are listing counts."

Facilitator: "Rival explanations for Pair F's 203?"

Observer 1: "They may think each bundle gets its own digit place. They may be listing 'two bundles, zero something, three loose.' They may not yet see that the position determines the value."

Facilitator: "What one revision and prediction follows?"

Team: "Before writing the numeral, have learners label the container '10' and the loose counters individually. Then write the numeral together. Prediction: the explicit label reduces 104 or 203 errors because the connection between physical group and digit value is made visible before notation is required."

## 14. Facilitator moves catalog

A facilitation move is chosen for the problem it addresses. Overusing any move creates a new problem.

| Move | Problem it addresses | Example phrasing | Risk if overused |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Redirect to record | interpretation outruns evidence | "What did you see or hear at that moment?" | team may feel surveilled or silenced; trust erodes |
| Redirect to mathematics | discussion drifts from the content | "What is the mathematical relationship we are testing?" | facilitator becomes the mathematics authority rather than the steward |
| Surface rival | premature consensus | "What else could explain that pattern?" | team may produce superficial alternatives to satisfy the prompt |
| Write first | dominant voices control airtime | "Take two minutes to write your hypothesis before we discuss." | slows discussion; may frustrate members who think aloud |
| Call on quiet member | minority views are lost | "We have not heard from everyone; [name], what did you record?" | puts individual on the spot; may feel tokenized |
| Restate and check | contribution is unclear or buried | "I hear you saying X; is that accurate?" | can distort the original meaning if the facilitator mishears |
| Link to conjecture map | revision loses its prediction | "Which conjecture does this evidence test, and what would falsify it?" | may force every observation into a rigid frame, missing emergent patterns |
| Name the silence | discomfort with disagreement | "We have three different views on the table. That is expected." | naming alone does not resolve power asymmetries that make disagreement costly |
| Table for later | digression threatens the protocol | "That is an important question; let's record it and return after we finish the evidence." | deferred items may never be addressed, breeding cynicism |
| Limit revision scope | multiple features change at once | "Choose one change and state the new prediction." | may oversimplify when two features are genuinely entangled |

No move is universally appropriate. The facilitator's judgment is a local teaching conjecture about what will advance inquiry in this conversation. That judgment is fallible and should be discussed in facilitator-development sessions.

## 15. Handling disagreement about the mathematics itself

Sometimes the team's mathematics is wrong mid-cycle. A facilitator must act without becoming the answer authority.

**Teaching conjecture.** When a team holds an incorrect mathematical claim, the facilitator's first response should be to ask for justification rather than to correct. Correction short-circuits inquiry; unjustified acceptance propagates error.

Consider a team planning a lesson on fraction comparison that asserts: "To compare $2/3$ and $3/4$, you just cross-multiply: $2 \times 4 = 8$ and $3 \times 3 = 9$, so $2/3 < 3/4$." The mathematical result is correct for this pair, but the reasoning is fragile. The book adopts the usual positive-denominator convention, under which the inequality direction is preserved. More generally, multiplying by the denominator product preserves direction when $bd>0$ and reverses it when $bd<0$. The team has not stated its convention or explained why the method works.

Facilitator move: "Show me why $2/3 < 3/4$ using a representation other than cross-multiplication." This redirects to mathematical justification without the facilitator supplying the proof.

If the team produces a common-denominator argument—$2/3 = 8/12$ and $3/4 = 9/12$, so $2/3 < 3/4$—the facilitator can ask: "What does cross-multiplication do that common denominator also does?" The team may discover that $2 \times 4$ and $3 \times 3$ are the numerators after scaling to twelfths.

If the team persists in an error—for example, claiming that $1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5$—the facilitator can ask: "Draw a picture of one half and one third. Does the shaded amount look like two fifths?" The representation tests the claim. If the team sees the discrepancy, they revise. If they do not, the facilitator may need to table the issue, assign individual study, and reconvene.

**Boundary.** The facilitator is not forbidden from stating a mathematical result. If a team is stuck and time is limited, the facilitator may say: "For positive denominators, $a/b < c/d$ exactly when $ad < bc$. This is a theorem. Your task is to decide whether learners need to know why it works, and if so, which representation makes the why visible." The facilitator states the result but returns authority to the team for pedagogical decisions.

## 16. The outside commentator role

An outside commentator is a knowledgeable visitor who attends the research lesson and the post-lesson discussion. They are not an inspector, evaluator, or director.

**Selection.** Choose a commentator with mathematical and pedagogical expertise relevant to the team's focus. A university mathematician may see structural connections the team missed; an experienced teacher from another school may recognize implementation patterns. Avoid commentators whose institutional role carries appraisal authority over team members.

**Briefing.** Before the cycle, the facilitator shares the conjecture map, the mathematical focus, and the evidence-first protocol with the commentator. The commentator agrees to:

- observe the research lesson as an evidence collector, not a critic;
- speak last in the post-lesson discussion, after teacher narrative and observer records;
- connect evidence to mathematics and research without delivering personnel verdicts;
- ask questions rather than pronounce answers.

**Boundaries.** The commentator does not:

- rewrite the lesson plan;
- rank the teacher;
- import external research as a verdict on the local cycle;
- dominate discussion time.

A well-briefed commentator might say: "I noticed that three pairs used counting-on after the bundling episode. In the research literature on place value, counting-on is sometimes treated as a precursor to unitizing. Your conjecture map tests whether bundling prompts unitizing. The evidence I saw is consistent with partial unitizing, but I could not tell whether the learners saw the container as a ten-unit or as a convenient pile." This connects local evidence to external knowledge without overriding the team's interpretive work.

## 17. Documentation systems: conjecture maps, revision records, and public professional knowledge

A team's archive becomes public professional knowledge only when it is structured, anonymized, and bounded.

### Conjecture maps

A conjecture map records the team's predictions. Each row links a lesson moment to a mathematical purpose, an anticipated activity, an evidence criterion, and a contingent response. The map is a living document: anticipated activities are revised during planning; evidence criteria are refined after observation.

### Revision records

Each revision should be recorded with:

```text
original feature:
intended mechanism:
observed evidence:
counterevidence/missing evidence:
revised feature:
new prediction:
evidence to collect next:
```

This format prevents the team from forgetting what was discarded and why. A revision record without counterevidence is incomplete; it risks presenting the revision as inevitable rather than tested.

### Worked anonymized product excerpt

The following excerpt is invented for illustration. It contains no learner data.

**Mathematical focus:** place-value structure of two-digit numbers.

**Conjecture:** If learners label a bundle of ten as "10" before writing the numeral, they will less often write 104 or 203 for fourteen or twenty-three.

**Evidence:** In a cycle with four observed pairs, two pairs who labeled the bundle correctly wrote 14 and 23; one pair who did not label wrote 203; one pair who refused bundling wrote 23 by counting.

**Revision:** Require explicit labeling of the bundle and loose counters before notation. Prediction: the rate of place-value confusion decreases.

**Limitations:** Four pairs, one cycle, no counterfactual, one researcher-teacher. The label may help only when learners already accept bundling. Pair G's refusal suggests that container choice matters and was not tested.

This excerpt is useful because it states the conjecture, the evidence, the revision logic, and the limitations. Another team can test the conjecture in their setting and compare results.

## 18. Sustainability: facilitator development, cross-school networks, and ritual degradation

Lesson study degrades when its forms are preserved but its inquiry function is lost.

**Possible failure mode.** A team that loses scheduled time, mathematical expertise, or psychological safety may keep the name "lesson study" while omitting serious anticipation, low-inference observation, or evidence-first discussion. Whether this occurs in a setting is an empirical question requiring records; the sentence names a risk to audit, not a prevalence finding.

**Teaching conjecture.** Cross-school networks sustain lesson study by exposing teams to external conjectures, by creating accountability for documentation, and by rotating facilitation roles so that no single person becomes indispensable. A network that meets only to share success stories, however, may reinforce ritual by rewarding polished products over honest inquiry.

**Conditions that predict degradation:**

| Condition | Why it degrades inquiry | Possible repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mandatory participation without choice of focus | teachers study mathematics they do not find problematic | negotiate focus from curriculum gaps the team identifies |
| evaluation-linked observation | records become defensive rather than investigative | separate lesson-study observation from appraisal governance |
| facilitator never develops | moves become habitual and poorly timed | rotate facilitation; attend external development sessions |
| no time for study or discussion | planning and observation are rushed | protect minimum cycle length in scheduling |
| products shared only as success stories | negative evidence and revisions are hidden | require limitations paragraphs; share revision records, not only final lessons |
| no cross-school connection | team recycles its own assumptions | invite outside commentators; read conjecture maps from other teams |

**Research finding, bounded.** Lewis, Perry, and Hurd's theoretical model proposes that lesson study affects instruction through pathways involving knowledge and beliefs, professional community, and teaching-learning resources. The model does not claim that these pathways operate automatically; it identifies them as mechanisms to investigate. A randomized trial by Lewis and Perry found effects under resource-supported conditions; whether those effects persist when support is withdrawn remains an open question.

## 19. Equity in facilitation extended: labor, credit, and career-stage asymmetries

Equity in lesson study is not only about speaking time. It is also about who does the invisible labor, who receives credit, and whose career stage creates vulnerability.

**Labor distribution.** Taking notes, preparing materials, scheduling rooms, and cleaning up are real work. If early-career teachers or support staff perform this labor while senior teachers lead the intellectual discussion, the team reproduces hierarchy under the guise of collaboration. A facilitator should rotate roles explicitly: who takes observer notes, who prepares the conjecture map template, who writes the revision record.

**Credit.** When a lesson-study product is shared, whose names appear? If the teacher whose classroom was used is listed as sole author while the team that designed the conjecture map is omitted, the incentive structure rewards individual performance over collective inquiry. Shared authorship or explicit team acknowledgment is a norm, not a universal rule.

**Career-stage asymmetries.** Early-career teachers may fear that critical observation will affect their employment. They may agree with senior colleagues to avoid conflict. They may teach the research lesson more often because their schedules are more flexible. A facilitator can:

- ensure that research-lesson teaching rotates, not concentrates on the most junior member;
- state explicitly that observation is not appraisal;
- invite early-career teachers to comment first in discussion, before senior voices set the frame;
- protect dissent by recording it before evaluating it.

**Teaching conjecture.** Teams with stable membership, protected time, and explicit role rotation are more likely to sustain equitable inquiry than teams with high turnover, evaluative observation, and implicit labor hierarchies. This is a conjecture, not a research finding; it should be tested locally.

## Solutions

1. Record: "In the transfer task, three of four observed pairs did not use the target strategy." Interpretation: "The representation may not have transferred." Rival: "The transfer task was too different." Follow-up: keep the representation but make the transfer task more similar, with one changed feature.
2. Changing multiple features makes it impossible to attribute any observed change to a specific mechanism. One change with a new prediction preserves testability.
3. Template columns: time, task state, learner action or exact words, area model used (yes/no/how), justification given (yes/no/summary), observer hypothesis, follow-up.
4. Consent for sharing and in what form; re-identification risk from voice, clothing, or background; institutional approval; intended audience; access controls; and whether the clips are necessary or whether anonymized transcripts suffice.
5. "What did you see or hear at that moment?" and "Can you read the exact record from your notes?" Both redirect from interpretation to observable evidence.
6. Pair D guessed initially, aligned the number line incorrectly, and produced no transfer artifact. Their reasoning is largely unavailable; the incorrect alignment suggests the number line was not understood, but missing data prevent a firm conclusion.
7. Facilitator move: "Before we respond, let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet." Norm: "Every contribution is recorded before it is evaluated."
8. Mathematical result; fictional local observation; external research finding bounded to that study; local teaching conjecture.
9. "Four pairs were observed, with audio failure for one pair. No counterfactual condition was included. Patterns cannot be generalized beyond the observed tasks and setting. Missing data and small sample limit conclusions about transfer and mechanism."
10. Agenda: (1) introduce lesson-study cycle and norms; (2) practice low-inference observation with a video excerpt; (3) study a mathematical focus together; (4) draft a conjecture map; (5) review ethics and recording procedures; (6) schedule the cycle; (7) plan the first evidence-first discussion.
11. Rival 1: Pair F thinks each bundle gets its own digit place, so two bundles and three loose become 203. Discriminating evidence: ask what the 2 means and what the 0 means. Rival 2: Pair F is listing counts in order—two bundles, zero something, three loose—without understanding positional value. Discriminating evidence: ask them to read 203 aloud and compare with the physical arrangement. Rival 3: Pair F has seen three-digit numbers more often than two-digit numbers and overgeneralizes the length of the numeral. Discriminating evidence: present 23 and 203 side by side and ask which matches the physical arrangement.
12. Facilitator move: "Cross-multiplication gives a correct answer for positive fractions. Show me why it works using a common-denominator representation, and then show me a case where a learner might use cross-multiplication and get a correct answer without understanding why." This asks the team to justify and to anticipate, preserving their authority over pedagogy while requiring mathematical depth.
13. Briefing checklist: (1) share conjecture map and mathematical focus; (2) explain evidence-first protocol order; (3) confirm commentator will speak last; (4) confirm no personnel evaluation; (5) share observation template; (6) agree on questions rather than verdicts; (7) schedule pre-cycle and post-cycle briefings.
14. A revision record without counterevidence presents the revision as if the evidence unambiguously supported it. The facilitator should ask: "What evidence contradicted our conjecture, or what evidence did we fail to collect?" and require that the missing or counter evidence be recorded before the revision is accepted.
15. Condition 1: mandatory participation without choice of focus. Repair: negotiate focus from curriculum gaps the team identifies. Condition 2: evaluation-linked observation. Repair: separate lesson-study observation from appraisal governance, or disclose appraisal honestly if separation is impossible.
16. Facilitator move: "We have not yet heard a research lesson from everyone. Who would like to teach the next cycle?" Structural change: establish a rotation schedule at the start of the year, with opt-in and protected time for research-lesson preparation.
17. Example: overusing "Redirect to record." If the facilitator interrupts every interpretive comment with "What did you see?", team members may stop offering hypotheses altogether. Inquiry requires both records and interpretations; the facilitator's job is to keep them in the right order, not to eliminate interpretation.
18. Step 1: "Draw a rectangle and shade half of it. Now shade half of the shaded half. What fraction of the whole is doubly shaded?" Step 2: If the team draws 1/4, ask them to explain the operation that produced it. Step 3: If the team says 0.10, ask them to draw 0.10 on the same diagram and compare the shaded areas. Step 4: If disagreement persists, the facilitator may state: "By definition of decimal multiplication, $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$. The pedagogical question is why learners might predict 0.10 and what representation would make the correct product visible." The facilitator states the result only after the team has explored representations, and immediately returns to pedagogical authority.

## Project: Design a facilitator handbook entry for a new mathematical focus

Choose a mathematical focus not used in this chapter (for example, integer addition on a number line, covariation in a linear table, or geometric transformation composition). Write a facilitator handbook entry of approximately 800–1,000 words that includes:

1. a mathematical map with definitions, valid strategies, common responses, and boundary cases;
2. a conjecture map with at least three lesson moments, each with mathematical purpose, anticipated activity, evidence criterion, and contingent response;
3. a low-inference observation template tailored to the focus;
4. two anticipated facilitation challenges (for example, a team member who wants to change multiple features, or a dominant senior voice) with the specific move you would use and the risk of overusing it;
5. a limitations paragraph that honestly bounds what the cycle can claim.

The entry should follow the claim-status discipline of this chapter and should be usable by a facilitator who has read Chapters 4 and 4a but is not an expert in the specific mathematical focus.

### Solution blueprint

**Mathematical focus:** integer addition on a number line, specifically why $(-3) + 5 = 2$ and not $-8$.

**Mathematical map.** **Definition.** An *integer* is a member of the set $\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}$. Integer addition on a number line is defined as directed displacement: the first addend locates the starting point, the sign of the second addend indicates direction, and its absolute value indicates distance. **Valid strategies:** directed displacement on a number line; chip model with zero pairs; algebraic decomposition $(-3) + 5 = (-3) + 3 + 2 = 0 + 2 = 2$. **Common responses:** sign error $(-3) + 5 = -8$; magnitude-only error $-3 + 5 = 8$; correct answer without justification. **Boundary cases:** adding two negatives; adding opposites; adding zero; commutativity with mixed signs.

**Conjecture map.**

| Lesson moment | Mathematical purpose | Anticipated activity | Evidence | Contingent response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| present $(-3) + 5$ without diagram | elicit initial strategy | sign error, magnitude error, or correct guess | written answer and explanation | offer number line if strategy is unclear |
| model as directed displacement | make direction and distance visible | start at $-3$, move 5 units right | accuracy of endpoint and explanation | ask what would happen if we moved left |
| transfer to $(-4) + (-2)$ | test independence from positive second addend | apply directed displacement or revert to rule | written work and explanation | collect before summary |

**Observation template.** Columns: time; task state; exact words or action; number-line used (yes/no/how); direction identified correctly (yes/no); final answer; justification given (yes/no/summary); observer hypothesis; follow-up needed.

**Facilitation challenge 1:** a team member wants to add a chip model, a number line, and an algebraic decomposition all in one lesson. **Move:** "Each representation is valuable. Choose one to introduce first, state the prediction for that representation, and schedule the others for later cycles." **Risk if overused:** the team may feel that their expertise is undervalued; use only after acknowledging the validity of each representation.

**Facilitation challenge 2:** a senior teacher says, "They just need to memorize the rules: same sign add, different sign subtract." **Move:** "That rule produces correct answers. Before we settle on it, let's anticipate what a learner who memorizes the rule might do on $(-4) + (-2)$ if they forget whether to add or subtract magnitudes." **Risk if overused:** the facilitator may appear to dismiss efficient computation; balance by acknowledging that fluency matters and asking when justification becomes necessary.

**Limitations paragraph.** "This cycle observed four pairs in one classroom with one teacher-researcher. No counterfactual condition was included. The number line was the only representation tested; chip and algebraic models remain unexamined. Patterns cannot be generalized to other integer operations, other representations, or other settings. Missing data and small sample limit conclusions about transfer and mechanism."
