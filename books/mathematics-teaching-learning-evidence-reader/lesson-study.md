
# Professional learning through lesson study

Lesson study is collaborative inquiry in which educators investigate mathematics and curriculum, plan a research lesson, observe learner activity, discuss evidence, and revise shared knowledge or design. Its object is a learning conjecture—not the performance rating of the person teaching. The point is not a universally "perfect lesson."

One useful cycle is:

```text
study -> plan -> teach/observe -> discuss -> revise/share -> next question
```

Local forms differ. Preserve the functions—serious mathematical study, anticipation of learner reasoning, observation tied to a question, and evidence-based revision—rather than copying ceremonial features.

## 1. Establish a safe professional compact

Before selecting a lesson, agree:

- who participates and how decisions are made;
- that observation follows the research question rather than personnel appraisal;
- what records can be collected, by whom, and for how long;
- required institutional review, family consent, learner assent, and opt-out routes;
- who may see quotations, images, audio, or work samples;
- how accessibility needs and ordinary teaching adjustments override the plan;
- how disagreement, facilitator authority, and outside expertise will work;
- which products can leave the team and how participants review them.

If observation affects employment, say so explicitly; calling it "collaboration" does not erase power. If learners cannot decline recording without losing instruction, voluntariness requires special care. A research lesson can be studied with low-inference notes and de-identified artifacts; video is not automatically necessary.

## 2. Begin with a research theme and bounded question

A long-term theme might be "learners use representations to justify rather than decorate." A bounded question might be:

> When comparing $5/8$ and $3/4$, how do learners coordinate a common whole with symbolic equivalence, and which task contrast prompts them to revise numerator-only reasoning?

The question names observable mathematical activity. "Will the lesson be engaging?" is too diffuse. "Will learners get the right answer?" is too narrow.

### Writing the bounded research question: a worked ladder

A diffuse question must be narrowed through successive rewrites, each making the observation target more exact. Consider this progression:

**Draft 1 (too diffuse):** "How can we improve learners' understanding of fractions?"

This names a topic but no observable activity, no specific mathematical relation, and no evidence criterion. Improvement is a goal, not a research question.

**Draft 2 (still diffuse):** "Do area models help learners understand fraction comparison?"

This narrows to a representation and a topic, but "understand" remains unobservable, and "help" implies a causal claim that a single lesson cannot warrant.

**Draft 3 (bounded):** "When learners compare fractions using area models, which specific model features prompt them to coordinate the whole, and what does their revised language or action look like?"

This names the mathematical activity (coordinate the whole), the representation feature (model structure), and the evidence (revised language or action). It is bounded enough to plan observation and revision.

A second example:

**Draft 1:** "How do we teach proportional reasoning better?"

**Draft 2:** "Does a double number line improve proportional reasoning?"

**Draft 3 (bounded):** "In missing-value proportion problems, does introducing a double number line after an initial additive error make learners' multiplicative coordination visible in a subsequent independent problem, and what records would show this?"

Each rewrite moves from a global goal to a specific conjecture about mechanism and evidence. The team should be able to answer, before the lesson: "If we see X, the conjecture is supported; if we see Y, it is challenged; if we see neither, the evidence is missing."

## 3. Study the mathematics before writing prompts

The team prepares a content map.

### Definition and proof

For positive denominators, $a/b<c/d$ exactly when $ad<bc$. Here

\[
5\cdot4=20<24=3\cdot8,
\]

so $5/8<3/4$. But cross-multiplication can hide why. Expressing both in eighths gives

\[
\frac34=\frac68>\frac58.
\]

On a shared number line, $5/8=0.625$ and $3/4=0.75$. An area model is valid only if the wholes have equal area. The team records:

- prerequisites: unit fraction, equal whole, equivalence, order;
- valid strategies: common denominator, benchmark, number line, area with common whole, cross-products with justification;
- likely but not fixed responses: compare numerators, compare denominators, use unequal drawings, convert to decimals incorrectly;
- boundary cases: equal fractions, improper fractions, negative fractions, unequal wholes;
- forward connections: ratio, slope, rational order, density.

Anticipating a response means preparing to notice it, not assigning it to a child in advance.

### How a team reads curriculum and research together

Serious study is not skimming. The team assigns sections, reads with a common template, and discusses until the mathematics is precise. A useful template is the *knowledge package*: a structured outline of the mathematical ideas surrounding a single topic, their logical dependencies, and the conceptual obstacles learners encounter.

For fraction comparison, a knowledge package might contain:

1. **Core idea:** Fraction magnitude depends on the multiplicative relation between numerator and denominator, coordinated with a common whole.
2. **Immediate prerequisites:** partitioning a whole into equal parts; naming parts with unit fractions; equivalence as renaming the same quantity.
3. **Procedural knowledge that can outrun understanding:** cross-multiplication, decimal conversion, "bigger denominator means smaller pieces."
4. **Conceptual obstacles:** treating the numerator and denominator as separate whole numbers; using unequal wholes; assuming a single diagram shows all cases.
5. **Forward connections:** ratio as a multiplicative comparison; slope as constant rate; density of rationals.
6. **Representational affordances and risks:** area models require equal wholes; number lines require common unit intervals; strip diagrams can be folded and overlaid; symbolic notation is compact but can hide referents.
7. **Key questions for learners:** What must stay the same when we rename a fraction? What must be shared before two shaded amounts are comparable?

The team studies curriculum materials to see how these ideas are sequenced, where gaps appear, and which prior lessons may have left partial conceptions intact. They also read external research with the same discipline: noting the study's design, sample, measures, and limitations, not extracting a classroom script. A finding about Japanese elementary classrooms with a specified curriculum and stable faculty does not automatically transfer to a different setting.

## 4. Investigate curriculum and external evidence

Review prior learner work, curriculum sequence, tasks, and relevant research. Every note receives a status:

- **mathematical:** $3/4=6/8$;
- **local observation:** four recent work samples used unequal wholes;
- **external finding:** a named study reported a result in its sample and design;
- **team conjecture:** requiring a single reusable whole may foreground comparability.

Lewis, Perry, and Hurd's theoretical account identifies investigation, planning, a research lesson, and reflection, with proposed pathways through knowledge/beliefs, professional community, and teaching-learning resources. Their North American case was offered with limitations, not as proof that every lesson-study implementation works. A later randomized trial by Lewis and Perry studied lesson study supported by fractions resource kits across 39 educator teams and reported improvements on named educator and learner outcomes relative to controls. That intervention included particular resources and support; it does not isolate a context-free "lesson study effect."

## 5. Write a conjecture map

Plan learner activity, not only teacher speech.

| Lesson moment | Mathematical purpose | Anticipated activity | Evidence | Contingent response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| choose between two unequal area drawings | expose common-whole issue | compare shaded pieces or total shaded area | initial choice and explanation | ask whether wholes match |
| rebuild on one strip | coordinate equivalence | partition fourths into eighths | annotations, talk, gesture | ask what stayed equal |
| place both on number line | connect order representations | mark $5/8$,$6/8$ | independent placement | offer tactile/number table route |
| compare $7/12$ and $3/5$ | test transfer | choose common unit or benchmark | exit explanation | collect before summary |

The critical conjecture is explicit:

> If learners first encounter a conflict created by unequal wholes and then rebuild both fractions on one reusable whole, they may treat common-whole coordination as necessary rather than decorative; evidence would be spontaneous reference to equal wholes in the transfer task.

The "if" does not assert a finding.

## 6. Access without replacing the goal

The mathematical goal is fraction comparison with a common whole, not visual inspection or fast cutting. Prepare:

- tactile strips with distinguishable boundaries;
- high-contrast large-print diagrams and spoken structural descriptions;
- pre-divided strips for learners for whom cutting is an irrelevant motor barrier;
- keyboard-accessible numeric partition controls if technology is used;
- a structured table of fraction names, equivalent forms, and positions;
- response through speech, signing, symbols, construction, or selected statements;
- quiet individual prediction before public discussion;
- vocabulary support without reducing reasoning demand.

Do not give one group a diagram that reveals the answer while another must derive it, unless representation access is itself planned and interpreted. Equivalent access carries equivalent mathematical information and agency.

## 7. Observe records, not personalities

Assign observers to evidence zones: selected pairs, representation changes, teacher deviations, or transfer responses. A timestamped template helps:

```text
time | task state | exact words/action | artifact | observer interpretation | follow-up
```

"Confused" is an interpretation. "Placed $5/8$ to the right of $3/4$; said 'five is more than three'; did not refer to denominators or whole" is a record. Both can be retained only when labeled separately.

Observers do not coach unless the plan gives that role. The teacher makes ordinary safety, dignity, and access decisions even when they alter the plan; those deviations become part of the evidence, not faults to hide.

### Observation logistics: evidence zones, sampling, and nonverbal reasoning

A single observer cannot watch everything. The team decides in advance which zones matter to the research question and assigns coverage accordingly.

**Evidence zones** are regions of the classroom or moments in the lesson that are most likely to yield relevant records. For the fraction comparison conjecture, useful zones might be:

- Zone A: two target pairs working with the initial unequal drawings;
- Zone B: the whole-class discussion immediately after the conflict is exposed;
- Zone C: independent work on the transfer task;
- Zone D: any learner who uses gesture or manipulative action rather than speech.

**Sampling decisions** shape what can be claimed. Watching only the pairs the teacher expects to struggle produces a biased record. A deliberate sample might include one pair the teacher expects to reason fluently, one pair with mixed prior performance, and one pair whose reasoning is usually nonverbal. The sample is small; the claim must stay bounded to these learners and this lesson.

**Low-inference recording of nonverbal reasoning** is especially important for learners whose mathematical thinking is expressed through action, gesture, or construction rather than words. A low-inference record of nonverbal work states exactly what was done, in what order, and with what materials:

> 10:23. Learner picks up the $3/4$ strip, folds it in half lengthwise, aligns it with the $5/8$ strip, then unfolds and counts eighths by pointing. No speech. Writes "six" on the $3/4$ label. Pauses, then writes "$5/8 < 6/8$."

This record describes action, material, sequence, and product without interpreting whether the learner "understands" equivalence. The observer may add a separate hypothesis column: "Possible coordination of common whole through overlay; speech absent so verbal warrant unavailable." The hypothesis is labeled as such.

When a learner uses sign language, AAC device, or written symbols, the record should preserve the exact output where possible, with a note about the modality. Do not paraphrase a signed explanation into speech without noting the translation.

## 8. Worked cycle with fictional records — fraction comparison

The following dataset is invented for professional rehearsal. It contains no learner data.

### Planned task

Pairs compare $5/8$ and $3/4$. They first see two separately drawn rectangles of unequal size, then receive one common strip that can be folded into eighths. Finally they compare $7/12$ and $3/5$ without the original materials.

### Low-inference record

| Pair | Initial episode | Common-strip episode | Transfer response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | "five is more"; points to numerators | marks $3/4$ as $6/8$, says "same-sized pieces now" | writes $7/12<3/5$ via $35<36$; no whole mentioned |
| B | chooses larger drawn shaded region | says rectangles were "not fair"; aligns endpoints | uses decimal approximations correctly, states same unit interval |
| C | says $3/4$: it is $1/4$ above $1/2$, while $5/8$ is $1/8$ above | watches partner fold; no audible explanation | writes correct answer with no warrant |
| D | audio unavailable; artifact circles $5/8$ | tactile strip shows fifth and twelfth partitions on same length after prompt | signs "same whole," selects $35<36$ card |

Teacher deviation: because separate rectangles caused extended debate about physical size, the teacher introduced the common strip four minutes earlier than planned.

### Evidence-first discussion

Start with what can be said:

- A and B revised or strengthened reasoning while using the common strip.
- B explicitly identified unfair unequal wholes.
- C's initial benchmark reasoning was already valid; the strip episode did not reveal a change.
- D's initial reasoning is unavailable; the later artifact combines common-whole and cross-product information.
- A's transfer is valid but does not establish spontaneous common-whole language.
- The planned timing was not implemented.

Do not write "all learners understood" or "the strip caused improvement." Four fictional pairs, missing audio, one lesson, several simultaneous prompts, and no counterfactual cannot warrant those statements.

### Rival explanations

- revision may come from partner talk rather than strip structure;
- learners may imitate the teacher's wording;
- cross-products may produce correct comparisons without conceptual coordination;
- the unequal drawings may create a visual-conflict lesson different from the intended fraction lesson;
- the early teacher pivot changes exposure.

### One revision

Change one causal conjecture at a time. Retain a common whole but remove misleading unequal outer rectangles. Begin with two fractions on separate same-length strips, ask learners to overlay them, then transfer to a number line with no strip. Add a prompt: "What must be shared before these shaded amounts are comparable?"

New evidence criterion: an independent transfer explanation names or enacts a common whole before a teacher prompt. This revision follows the record; it is not certified by it.

## 9. Second mathematical map: proportional reasoning

A second content map, for a different topic, shows how the same study discipline applies elsewhere. Consider the missing-value proportion problem: "A recipe uses 3 cups of flour for 4 cups of milk. How much flour for 10 cups of milk?"

### Definition and structure

Two variable quantities are proportional when their ratio is constant: $y/x = k$ for some constant $k$, or equivalently $y = kx$. For the recipe, $k = 3/4$ cups of flour per cup of milk. The desired value satisfies $x/10 = 3/4$, so $x = 7.5$.

**Mathematical result:** for positive quantities, $a/b = c/d$ exactly when $ad = bc$. Cross-multiplication is valid but can obscure the multiplicative structure. A unit-rate argument may be more transparent for some learners.

The team records:

- prerequisites: multiplicative comparison, ratio notation, fraction equivalence, division as sharing;
- valid strategies: unit rate ($3/4$ per cup, times 10), scale factor ($10/4 = 2.5$ batches, times 3), table completion with constant ratio, cross-multiplication with justification;
- likely but not fixed responses: additive error ("10 is 6 more than 4, so add 6 to 3"), same-difference error ("both go up by 7"), guess-and-check without ratio structure;
- boundary cases: zero quantities, non-integer scale factors, inverse proportion, non-constant ratios disguised by nearby numbers;
- forward connections: slope as rate, percent change, similarity, linear functions.

### Representational affordances and risks

A double number line coordinates two units on parallel scales. Its affordance is visual alignment of coordinated values. Its risk is that learners may treat it as two separate additive number lines rather than one multiplicative structure. A ratio table makes the constant relationship explicit but can become a rote pattern if learners fill it without attending to the invariant ratio.

## 10. Second worked cycle with fictional records — proportionality

The following dataset is invented for professional rehearsal. It contains no learner data.

### Focus and conjecture

Does a double-number-line representation help learners distinguish multiplicative from additive reasoning in missing-value problems?

### Conjecture map

| Lesson moment | Mathematical purpose | Anticipated activity | Evidence | Contingent response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| present recipe problem without diagram | elicit initial strategy | additive or multiplicative attempt | written strategy label | offer double number line if additive |
| introduce double number line | make coordinated units visible | mark flour and milk alignments | accuracy of alignment and explanation | ask what stays equal |
| transfer to new problem without diagram | test representation independence | strategy choice and justification | written work and explanation | collect before summary |

### Low-inference record

| Pair | Initial strategy | Number-line episode | Transfer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | additive: 3+6=9 | aligned 3-4 and 6-8, then adjusted to 7.5-10 | multiplicative table, no number line |
| B | multiplicative: 3/4 times 10 | confirmed alignment, explained "same ratio" | cross-multiplication correctly |
| C | additive: 3+6=9 | watched partner align, no independent work | additive again, 9 |
| D | guessed 8 | aligned 3-4 and 9-10 incorrectly | no transfer artifact |

### Evidence-first discussion

Start with records:

- Pair A used an additive strategy initially, engaged with the number line, and produced a correct multiplicative transfer using a table. The table strategy was not anticipated in the conjecture map.
- Pair B was already multiplicative before the number line; the representation confirmed rather than caused the reasoning.
- Pair C repeated the additive error after watching the number line episode. No record shows independent engagement with the alignment.
- Pair D guessed initially, produced an incorrect additive alignment (adding 6 to both quantities gives 9-10 rather than 7.5-10), and left no transfer artifact.
- The teacher did not deviate from the planned sequence.

Do not write "the number line helped most learners" or "Pairs C and D need more time." Four fictional pairs, one lesson, no counterfactual, and missing audio for Pair D's discussion cannot support prevalence claims or individual diagnoses.

### Rival explanations

- Pair A's transfer may reflect prior knowledge of tables rather than learning from the number line;
- Pair C may have watched without processing the alignment because the partner dominated the manipulative;
- Pair D's incorrect 9-10 alignment may indicate additive reasoning (adding 6 to both 3 and 4) or random placement;
- the recipe context may cue multiplicative reasoning for some learners and not others independently of representation;
- the transfer problem's numbers may be more or less friendly than the initial problem.

### One revision

Change one feature: add an individual written prediction before pair work. Each learner solves the initial problem alone and records their strategy before seeing the number line. Prediction: initial reasoning becomes observable, and the team can compare partner influence with independent strategy. No other feature changes. The number line remains; the transfer task remains; the recipe context remains.

New evidence criterion: at least one learner who used an additive initial strategy independently produces a multiplicative response on transfer, with a written record showing the shift.

## 11. Anticipation practice: building the anticipated-response table

Anticipation prepares observers to notice; it does not assign responses to children in advance. A complete anticipated-response table includes wrong-but-informative responses, their mathematical status, and a planned follow-up.

Consider the task: "A car travels 120 miles in 3 hours. At the same speed, how far in 5 hours?"

| Anticipated response | Mathematical status | Why it is informative | Planned follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "120 + 2 = 122 because 5 is 2 more than 3" | additive error; adds the change in hours directly to a distance | reveals failure to coordinate the two units in a constant ratio | ask what distance belongs to each additional hour; offer a table with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 hours |
| "40 miles per hour, so 200 miles" | valid unit-rate strategy | confirms multiplicative coordination; shows whether the learner connects rate to total distance | ask for a double number line showing the same information |
| "120/3 = x/5, so x = 200" | valid cross-multiplication | confirms symbolic proportion skill; may hide whether the learner sees 40 mph as a rate | ask what 120/3 means in the story before solving |
| "I guessed 150" | unsupported; may indicate avoidance, fatigue, or missing prerequisite | signals that the task may be too complex or that the learner needs a simpler entry | offer a 1-hour and 2-hour case first, then build to 5 |
| "It depends on traffic" | valid real-world qualification | shows the learner is attending to context rather than abstracting to mathematics | acknowledge the qualification, then specify "at the same speed" as a mathematical assumption |
| "120 times 5 is 600, divided by 3 is 200" | valid scale-factor strategy | confirms multiplicative reasoning through a different path | ask why multiplying by 5 and dividing by 3 gives the right answer |

The table is a planning tool, not a prediction of who will do what. Observers use it to recognize responses quickly and to avoid interpreting a valid but unfamiliar strategy as error.

## 12. The public research lesson tradition

Lesson study in Japan, known as *jugyou kenkyuu* (lesson study or lesson research), is a long-standing form of collaborative professional inquiry. It is not a single uniform method; schools, districts, and national projects have developed varying structures over decades. Understanding the tradition helps teams adapt its functions without romanticizing its context.

**External finding, bounded.** Takahashi and McDougal describe collaborative lesson research as a structured process involving a research theme, study of curriculum and learner thinking, collaborative planning, a research lesson taught by one team member, observation focused on learner learning, and a post-lesson discussion with an outside commentator. They emphasize that the commentator's role is to connect the lesson evidence to mathematics and research, not to evaluate the teacher. Their account is conceptual and practical, not an effect estimate.

Key features of the tradition that differ from many Western adaptations include:

- **Sustained time:** teams may meet for a year or more around a single research theme, with multiple research lessons across grade levels.
- **Curriculum study:** the team studies the national curriculum guidelines, textbook treatments, and learner thinking research before writing tasks.
- **Public research lesson:** the lesson is taught in front of colleagues from other schools, sometimes at a regional or national level, with a designated outside commentator.
- **Focus on learner learning:** observers watch learners, not the teacher, and collect evidence about whether the lesson's mathematical goal was achieved.
- **Revision and re-teaching:** the team often revises the lesson and teaches it again, sometimes with a different teacher, to test the revision.

These features depend on institutional conditions: stable faculty, scheduled collaboration time, a shared curriculum, and a culture of public professional inquiry rather than individual performance appraisal. A team working without these conditions can still preserve the functions—serious mathematical study, anticipation, low-inference observation, evidence-first discussion, and revision—while adapting the form. The tradition is a resource, not a script.

**Teaching conjecture:** in settings where lesson study is mandated without the supporting conditions, the ceremonial features may persist while the inquiry mechanism erodes. The safeguard is to attend to function over form.

## 13. Conduct the post-lesson discussion

Use this order:

1. teacher records intended and actual pivots;
2. observers present time-linked records and artifacts;
3. team compares anticipated with observed strategies;
4. team examines missing and discrepant evidence;
5. team revisits the mathematical map;
6. only then proposes mechanisms and revisions;
7. outside commentator, if any, connects evidence to mathematics and research without delivering a personnel verdict.

Replace "It worked" with a claim-sized statement. Replace "They were lost" with an observable episode and an uncertainty note. Preserve minority cases; the purpose is learning, not consensus theater.

## 14. Revise and share an auditable product

Each revision record includes:

```text
original feature:
intended mechanism:
observed evidence:
counterevidence/missing evidence:
revised feature:
new prediction:
evidence to collect next:
```

Share the mathematical map, task rationale, anonymized response categories, revision logic, access plan, and limitations. Do not share a "best lesson" stripped of the conditions that made it intelligible. A useful product helps another team test and adapt the conjecture.

## 15. Professional learning beyond one cycle

Track changes in shared resources and reasoning rather than attendance alone:

- richer anticipated solution strategies;
- more precise mathematical explanations;
- observation notes with lower inference;
- revised tasks linked to evidence;
- increased attention to learners previously made less visible;
- durable routines for disagreement and checking;
- new questions generated by failed predictions.

These are plausible indicators, not automatic evidence of learner outcomes. If learner impact is claimed, collect an appropriate design and measure. If teacher learning is claimed, define what changed and how it was evidenced.

Conditions matter: scheduled time, access to mathematical and research expertise, psychological and employment safety, stable participation, usable records, and authority to revise. A mandated imitation lesson under evaluative surveillance may retain the name but lose the inquiry mechanism.

## 16. Failure modes and repairs

| Failure | Why it fails | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| polish one teacher's script | centers performance, hides learner reasoning | make a shared conjecture map |
| collect everything | creates privacy burden and unfocused analysis | collect minimum evidence tied to question |
| observers give impressions first | high-inference consensus outruns records | records and artifacts before interpretation |
| anticipated errors become labels | expectation can narrow attention | write multiple hypotheses and counterevidence |
| revise five features at once | mechanism remains uninterpretable | prioritize one revision and new prediction |
| publish a success story | erases missing and negative evidence | publish limitations and discrepant cases |
| treat external research as a script | context and intervention differ | appraise, adapt, and test locally |

25. A safe professional compact requires that observation follow the research question rather than personnel appraisal. Under evaluative surveillance, the teacher's employment interests conflict with the research goal of testing a conjecture; the teacher may perform a polished script rather than teach responsively. Evidence zones become impossible to define because the observer is watching the teacher's performance, not learner reasoning. The inquiry mechanism—evidence-first discussion, rival explanations, revision—requires honest reporting of deviations and failures, which surveillance discourages. The result is a ceremonial imitation: the forms of lesson study persist without the function.

## Project: Design a complete lesson-study cycle on place-value exchange

### Problem

Choose a specific group of learners and a specific place-value exchange (for example, exchanging ten unit cubes for one ten rod, or ten tens for one hundred flat). Design a complete lesson-study cycle including: a bounded research question; a knowledge package with at least five components; a conjecture map with four lesson moments; an anticipated-response table with at least four entries including one wrong-but-informative response; an observation plan with two evidence zones and one nonverbal recording target; a fictional low-inference record for four learners; an evidence-first discussion summary; rival explanations; one revision with a new prediction; and a limitations statement.

### Solution blueprint

**Bounded research question:** When year-2 learners exchange ten unit cubes for one ten rod, does asking "How many cubes are hiding inside the rod?" before the exchange prompt them to treat the rod as a composed unit of ten rather than a new object, and what does their independent written record show?

**Knowledge package:**
1. Core idea: place value is a system of bundling and positional notation in which the value of a digit depends on its position and the base-ten structure of the number system.
2. Immediate prerequisites: one-to-one correspondence, stable order, cardinality, and counting-on.
3. Procedural knowledge that can outrun understanding: reading and writing two-digit numbers correctly without understanding that the tens digit represents a quantity of ten.
4. Conceptual obstacles: treating the tens digit as a label rather than a quantity; seeing the rod as a different object rather than ten cubes composed; failing to conserve the total quantity across exchange.
5. Forward connections: addition and subtraction with regrouping, multiplicative structure of place value, decimal fractions.
6. Representational affordances and risks: physical cubes allow enaction but may cue play; drawn diagrams require abstraction; symbolic notation is efficient but can hide referents.
7. Key questions: What is the same and what changes when we exchange? Where do the ten cubes go? Does the total number of cubes change?

**Conjecture map:**

| Lesson moment | Mathematical purpose | Anticipated activity | Evidence | Contingent response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| count out 23 cubes and propose exchange | elicit initial conception of ten | count by ones, group by tens, or resist grouping | initial grouping strategy and explanation | ask what would make counting easier |
| ask "How many cubes are hiding inside one rod?" before handing it over | prompt composed-unit view | predict ten, or guess a different number, or say "it's a rod" | prediction and justification | offer to unwrap a rod if available |
| exchange and recount | test conservation and coordination | trade, recount, compare total | written or spoken record of total before and after | ask if any cubes were lost or gained |
| independent written record: "Show 23 with rods and cubes, then with digits" | test symbolic coordination | draw or write representation | written artifact showing rods, cubes, and digits | offer a place-value chart if digits are misaligned |

**Anticipated-response table:**

| Anticipated response | Mathematical status | Why informative | Follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Ten" before receiving rod; explains "because ten cubes make one rod" | valid composed-unit reasoning | shows anticipation of structure, not just recall | ask what would happen with eleven cubes |
| "It's a rod" or "I don't know"; no number given | treats rod as object, not as quantity | reveals object-level rather than quantitative reasoning | unwrap a rod and count together |
| Exchanges willingly but recounts by ones after | procedural compliance without conceptual change | shows that action does not guarantee understanding | ask to count by tens first, then by ones |
| Writes 2 and 3 with no connection to rods and cubes | digit recall without place-value meaning | common procedural outrun; signals need for explicit linking | point to the rods and ask what the 2 stands for |

**Observation plan:**
- Zone A: two target pairs during the physical exchange, recording exact hand movements, verbalizations, and sequence of actions.
- Zone B: independent written work, recording the exact symbols, drawings, and labels produced.
- Sampling: observe one pair with strong counting fluency, one pair with known difficulty in grouping, and one pair who often responds nonverbally.
- Nonverbal target: record whether the learner pushes all ten cubes simultaneously toward the rod, counts them one by one into a pile, or holds back some cubes—each action suggests a different conception of the ten as a unit.

**Fictional low-inference record:**

| Learner | Initial count | Prediction | Exchange action | Written record |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E | counts 23 by ones; groups into a pile of 10 and 13 loose | "Ten, because that's how many fit" | pushes ten cubes together with both hands, takes rod, nods | draws 2 rods and 3 cubes; writes 23 |
| F | counts 23 by ones; leaves scattered | "It's a stick" | watches partner exchange; no hand movement | writes 23; no drawing |
| G | counts 23 by ones; makes two groups of about ten | "Ten?" (rising intonation) | counts ten cubes individually into a pile, then takes rod | draws 20 dots and 3 dots; writes 23 |
| H | counts 23 by twos; stops at 22, adds one | no audible response | holds five cubes in each hand, looks at rod, does not exchange | writes 2 and 3 separated by a space; no drawing |

**Evidence-first discussion:**
- E predicted ten, explained the composed unit, enacted the exchange with simultaneous pushing, and produced a correct linked representation. The evidence supports the conjecture for this learner.
- F treated the rod as an object, did not engage physically, and produced only digits. The evidence challenges the conjecture for this learner; the prompt did not prompt composed-unit reasoning.
- G made approximate groups, predicted ten with uncertainty, counted individually into a pile before exchanging, and drew 20 dots rather than 2 rods. The evidence is mixed: some coordination is present but the symbolic link is partial.
- H counted by twos, gave no prediction, held cubes without exchanging, and wrote separated digits. The evidence does not establish whether the task was too difficult, whether the learner was not attending, or whether a different prerequisite is missing.

**Rival explanations:**
- E's success may reflect prior knowledge rather than the prompt;
- F's object-level response may be a language issue rather than a mathematical one;
- G's individual counting may indicate that simultaneous pushing is not yet comfortable;
- H's non-exchange may indicate fine-motor difficulty, distraction, or missing cardinality;
- the physical presence of rods may cue recall in some learners and novelty in others.

**One revision:**
Change one feature: add an individual prediction in writing before any physical exchange. Each learner writes or draws how many cubes they think are in the rod and why, then exchanges. Prediction: the written prediction makes initial reasoning visible and reduces partner influence. No other feature changes.

New evidence criterion: at least two learners who did not previously demonstrate composed-unit reasoning produce a correct prediction and a linked written representation.

**Limitations statement:**
Four learners were observed in one lesson. One learner gave no audible response during the prediction phase. No counterfactual condition was included. Patterns cannot be generalized beyond this task, materials, and setting. The observation focused on two zones; other learners' reasoning was not recorded in detail. The revision is a conjecture, not a finding.
