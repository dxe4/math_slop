
# Early number as a network of relations

Reciting number words, counting objects, saying how many, comparing two sets, splitting a quantity, and reading a multi-digit numeral are related but not identical accomplishments. This chapter analyzes early numeracy as a network of relations among quantities, actions, spoken words, written signs, and spatial representations. It gives educators mathematical probes, not age norms or clinical diagnoses.

## 1. Counting is a coordinated argument

To count a finite collection, a learner coordinates:

- a stable sequence of count words;
- one count word with each object and no object twice;
- the final word with the cardinality of the whole collection;
- the idea that object type and counting order do not affect the total.

The mathematical invariant is a one-to-one correspondence between the objects and the initial segment $\{1,\dots,n\}$. Saying "seven" after touching seven objects is evidence for that coordination only if alternatives have been tested.

### Counting principles as logical conditions

Gelman and Gallistel identified five logical conditions that competent counting satisfies: stable order, one-to-one correspondence, cardinality, order irrelevance, and abstraction. These are normative conditions, not a universal developmental schedule. A learner may satisfy one principle in one context and not in another.

**Stable order** means the count words are produced in a fixed sequence. A learner who always says "one, two, three, five, four" has a stable but noncanonical sequence: repeated counts of the same number of objects may consistently end on the same wrong conventional label. Compare the learner's sequence with the shared count-word convention and vary collection size; stability and conventional correctness are separate questions.

**One-to-one correspondence** means each object receives exactly one count word. Double-touching or skipping breaks the bijection.

**Cardinality** means the final word names the quantity of the whole set. Recounting after "how many?" is compatible with several explanations: the learner may not yet use the final word as the set's quantity name, may be checking an answer, may not trust the arrangement, or may be responding to the repeated question. A contrasting task is needed.

**Order irrelevance** means the count total is invariant under rearrangement of the counting order.

**Abstraction** means the objects counted may be of any kind: sounds, actions, imaginary items, or heterogeneous collections.

**Zero and empty collections.** The empty set has cardinality zero. A learner who has coordinated the five principles for positive counts may still hesitate when asked "how many are in this empty box?" The count sequence typically begins at one, so zero requires an extension: there are no objects to touch, no count words to say, and the answer is zero. This is a mathematical result: the cardinality of the empty set is $0$ by definition. Teaching conjecture: presenting an empty container after counting nonempty ones may help learners extend cardinality to the zero case, but the transfer is not automatic.

### Diagnostic triad

Give a learner six movable counters.

1. Ask, "How many?"
2. Spread the same counters and ask, "How many now? How do you know?"
3. Ask, "Please give me six."

Suppose the learner correctly counts the first row, recounts the spread row as seven after touching one counter twice, and hands over the entire collection when asked for six. The evidence supports cardinality more strongly than tracking an irregular arrangement. A next task can reduce the tracking demand: invite the learner to move each counted object into a cup. Repeating "six" would not target the likely obstacle.

**Research finding, not universal schedule.** The U.S. Institute of Education Sciences practice guide *Teaching Math to Young Children* recommends developmental progressions for number and operations and grades the evidence behind its recommendations. It includes subitizing, counting, magnitude comparison, numeral links, and simple operations. Its review covered specified ages, years, designs, and outcomes; it does not make one sequence a law of development. The National Academies' consensus report likewise synthesizes a broad research base and emphasizes access to substantial early mathematics. These syntheses motivate careful sequencing, while local tasks still determine an appropriate next move.

## 2. Quantity, pattern, and subitizing

**Perceptual subitizing** is rapid recognition of a small quantity without serial counting. **Conceptual subitizing** recognizes a structured whole through parts, such as seeing eight as two rows of four or five and three.

Flash these patterns separately for about two seconds, then hide them:

```text
A:  ● ● ●

B:  ● ●
    ● ●

C:  ● ● ●
      ● ●
    ● ● ●
```

Ask for the amount and how it was seen. In C, "three, two, three makes eight" reveals composition; "I counted very fast" is a different strategy. Speed alone is not the target. Allow a tactile card, a spoken spatial description, or sufficient viewing time when the flash creates an irrelevant visual or processing barrier.

**Worked design.** To test whether a learner's "five" depends on a familiar die pattern, show five in a row, a ring, and an irregular arrangement. Keep quantity constant while varying arrangement. Then show four in the familiar five-pattern with one position empty. The contrast separates quantity from visual template more effectively than ten repetitions of one card.

### Subitizing, magnitude, and the approximate number system

Beyond the subitizing range, humans and many non-human animals can discriminate approximate magnitudes. The **approximate number system** (ANS) is a hypothesized cognitive mechanism that supports rapid, imprecise comparisons of large quantities without exact counting. **External research finding:** the evidence review by Feigenson, Dehaene, and Spelke (2004) reports approximate-number performance in adults, infants, and other animals, with discrimination depending on the ratio between quantities. **Teaching conjecture:** ANS acuity may be related to later exact arithmetic, but this chapter does not infer a causal pathway or a classroom-training effect from the discrimination evidence. The ANS should not be presented as the sole origin of number understanding.

Magnitude comparison tasks that do not require exact counting—"which pile has more?" with 15 versus 20 objects—can tap ANS-based discrimination. Exact counting provides a different, more precise warrant. A learner who consistently chooses the larger pile without counting may have magnitude sensitivity; a learner who counts both collections may prefer exact methods or may not trust approximate judgments. Neither pattern alone diagnoses a level.

## 3. Comparison needs a common question

Place four red counters far apart and five blue counters close together. Visual length and cardinality conflict.

```text
red:   ●       ●       ●       ●
blue:  ● ● ● ● ●
```

"Which row is longer?" and "Which row has more counters?" are different questions. Pairing each red counter to one blue counter leaves one blue unmatched, proving the blue collection has greater cardinality. Counting both collections is another justification. A learner choosing red may have answered length rather than number; a follow-up that names both attributes is more informative than "try again."

On a number path, comparison becomes order: $5>4$ because 5 lies one step after 4 in the chosen increasing orientation. Tasks that connect correspondence, counting, and spatial order can test whether a learner coordinates these relations without pretending the representations are interchangeable.

### Number paths versus number lines

A **number path** shows numbers as consecutive locations, like steps on a staircase or squares on a board. Each number occupies a discrete cell. A **number line** represents numbers as points on a continuous axis; the distance between 0 and 1 is the same unit interval as the distance between 5 and 6, and fractions or decimals can lie between whole numbers.

For early learners, the number path has lower representational demand: counting steps corresponds directly to counting objects. The number line introduces the additional structure of scale and the possibility of non-integer values. **Teaching conjecture:** introducing a number path before a number line may reduce extraneous load when the goal is counting-based comparison or simple addition. However, remaining on the path too long may delay the unit-interval understanding needed for fractions and measurement.

A common error is treating the number line as a number path: a learner counts the tick marks starting at 0 to find "5" but believes the interval from 0 to 5 contains five tick marks rather than five unit lengths. To discriminate this, ask the learner to mark 1, then 2, then 3 on an unmarked line segment, and observe whether the spacing is equal. Unequal spacing suggests tick-counting; equal spacing suggests unit-interval understanding.

## 4. Operations describe transformations and relations

Addition can mean joining, adding to, or comparing via an unknown difference. Subtraction can mean taking away, finding a missing addend, or measuring difference.

### One equation, three situations

$5+3=8$ can model:

- five birds joined by three birds;
- a length of five units extended by three;
- the part-whole relation among 5, 3, and 8.

$8-5=3$ can model removing five from eight, asking what must be added to five to reach eight, or asking how much greater eight is than five. Learners may succeed in one structure before another.

### Worked diagnostic interview

A learner answers $8-5$ by drawing eight dots and crossing out five, but answers "two" to "Mina has five shells; Jo has eight; how many more does Jo have?" Competing hypotheses include:

1. subtraction is currently tied to removal actions;
2. the phrase "how many more" is unfamiliar;
3. the learner compared the visible arrangement incorrectly.

Next tasks should discriminate:

- show the two sets aligned and ask for unmatched objects;
- ask "what must we add to five to make eight?";
- use the same language with 2 and 5, then change only the language.

Record action, explanation, and representation. One answer cannot decide among the hypotheses.

### Worked error analysis: doubling and halving as relations

A learner says that $6+6=12$ but answers "ten" to $5+6$. The error might be a counting slip, or it might indicate that the double $6+6$ is known as a fact while the near-double $5+6$ is not yet derived from it. A discriminating follow-up:

1. "What is six plus six?"
2. "So what is five plus six?"
3. "What is seven plus six?"

If the learner answers $6+6$ correctly but counts on for $5+6$ and $7+6$, this task does not establish that the double is being used for its neighbors. If the learner says "five plus six is one less than twelve," derived-fact reasoning is supported. If the learner says "ten" to $5+6$ and "twelve" to $7+6$, a systematic one-low pattern may indicate a counting-on error rather than an isolated structural gap.

## 5. Part-whole structure before fact speed

Place seven counters under two cups, showing three before covering both. Ask, "There are seven altogether; three are here. How many are hidden?" Strategies might include counting on $4,5,6,7$, using a known pair $3+4$, showing fingers, or reconstructing all seven.

Vary systematically:

| Total | Shown | Hidden | Structural opportunity |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 7 | 3 | 4 | complementary parts |
| 7 | 6 | 1 | one less than total |
| 8 | 4 | 4 | doubles |
| 10 | 7 | 3 | complements to ten |

Fluency eventually includes accuracy, flexibility, efficiency, and appropriate recall. Timed performance can confound knowledge with anxiety, motor speed, language, or access. Use timing only when speed is genuinely the construct and the consequences are justified.

## 6. Place value is an exchange system

In base ten, ten units of one place exchange for one unit of the next. The numeral $34$ denotes

\[
3\times10+4\times1.
\]

Build 34 with loose objects. Repeatedly exchange any ten ones for one bundle. The material action preserves quantity while changing unit:

```text
34 ones  <-->  3 bundles-of-ten + 4 ones
```

The digit 3 has value 30 because of position, not its shape. The zero in $205$ records zero tens and prevents the 2 and 5 from moving into different places.

### Worked error analysis: $205$ read as $25$

Do not infer "does not understand zero." Ask the learner to:

1. build two hundreds, zero tens, and five ones;
2. point to the digit recording each unit;
3. compare $205$, $250$, and $25$ using the materials;
4. write a numeral for two hundreds and five tens;
5. explain why $205>25$.

Possible evidence ranges from a vocabulary issue to positional confusion to difficulty coordinating written and material forms. A pre-made block may hide the exchange; include opportunities to compose and decompose the large unit.

## 7. Conservation tasks and what they warrant

A **conservation task** presents a quantity, transforms its perceptual appearance without adding or removing material, and asks whether the quantity has changed. The classic Piagetian version pours liquid from a short wide glass into a tall narrow one and asks whether the amount is the same.

**Mathematical result:** if no object is added or removed, the cardinality of a discrete set is invariant under rearrangement: the identity map on the objects is a bijection. A liquid-pouring task uses a separate physical idealization. For an incompressible liquid under conditions where temperature, evaporation, spillage, and trapped gas do not change the amount, transferring the same liquid preserves volume. Conservation of mass alone does not imply volume conservation under compression or temperature change.

**External research finding:** two experiments illustrate how conservation performance can depend on the task context. Rose and Blank (1974) found fewer errors when first graders made one judgment after the rearrangement instead of judgments both before and after it. McGarrigle and Donaldson (1975) found more correct conservation judgments when the transformation appeared accidental rather than an intentional change by the experimenter. These results do not make conservation merely verbal; they show that the standard task can mix reasoning about invariance with interpretation of the experimenter's action and repeated question.

**Teaching conjecture:** asking learners to predict before transforming may reduce perceptual capture and reveal conservation reasoning that a post-hoc question misses. However, prediction alone does not guarantee understanding; some learners may guess correctly without a principled basis.

A discriminating design for discrete conservation:

1. Show six counters in a row. Ask, "How many?"
2. Ask, "If I spread them out, will there be more, fewer, or the same?"
3. Spread them. Ask, "How many now?"
4. Ask, "If I put them in a cup and shake it, will the number change?"

If the learner predicts "same" and confirms by counting, conservation is supported for this material and transformation. If the learner predicts "more" when spread but counts to six, the prediction was based on length, while counting reveals cardinality knowledge. The two pieces of evidence should not be collapsed into a single label.

## 8. Early multiplicative structure

Multiplicative thinking begins before formal multiplication instruction. Three early roots are equal grouping, unitizing, and doubling or halving as relational actions.

### Equal groups and unitizing

**Unitizing** is treating a collection of objects as a single countable unit. A learner who counts "two, four, six, eight" while touching pairs of counters is unitizing by twos. The mathematical structure is repeated addition: $2+2+2+2=8$, or four groups of two. The expression $4\times2$ records this structure compactly.

A learner may be able to make equal groups without yet seeing the multiplicative structure. To discriminate, present eight counters and ask:

1. "Make groups of two. How many groups?"
2. "Make four equal groups. How many in each group?"
3. "If you have four groups of two, how many altogether?"

Success on all three indicates coordination of the unit, the number of units, and the total. Success on the first two but not the third suggests that unitizing is present but the multiplicative relation between unit size, number of units, and total is not yet explicit.

### Doubling and halving as relations

Doubling is the operation $n\mapsto2n$. Halving is the inverse operation $n\mapsto n/2$, defined for even integers in the whole-number domain. These are not merely facts to recall; they are structural relations that preserve or partition multiplicative structure.

**Worked example.** A learner knows $4+4=8$ and $5+5=10$ but hesitates on $6+6$. One hypothesis is that the double facts are memorized in isolation. A discriminating task: "If four plus four is eight, and five plus five is ten, what is six plus six?" If the learner counts on or uses fingers, the doubles may not be connected by a pattern. If the learner says "twelve" and explains "it goes up by two each time," a relational pattern is supported.

Halving is harder because it requires partitioning a quantity into two equal parts, which may not be visually obvious. A learner who can double $6$ to get $12$ may not immediately halve $12$ to get $6$. The operations are inverses mathematically, but cognitive access to one does not guarantee access to the other.

## 9. A six-step lesson design

**Goal.** Learners justify that rearrangement preserves number when no object is added or removed.

1. **Elicit:** show six counters in a row; obtain and record the total.
2. **Predict:** before spreading them, ask whether the number will change.
3. **Transform:** move the same objects slowly; narrate no addition or removal.
4. **Justify:** accept pointing, signing, speech, matching, or recounting; ask what stayed the same.
5. **Contrast:** visibly add one object. Ask what operation differed.
6. **Transfer:** rearrange six sounds, steps, or tactile markers where accessible.

Mathematical result: a bijective rearrangement preserves cardinality. Teaching conjecture: prediction before movement will foreground conservation for this group. Local evidence: learners' predictions, counts, and explanations. Do not collapse those statuses.

## 10. Equity and access audit

- Use the learner's language resources; number-word structure differs across languages. Mandarin number words, for example, name place value explicitly ("十二" is literally "ten-two"). **External research finding:** Miura and colleagues' (1994) cross-national base-ten-block study found group differences in children's number representations associated with language-system characteristics. The design establishes neither a language-only cause nor a deterministic claim about any individual speaker.
- Offer objects large enough to perceive and manipulate, tactile or high-contrast alternatives, and a stable layout.
- Let learners indicate quantity through selection, eye gaze, pointing, signing, speech, construction, or symbols when the response mode is not the target.
- Do not assume household access to bought manipulatives; ordinary safe objects work.
- Avoid public speed rankings and fixed-ability labels.
- Interpret unfamiliarity with a context separately from the mathematics.
- Collect only records needed for a declared instructional purpose.
- When a learner uses an unconventional but mathematically valid strategy, record the reasoning rather than correcting to a standard procedure. The goal is mathematical justification, not uniform performance.
- For learners who process visual information slowly, extend time limits or replace rapid flashes with sustained exploration. The subitizing or magnitude goal can often be preserved through tactile or auditory patterns.
- Ensure that group tasks do not require a learner to disclose a difficulty publicly in order to receive an adaptation. Access adjustments are an ordinary part of instruction, not a special exception.

## Solutions

1. The stable verbal sequence is supported in that context. One-to-one coordination is not yet reliable in the observed arrangement. Cardinality is undecided unless a separate response establishes it.
2. Show four as a square, four in a line, and the square-with-one-missing (three). Ask amount and explanation. Quantity stays four across the first two; familiar outline is disrupted in the third.
3. "Nine birds, six leave; how many remain?" is removal. "Nine is how many more than six?" is comparison. A missing-addend version asks what joins six to make nine.
4. The learner may compare spatial length, or may not conserve cardinality under spacing. Align one-to-one without recounting and ask what is unmatched; then spread the same row while the learner predicts. These contrasts target different explanations.
5. The exchange partitions the same set into one subset of ten and the remainder. The identity map on individual objects is a bijection before and after grouping; only the unit of description changes.
6. Provide a tactile card with grouped marks, allow exploration, then cover it and ask the learner to reconstruct and describe its parts. Rapid perception is removed; composition of the whole from parts remains.
7. (a) mathematical result; (b) local observation; (c) external research finding; (d) teaching conjecture.
8. Present one hundred-bundle, no ten-bundles, and three ones; ask the learner to write, expand, and compare $103$ with $13$ and $130$. Ask which place would be ambiguous if zero were omitted.
9. Hypothesis A: the learner has one-to-one correspondence in the original arrangement but loses track when objects are spread, possibly double-touching or skipping. Hypothesis B: the learner does not yet trust that cardinality is preserved under rearrangement and therefore recounts as a check, but makes a counting error on the second pass. Discriminating follow-up: have the learner move each object into a cup while counting, then ask how many are in the cup without removing them. An answer of six supports the idea that controlled movement reduced a tracking error, but does not by itself settle whether cardinality is trusted. A request to remove and recount supports a checking or task-interpretation hypothesis, while still not proving a conservation gap. Compare the pattern across further rearrangement and wording contrasts.
10. The empty set contains no elements. By definition, its cardinality is $0$. A task: present a box with five objects, count them, remove all five, and ask "how many are in the box now?" If the learner hesitates, confirm that there are none, and introduce "zero" as the word for the cardinality of the empty collection. Then ask the learner to count an empty plate and an empty bag to test transfer across contexts.
11. The contrast suggests that the learner's "five" is tied to a specific visual template (the die pattern) rather than to the abstract quantity. A follow-up task: show five in a ring, five in an irregular cluster, and four in the die pattern with one position empty. Ask for the amount each time and how the learner knows. If the die-pattern-with-missing is called "five," the template dominates over enumeration.
12. "Four groups of three objects each" is equal grouping. "Three groups of four objects each" is the commutative structure. "Three repeated four times as an addend: $3+3+3+3$" is repeated addition. Any two distinct structures are acceptable.
13. Task 1: "What is seven plus seven?" (establish the double). Task 2: "So what is seven plus eight?" If the learner says "fifteen" and explains "one more than fourteen," the near-double relation is available. If the learner counts on from seven or eight, this response does not establish use of the double in that task. Task 3: "What is eight plus eight?" If the learner answers "sixteen" instantly after struggling with $7+8$, a specific transfer difficulty becomes a hypothesis for another contrast, not a settled cognitive label.
14. A number path shows numbers as discrete consecutive cells; a number line shows numbers as points on a continuous axis with equal unit intervals. One diagnostic error: a learner asked to find 5 on a number line counts tick marks starting at 0 and stops at the fifth tick, treating the line as a path. The interval from 0 to 5 contains five unit lengths, not five tick marks. A discriminating task: ask the learner to place 1, 2, and 3 on an unmarked segment; unequal spacing suggests tick-counting.
15. The prediction "more" warrants that the learner's initial response was influenced by perceptual length or area, not by cardinality alone. The subsequent count to six warrants that the learner can coordinate one-to-one correspondence and cardinality in this context. These are separate pieces of evidence. Collapsing them into a single label such as "does not conserve" would lose the information that cardinality counting is available while length cues dominate predictions. The two responses should be recorded separately.
16. Present eight counters. Task A: "Count these by twos." Task B: "Make groups of two. How many groups?" Task C: "Make four groups. How many in each group?" A learner who counts by twos but cannot make groups of two or answer Task C may be reciting a memorized sequence without unitizing. A learner who succeeds on all three has coordinated unit size, number of units, and total.
17. Hypothesis A: the learner understands the spoken name "two hundred five" but omits the zero in writing because they think "no tens" means "write nothing." Hypothesis B: the learner has not yet coordinated the positional value of zero as a placeholder. Discriminating task: ask the learner to build $205$, $250$, and $25$ with bundles and loose objects, then write each numeral. Ask what would happen to the meaning if the zero were removed from $205$. If the learner writes $25$ for $205$ but correctly builds two hundreds and five ones, Hypothesis A is supported.
18. Conservation of cardinality is a mathematical result because a rearrangement without addition or removal establishes a bijection between the original set and the rearranged set; bijections preserve cardinality by definition. The claim that children typically conserve by age seven is an external research finding because it comes from empirical studies with specific samples, measures, and designs. Its scope is bounded by the populations and tasks studied. In the two later studies cited here, Rose and Blank compared one judgment with repeated judgments, while McGarrigle and Donaldson compared apparently accidental with intentional transformations; both found that this task context changed observed performance. Those bounded results do not establish one universal age norm.
19. Hypothesis A: the learner knows doubling facts as forward procedures but has not yet connected halving as the inverse operation. Hypothesis B: the learner can halve even numbers in some contexts but the word "halve" is unfamiliar, while "share equally between two" might elicit the correct response. Transfer task: ask "what is half of twelve?" and "what is half of eight?" and then "ten shared equally between two people—how many each?" Contrasting responses test number and wording sensitivity; no single wrong answer settles the explanation.
20. One-to-one matching creates a partial bijection between the two sets. If every element of the smaller set is matched to a distinct element of the larger set and one element of the larger set remains unmatched, then no bijection between the two sets exists. By definition, a set has greater cardinality than another if there is an injection from the smaller into the larger but no bijection between them. The unmatched object proves the non-existence of a bijection, establishing the cardinality order as a matter of logical necessity, not opinion.
21. Provide two collections of objects with distinct tactile properties—one set of smooth counters and one set of rough counters. Allow the learner to explore both collections by touch. Ask the learner to move one object from each collection simultaneously into separate containers until one collection is exhausted. The remaining objects in the other container establish which quantity is greater. The mathematical goal of comparing cardinality is preserved; the sensory modality is changed from visual to tactile.
22. Hypothesis A: the learner associates "minus" with removal and "how many more" with comparison, and has not yet connected the two structures. Hypothesis B: the learner understands comparison but made a counting or language error on the symbolic subtraction item. Discriminating task: present the same numbers with both wordings in close succession. "Mina has six, Jo has nine. What is nine minus six?" Then, with the same materials: "How many more does Jo have?" If the answers differ, the wording triggers different strategies. If both are answered "three," the earlier "two" was likely a slip.
23. A post-hoc question asks the learner to evaluate a state after transformation, which may be answered by comparing the current perceptual appearance to memory. A prediction question requires the learner to apply a principle before seeing the outcome. If the learner predicts correctly, it suggests that an invariant principle is guiding the judgment rather than retrospective rationalization. However, correct prediction can also occur by chance or by pattern matching, so it should be followed by a justification request. The combination of correct prediction and coherent explanation is stronger evidence than either alone.
24. The learner likely understands that $34$ contains three tens and four ones but writes $304$ because the spoken form "thirty-four" contains the word "four," and the learner maps "thirty" to the hundreds place or inserts the four between the three and the zero. Follow-up: ask the learner to build $34$, $304$, and $43$ with bundles, then match each build to its written numeral. Ask which build has three tens and which has three hundreds. If the learner correctly builds but mismatches written forms, the obstacle is written-positional, not quantitative.
25. The response does not by itself establish whether the cardinality principle is coordinated. Competing hypotheses are that the learner does not yet use the final word as the whole-set quantity, is checking, distrusts the arrangement, or interprets the repeated question as a request to count again. Allow the recount and record its accuracy and explanation; then contrast "how many?" with "tell me the last number you said," ask for that many objects, and use a container or rearrangement task. The pattern across tasks, rather than a ban on recounting, can support one explanation.

## Project: Designing a discriminating interview for early number relations

### Goal

Design, conduct (or ethically simulate), and analyze a 15-minute diagnostic interview that distinguishes among at least three plausible hypotheses about one learner's early number reasoning. The interview must include counting, comparison, and operation tasks, and must preserve the mathematical goal while removing at least one potential access barrier.

### Specification

1. **Hypothesis ledger.** Before the interview, list at least three competing hypotheses about what the learner understands. Each hypothesis must be specific enough to be supported or weakened by a particular response pattern. Example hypotheses: (a) the learner has one-to-one correspondence in linear but not scattered arrangements; (b) the learner conserves cardinality under spreading but not under hiding; (c) the learner knows doubles but does not derive near-doubles from them.

2. **Task design.** For each hypothesis, design one task whose responses discriminate it from the others. Include: materials, exact wording, anticipated responses, and what each response would warrant. At least one task must have a non-visual access route (tactile, auditory, or kinesthetic).

3. **Evidence record.** Create a structured observation sheet with columns for: task number, learner action, learner explanation, your inference, and confidence level (high/medium/low). Record verbatim where possible.

4. **Analysis.** After the interview, state which hypotheses are supported, which are weakened, and which remain undecided. Identify any confounds: Was the wording ambiguous? Did motor demands interfere? Was the context unfamiliar?

5. **Next-step conjecture.** Propose one teaching move grounded in the evidence. Label it explicitly as a teaching conjecture and state what evidence would contradict it.

6. **Ethics check.** Confirm that the interview was conducted with appropriate consent or assent, or state that you used ethically constructed fictional data. If fictional, include a brief justification for why the response pattern is educationally plausible.

### Solution blueprint

**Hypothesis ledger example.**

| Hypothesis | Support pattern | Weaken pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H1: one-to-one breaks in scattered layouts | recounts scattered set incorrectly; linear set correct | scattered set correct without hesitation |
| H2: cardinality not trusted across hiding | recounts after objects are hidden | gives correct cardinal without recount after hiding |
| H3: subtraction tied to removal only | solves take-away but not comparison or missing-addend | solves all three structures correctly |

**Task 1: Linear versus scattered counting.** Materials: twelve identical counters, one opaque cup. Procedure: place counters in a line; ask "How many?" Record. Spread into a circle; ask "How many now?" Record. Move into cup one by one while learner counts; ask "How many are in the cup?" Record. If the learner counts correctly in line but miscounts scattered, H1 is supported. If the learner recounts in the cup despite having just counted them in, H2 is supported.

**Task 2: Subtraction structures.** Materials: nine small objects, two plates. Procedure: "Here are nine objects. Take away four. How many are left?" Record. Reset. "Mina has five objects. Jo has nine. How many more does Jo have?" Record. Reset. "What must we add to five to make nine?" Record. If the learner solves the first but not the second or third, H3 is supported. If all three are solved correctly, H3 is weakened and the learner likely coordinates multiple subtraction structures.

**Task 3: Tactile magnitude comparison (non-visual access).** Materials: two bags, ten smooth counters, ten rough counters. Procedure: place counters in bags. Allow the learner to explore by touch. Ask "Which bag has more?" The learner may remove pairs simultaneously, may count by touch, or may estimate. Record strategy. This task preserves the comparison goal while removing visual length cues.

**Evidence record excerpt.**

| Task | Action | Explanation | Inference | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1, scattered | counts 13, double-touches one | "I might have touched one twice" | one-to-one breaks when layout changes | high |
| 1, cup | says 12 without recounting | "I counted them in" | cardinality trusted across hiding | medium |
| 2, take-away | draws 9, crosses out 4, says 5 | "I took them away" | removal structure available | high |
| 2, comparison | says 4 after pause | "nine is four more" | comparison structure available | medium |

**Analysis example.** H1 is supported: the learner double-touched in the scattered arrangement and self-corrected, suggesting that tracking demand exceeded current skill. H2 is weakened: the learner trusted cardinality in the cup. H3 is weakened: both removal and comparison were solved, though comparison was slower. A confound: the learner's self-correction on Task 1 may indicate meta-cognitive awareness that was not part of the hypothesis ledger; this is valuable local observation but not a planned inference.

**Next-step conjecture.** Teaching conjecture: providing a physical boundary (a cup or frame) for counted objects in scattered arrangements may reduce tracking demand and allow one-to-one correspondence to become visible. This will be tested by comparing counting accuracy with and without a boundary in the next session. Evidence that would contradict it: the learner continues to double-touch even with a boundary, suggesting the obstacle is not spatial memory but sequence control.

**Ethics check.** If conducting with a real learner: obtain institutional review where required, informed consent from guardians, and developmentally appropriate assent from the learner. Use anonymized records. If using fictional data: construct the response pattern from documented common behaviors in the research literature (e.g., Gelman & Gallistel, 1978; National Research Council, 2009) and state that the record is illustrative.
