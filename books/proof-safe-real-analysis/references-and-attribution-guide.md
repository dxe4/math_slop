<a id="section-18-how-to-use-references-responsibly"></a>
## How to use references responsibly {#section-20-how-to-use-references-responsibly}

A reference can serve at least four different jobs:

- **attribution:** identifying a source connected to a result, formulation, example, or historical claim;
- **verification:** comparing hypotheses, proof details, or conventions against an independently edited treatment;
- **extension:** continuing into material beyond this book’s scope;
- **pedagogy:** finding an alternate explanation or exercise sequence.

These jobs should not be conflated. A modern textbook that gives a clean proof may not be the historical source of the theorem. A historical source may use concepts and notation unlike the modern reconstruction. A web page may be excellent for intuition but inappropriate as the only verification source for a delicate claim. The production citation-audit receipt records the checked job and locator for each audited claim.

<a id="section-29-first-course-and-proof-transition-reading"></a>
## First-course and proof-transition reading {#section-31-first-course-and-proof-transition-reading}

The following books offer complementary routes through elementary real analysis. They are intentionally unversioned reading routes rather than edition metadata.

- Stephen Abbott, *Understanding Analysis* — a learner-conscious first course with strong attention to examples and proof development.
- Robert G. Bartle and Donald R. Sherbert, *Introduction to Real Analysis* — a structured route through the real line, sequences, continuity, differentiation, and integration.
- Charles C. Pugh, *Real Mathematical Analysis* — geometric motivation and an expansive problem-solving style.
- Terence Tao, *Analysis I* and *Analysis II* — a construction-minded sequence with explicit logical foundations and later metric and measure theory.
- Michael Spivak, *Calculus* — a proof-oriented calculus bridge whose problems prepare readers for analysis.

Use these texts to compare pedagogical sequencing. Their notation, assumed prerequisites, and chosen integral may differ from this volume’s; translate conventions before comparing individual claims.

<a id="section-41-dense-proof-references"></a>
## Dense proof references {#section-43-dense-proof-references}

- Walter Rudin, *Principles of Mathematical Analysis* — a compact reference spanning metric spaces, continuity, differentiation, integration, sequences of functions, and several-variable topics.
- H. L. Royden and P. M. Fitzpatrick, *Real Analysis* — a broad treatment connecting Lebesgue theory, metric spaces, and functional analysis.
- Gerald B. Folland, *Real Analysis: Modern Techniques and Their Applications* — a graduate-level route through measure, integration, topology, and functional analysis.
- Elias M. Stein and Rami Shakarchi, *Real Analysis: Measure Theory, Integration, and Hilbert Spaces* — a measure-and-function-space treatment within a larger analysis sequence.

Dense references are useful for verification only after their hypotheses have been translated into the local setup. A shorter proof is not automatically more suitable for a first encounter.

<a id="section-50-metric-spaces-and-topology"></a>
## Metric spaces and topology {#section-52-metric-spaces-and-topology}

- James R. Munkres, *Topology* — point-set topology beyond the metric-space setting and a place to see which sequential arguments do not generalize automatically.
- John L. Kelley, *General Topology* — a more advanced structural reference for filters, nets, products, and general topological methods.

This book intentionally restricts most topology to metric spaces. Readers moving to arbitrary topological spaces should audit every use of sequences, countability, and distance.

<a id="section-57-measure-and-integration"></a>
## Measure and integration {#section-59-measure-and-integration}

- Paul R. Halmos, *Measure Theory* — a classical systematic development of abstract measure.
- Donald L. Cohn, *Measure Theory* — a broad reference for measures, measurable functions, product constructions, and related topics.
- Sheldon Axler, *Measure, Integration & Real Analysis* — an accessible modern route through measure and integration with further functional-analytic material.
- Frank Jones, *Lebesgue Integration on Euclidean Space* — a route emphasizing Euclidean measure and concrete integration.

The present volume constructs the core convergence machinery but does not fully develop product measures, signed measures, differentiation of measures, or Radon measures. Those subjects require a continuation reference.

<a id="section-66-functional-analysis-and-approximation"></a>
## Functional analysis and approximation {#section-68-functional-analysis-and-approximation}

- Erwin Kreyszig, *Introductory Functional Analysis with Applications* — normed and inner-product spaces, operators, and applications.
- John B. Conway, *A Course in Functional Analysis* — a graduate route through Banach and Hilbert spaces and operator theory.
- Walter Rudin, *Functional Analysis* — a dense continuation into topological vector spaces, distributions, and operator theory.
- Allan Pinkus, “Weierstrass and Approximation Theory,” *Journal of Approximation Theory* **107**(1) (2000), 1–66, [doi:10.1006/jath.2000.3508](https://doi.org/10.1006/jath.2000.3508) — a survey article on approximation ideas and their historical development.

Chapter 19 proves only the foundational finite \(L^p\) inequalities and completeness mechanism needed for the later chapters. It is not a substitute for a functional-analysis course.

<a id="section-75-fourier-analysis"></a>
## Fourier analysis {#section-77-fourier-analysis}

- Elias M. Stein and Rami Shakarchi, *Fourier Analysis: An Introduction* — Fourier series and transforms within a broad analysis programme.
- Yitzhak Katznelson, *An Introduction to Harmonic Analysis* — a focused reference for Fourier series and harmonic analysis.
- Antoni Zygmund, *Trigonometric Series* — an advanced classical reference whose scope lies well beyond the introductory approximation chapter.

Fourier normalization conventions vary. Before comparing coefficients or Parseval identities, record the interval, exponential sign, and normalization factors as one package.

<a id="section-83-counterexamples-and-problem-culture"></a>
## Counterexamples and problem culture {#section-85-counterexamples-and-problem-culture}

- Bernard R. Gelbaum and John M. H. Olmsted, *Counterexamples in Analysis* — a collection illustrating how nearby hypotheses separate.
- J. M. Stoyanov, *Counterexamples in Probability* — useful after measure theory for seeing analogous failure patterns in probability.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It* — general problem-solving questions rather than an analysis reference.

Collections of counterexamples should be used generatively. After reading one construction, identify its mechanism—deleted limit point, moving mass, concentrated spike, accelerated oscillation, disconnected domain—and rebuild a variant with one parameter changed.

<a id="section-102-attribution-checklist-for-release-review"></a>
## Attribution checklist for release review {#section-104-attribution-checklist-for-release-review}

For every named theorem, historical paragraph, borrowed data set, external figure, nontrivial numerical constant, or open-problem status claim, the release audit asks:

1. Is a citation needed for attribution, verification, or both?
2. Does the cited source actually contain the claim in the form used?
3. Have differences in hypotheses and notation been recorded?
4. Is the bibliographic edition and locator verified from the source itself?
5. Is any quoted wording within license and quotation limits?
6. Does a historical statement distinguish early examples, independent discovery, first proof, later generalization, and modern naming?
7. Has current-status language been checked near the release date?
8. Is an accessible alternative available for externally sourced visual material?
