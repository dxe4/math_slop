
# Lattices and Post-Quantum Cryptography

A lattice is a discrete set generated by integer combinations of independent vectors. It connects exact linear algebra, approximation and algorithmic search. Learning with errors instead presents noisy linear equations modulo an integer. The relation between these objects motivates cryptographic assumptions, but their geometric appearance does not by itself prove hardness. This chapter states a decisional LWE assumption and gives a complete secrecy reduction for one bit-encryption construction, including the statistical lemma it needs.

The qualifier post-quantum changes the allowed adversary's computation. A claim against classical polynomial-time algorithms is not automatically a claim against quantum algorithms. We identify where a stronger computational assumption enters and retain the classical input/output interface of the displayed scheme. No particular parameter set or standardised implementation is certified by the symbolic construction.

## Lattices and bases

For linearly independent columns of a real matrix $B$, the lattice they generate is $L(B)=\{Bz:z\in\mathbb Z^d\}$. Independence matters: it gives an injective linear map from the coefficient space and a positive lower norm bound there. Consequently a bounded region contains only finitely many lattice points, since the corresponding integer coefficients are bounded. This is the discrete structure absent from arbitrary dense integer combinations of dependent real directions.

Changing the basis by an integer matrix $U$ with determinant plus or minus one leaves the lattice unchanged. Such a matrix and its inverse have integer entries, so $BUz$ and $Bw$ generate the same sets in both directions. In full dimension the absolute determinant measures the volume of a fundamental parallelepiped; it is independent of this unimodular basis change.

The shortest-vector problem asks for a shortest nonzero lattice vector under a specified norm. The closest-vector problem asks for a lattice vector closest to a supplied target. Approximation versions need an explicit multiplicative factor or distance promise. A finite search can solve bounded instances, but the number of possibilities may grow rapidly with dimension and representation. A hardness assumption must name the actual family, distributions and approximation factor; the words shortest or closest are not themselves such an assumption.

## Worked example 1: one lattice, two bases and a nearest point

Take basis columns $(2,0)$ and $(0,3)$. Its points have even first coordinate and second coordinate divisible by three. Replacing the second basis vector by their sum gives columns $(2,0)$ and $(2,3)$, using the unimodular change matrix with columns $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Both bases generate the same lattice and have determinant six.

For target $(1.1,2.8)$, the closest allowed first coordinate is two and closest second coordinate is three. Minimising the sum of the two squared coordinate errors therefore gives closest lattice point $(2,3)$ with squared distance $0.9^2+0.2^2=0.85$. No other coordinate choice improves either independent term. This is a complete two-dimensional instance, not an indication that high-dimensional approximation problems have the same difficulty.

## A precise noisy-linear assumption

Fix prime modulus $q$, secret dimension $n$, sample count $m$ and an integer error distribution $\chi$. Generate a uniform matrix $A\in\mathbb F_q^{n\times m}$, a uniform secret $s\in\mathbb F_q^n$ and independent errors $e_i$ from $\chi$. Let
$$b=A^T s+e\pmod q.$$
The decisional LWE experiment distinguishes the public pair $(A,b)$ from a uniform independent pair of the same dimensions. Parameters and error distribution depend on the security parameter, with polynomial encoded sizes and efficient sampling.

This assumption is stated directly. We do not claim here a worst-case lattice reduction for every possible bounded error distribution, modulus or sample count. Too little error or an unsuitable parameter choice can make a family easy. The public matrix's column count also affects a statistical step in the encryption theorem; it cannot be omitted from the statement.

There is a geometric connection visible without a hardness theorem. The integer lattice generated by the columns of $A^T$ together with $q\mathbb Z^m$ consists of all vectors $A^T z+qw$ for integer vectors $z,w$. The representative $b$ differs from a vector of this lattice by the small error vector. That description explains noisy decoding intuition. It does not prove that distinguishing noisy samples is equivalent to arbitrary closest-vector search under all norms and promises.

## The encryption construction and correctness

The secret key is $s$ and the public key is $(A,b)$. To encrypt a bit $\mu$, sample $r$ uniformly from $\{0,1\}^m$, put $h=\lfloor q/2\rfloor$, and send
$$u=Ar,\qquad v=b^T r+\mu h\pmod q.$$
Compute $w=v-s^Tu$ on decryption and choose the nearer of zero and $h$ using circular residue distance. Ties need a defined failure rule, but the strict correctness condition below excludes them on honest ciphertexts.

The noise identity is $w=e^T r+\mu h\pmod q$. If every sampled integer error has magnitude at most $B$, then $|e^Tr|\le mB$. The circular separation of the two centres is $h$ for odd $q$. Thus the sufficient strict condition $mB<h/2$ places the error closer to its correct centre than to the other. This proves exact correctness for the bounded error support. For unbounded errors one instead needs a quantified probability that this condition holds; no tail bound is silently supplied here.

## Worked example 2: a noisy bit decrypted by hand

Take $q=97,n=1,m=3$, secret five and matrix row $(3,8,11)$. With errors $(1,-1,0)$ the public vector is $(16,39,55)$. Choose $r=(1,0,1)$, giving $u=14$ and the unshifted last value seventy-one. For bit one, add $h=48$ to obtain ciphertext scalar $v=22$ modulo ninety-seven.

Decryption gives $w=22-5\cdot14\equiv49$. This is one away from the centre forty-eight and far from zero in circular distance, so it decodes one. For bit zero, $v=71$ and the recovered value is one, decoding zero. Here $B=1$ gives $mB=3<24$, so the stated correctness bound covers every binary $r$ with those error bounds.

These parameters do not meet the later entropy condition for useful secrecy: three random selection bits cannot make a nearly uniform vector over ninety-seven squared possibilities by the provided lemma. The example establishes the noise arithmetic and exposes that missing security condition rather than claiming that a successful decryption validates all parameters.

## The finite linear-hash lemma

Let $M$ be a uniform $d\times m$ matrix over $\mathbb F_q$, $R$ uniform on binary $m$-vectors, and $U$ uniform in $\mathbb F_q^d$. Write $S=2^m$ and $Q=q^d$.

**Lemma 13.1.** The total variation distance between $(M,MR)$ and $(M,U)$ is at most $\tfrac12\sqrt{Q/S}$.

*Proof.* For distinct binary vectors $r,r'$, their difference has a nonzero coordinate modulo the prime. In one uniform matrix row, fixing all other entries leaves the dot product with that difference uniform as the selected coordinate varies. Independent rows therefore give $\Pr[Mr=Mr']=1/Q$.

For fixed $M$, let $p_M(z)$ be the distribution of $MR$. The collision probability of two independent selections is $\sum_z p_M(z)^2$. Averaging over matrices and splitting equal from unequal binary selections gives
$$\mathbb E_M\sum_z p_M(z)^2=\frac1S+\left(1-\frac1S\right)\frac1Q.$$
Subtracting $1/Q$ yields
$$\mathbb E_M\sum_z(p_M(z)-1/Q)^2=\frac{1-1/Q}{S}\le\frac1S.$$
For each matrix, Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the sum of the absolute deviations by the square root of $Q$ times their squared sum. Applying Cauchy–Schwarz once more to its expectation gives expected absolute deviation at most $\sqrt{Q/S}$. Half this quantity is the joint total variation distance because the matrix marginal is the same in both distributions. ∎

The matrix remains visible in both distributions. A bound only on an unconditioned output would be insufficient because the adversary receives the public matrix. The joint bound is exactly what lets the secrecy proof choose messages as a function of that public key. Binary-selection entropy and the full output dimension both appear in the lemma's explicit loss.

## The full LWE secrecy reduction

**Theorem 13.2 (One-bit IND-CPA).** For the construction above, the chosen-plaintext guessing advantage is at most the decisional LWE gap of an efficient distinguisher plus
$$\delta=\frac12\sqrt{q^{n+1}/2^m}.$$
Correctness additionally needs the error condition already stated; secrecy does not replace it.

*Proof.* Given a challenge pair $(A,b)$ that is either LWE or uniform, use it as the public key. Run the encryption adversary, receive its two bit messages, choose an independent hidden bit and encrypt the selected message using a fresh local binary $r$. The reduction can do so without knowing the secret or errors. Output one exactly when the adversary guesses the hidden bit correctly. Public encryption queries can be answered by that same public algorithm with independent local randomness.

With an LWE pair this is the real experiment. With a uniform pair, the matrix formed by stacking $A$ and the row $b^T$ is uniform with $n+1$ rows. Before the message shift, its product with $r$ is precisely the ciphertext vector. Lemma 13.1 makes that vector jointly with the public matrix at distance at most $\delta$ from an independent uniform vector.

Add the shift corresponding to the selected message, keeping the public key, adversary coins and hidden bit. This is a postprocessing of the two joint distributions, so it cannot increase their total variation distance: for an event, its preimage is an event whose original probability gap is already bounded. In the ideal uniform-vector experiment, either message shift preserves uniformity, so the hidden bit is independent of the resulting view and success is one half. Thus success with a uniform public pair differs from one half by at most $\delta$.

The difference between real success and success with the uniform pair is exactly the LWE distinguisher's gap. Triangle inequality gives the bound. The wrapper uses the supplied public samples, one adversary execution and polynomial-size matrix/vector operations for each public encryption. ∎

The argument applies even though the adversary chooses its bit messages after receiving the public matrix: the statistical lemma was joint in that matrix. It does not replace the noisy public key by an independent ciphertext mask without paying both the computational LWE change and the statistical linear-hash loss. Omitting either step would erase a real hypothesis.

## Worked example 3: reading the entropy bound

If $m\ge(n+1)\log_2q+2k$, then $2^m\ge q^{n+1}2^{2k}$ and the displayed statistical loss is at most $2^{-k-1}$. Integer choices use an appropriate ceiling. This is a sufficient relation among dimensions and randomness length, not a security parameter recommendation in isolation.

Increasing the sample count also increases the worst-case accumulated noise bound $mB$. The correctness condition and entropy condition must therefore be met simultaneously. Choosing many samples without adjusting the modulus or errors can improve one bound while breaking the other. A full parameter family also needs the assumed LWE hardness and polynomial algorithms. The symbolic theorem exposes those constraints rather than deriving them from a single numerical ratio.

## What the post-quantum assumption says

If the LWE comparison is assumed hard for quantum polynomial-time distinguishers using the stated classical samples and message interfaces, the reduction runs such an adversary as its subroutine. It performs classical public operations and does not rewind it. The statistical lemma compares classical input distributions; their total variation bound also limits any subsequent measurement procedure because probabilities of outcomes are linear bounded functions of those classical inputs.

Thus the same conditional proof extends to that adversary model under its stronger LWE premise. Classical hardness alone does not establish the premise. Nor does this argument cover quantum superposition access to an encryption or hash oracle that the experiment never supplied. Naming both the computation model and interface is the substance of the qualifier, rather than attaching it to every construction involving integer vectors.

The connection to error-correcting codes is conceptual and useful: recover a structured signal despite small errors, or reason about when that recovery should be difficult without a trapdoor. The dedicated Error-Correcting Codes book develops coding distance, decoding and its own guarantees; this chapter does not duplicate that theory or equate efficient communication decoding with the stated LWE problem.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-1"></a>
Why does a unimodular integer basis change preserve a lattice in both directions? Contrast determinant two.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Both the change matrix and its inverse have integer entries when its determinant is plus or minus one.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

An integer matrix with determinant two need not have an integer inverse.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Every integer combination of columns of $BU$ is an integer combination of columns of $B$. The inverse integer matrix supplies the reverse inclusion. A determinant-two change can give only a proper sublattice: multiplying the first basis vector of the integer plane by two restricts its first coordinate to even values. Equal real spans do not guarantee equal integer-generated sets. The integrality of the inverse is the decisive property.

</details>

### Exercise 2
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-2"></a>
Prove the nearest-point claim for target $(1.1,2.8)$ in the lattice generated by $(2,0),(0,3)$.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Squared distance separates into two independent coordinate terms.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Choose the nearest even integer and nearest multiple of three.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Two is nearer to 1.1 than zero or any other even integer, and three is nearer to 2.8 than any other multiple of three. Since squared Euclidean distance is the sum of those independently minimised terms, $(2,3)$ is the unique nearest point. Its squared distance is 0.85. The argument proves the whole small search complete through coordinate structure rather than merely finding one plausible candidate.

</details>

### Exercise 3
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-3"></a>
Recompute $b$, $u$ and $v$ for bit one in Worked Example 2 and identify the recovered error.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

The error vector is $(1,-1,0)$ and the selection is $(1,0,1)$.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The centre shift is forty-eight modulo ninety-seven.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The public values are $3\cdot5+1=16$, $8\cdot5-1=39$ and $11\cdot5=55$. The selected matrix sum is fourteen; the selected public-vector sum is seventy-one. Adding forty-eight gives $119\equiv22$. Subtracting $5\cdot14$ yields $49$ modulo ninety-seven, exactly centre forty-eight plus the selected error one. The calculation proves correctness of that instance while its small selection entropy leaves the secrecy bound unhelpful.

</details>

### Exercise 4
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-4"></a>
Derive the sufficient correctness condition from bounded errors and explain why a strict inequality avoids ties.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

At most $m$ selected errors contribute to the sum.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Two radius-half-separation balls do not overlap when the radius is strict.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Binary selection gives $|e^Tr|\le\sum_i|e_i|r_i\le mB$. The two residue centres have circular separation $h$. If $mB<h/2$, the recovered value lies strictly closer to its intended centre; reaching the other centre would require at least the remaining separation. Equality at the midpoint could be a tie, so the proof deliberately requires a strict bound. Unbounded errors need a separate failure-probability estimate.

</details>

### Exercise 5
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-5"></a>
Prove that distinct binary selections collide under a uniform $d$-row matrix with probability $q^{-d}$.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Their difference has a nonzero field coordinate.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Solve each row's dot-product condition for its corresponding matrix entry.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Fix all but one entry of a row at a coordinate where the difference is nonzero. Varying that uniform field entry makes the dot product uniform, so equality to zero has probability $1/q$. Rows are independent, giving probability $q^{-d}$ that all dot products vanish. This concerns distinct selections; equal selections collide with probability one and supply the separate diagonal term in the collision-moment calculation.

</details>

### Exercise 6
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-6"></a>
Explain the two terms in the expected collision probability of Lemma 13.1.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Two independent binary selections agree with probability $1/S$.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Conditional on being distinct, the matrix collision probability is $1/Q$.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Equal selections contribute $1/S$ because their outputs agree for every matrix. Distinct selections occur with probability $1-1/S$ and collide with probability $1/Q$, giving the other term. Their sum is the averaged squared probability mass. Subtracting the uniform distribution's squared mass leaves at most $1/S$, which Cauchy–Schwarz turns into the stated total variation bound. Ignoring the diagonal would incorrectly predict exact uniformity.

</details>

### Exercise 7
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-7"></a>
Why does the statistical lemma keep the matrix in its joint distribution, and why does that matter for adaptive message choice?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

The adversary sees the public key before selecting candidates.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

A marginal output distribution can hide correlations with that visible matrix.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The adversary's message selection can depend on the visible matrix. A bound only after averaging and discarding it would not control those correlations. The joint bound permits any subsequent message-selection and shifting computation as a postprocessing, preserving the distance bound. This is exactly the interface required by the encryption proof. Uniform-looking output marginals alone would not justify replacing the adversary's whole view.

</details>

### Exercise 8
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-8"></a>
Show why the three selection bits in the ninety-seven-modulus toy example cannot give a useful bound from Lemma 13.1.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Its output vector has two field coordinates.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compare eight possible selections with $97^2$ possible output vectors.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Here $S=8$ and $Q=97^2=9409$, so the displayed half-square-root bound exceeds one and becomes only the trivial distance bound. A distribution supported on at most eight outputs for each matrix cannot be close to uniform on all those output possibilities. Correct decryption does not establish the entropy condition or the LWE assumption. The example deliberately isolates arithmetic correctness from a useful secrecy parameter family.

</details>

### Exercise 9
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-9"></a>
Does a classical LWE assumption imply the chapter's post-quantum conclusion? State the stronger premise and retained interface.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

The adversary's allowed computation is part of the assumption.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The reduction uses classical samples and classical message interactions.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

No. The conclusion against quantum polynomial-time distinguishers requires assuming LWE hardness against that class for the given sample distribution. The wrapper itself can run such an adversary without rewinding, while its public-key and message interfaces remain classical. A superposition oracle would be a different experiment. The algebra and statistical distance lemma do not turn classical hardness into quantum hardness by changing the construction's label.

</details>

### Exercise 10
<a id="lattices-post-quantum-cryptography-ex-10"></a>
Why must the sample-count entropy bound and the noise correctness bound be considered together?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

More selection bits improve one displayed expression.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

More selected bounded errors can enlarge another expression.

</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Increasing $m$ makes $2^m$ larger and reduces the statistical loss, but also increases the worst-case error bound $mB$. If the latter reaches half the centre separation, honest decryption can fail or become ambiguous under the stated sufficient condition. A valid parameter family must satisfy both constraints and the hardness premise with efficient sampling. Improving one term alone is not a proof that the resulting full encryption scheme meets all its claims.

</details>

## Summary and sources

Lattice geometry supplies exact discrete objects and search questions. The displayed LWE assumption is a separate noisy-linear distributional claim. A full collision-moment lemma and a direct LWE hybrid prove one-bit secrecy with an explicit statistical loss, while the error bound proves correctness. Post-quantum interpretation strengthens the adversary premise and retains the stated interfaces. This closes the book's central habit: separate algebra, probability, algorithms and conditional reductions in every security claim.

Katz and Lindell, *Introduction to Modern Cryptography*, is the general comparison text for assumption-based public-key reasoning. The finite-field construction, linear-hash proof, exact examples and exercise sequence are developed here; no worst-case lattice reduction, current standard parameter assessment or unproved universal hardness theorem is claimed. The separate Information Theory and Error-Correcting Codes books own their full probability/coding developments.

Continue with [Error-Correcting Codes](#/books/error-correcting-codes) for Hamming distance, linear-code parity checks, syndromes and correction-radius proofs. Its authored **Linear Codes** chapter develops the algebra behind those interfaces. Here the one-bit decryption argument uses a declared bound on accumulated error and separation of the two message centres; in the coding book the corresponding recovery question is developed for finite-alphabet codewords and specified error families. Keep the decoding/correctness contract separate from the adversarial secrecy reduction in both readings.
