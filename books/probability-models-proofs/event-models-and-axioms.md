
# Event Models, Sigma-Algebras, and Probability Axioms

Probability begins before any number is calculated. We first decide what a complete outcome records, which collections of outcomes count as events, and how mass is assigned to those events. Only after those choices are explicit can the axioms turn model assumptions into theorems.

This chapter answers three foundational questions.

1. What information must a probability model specify?
2. Why are events organized as a sigma-algebra rather than as an arbitrary list?
3. Which familiar rules follow from countable additivity, and which require extra modeling assumptions such as equal likelihood?

The local prerequisites are sets, functions, finite sums, and limits of nonnegative increasing sequences. No measure theory is assumed. The next chapter will condition these models on observed information.

## 1. Outcomes are records, not merely labels

A **sample space** $\Omega$ is the set of complete outcomes admitted by a model. “Complete” is relative to the question. For one toss of a coin, $\Omega =\{H,T\}$ may be sufficient. For the landing angle of the same coin, the temperature, launch velocity, surface, and observation protocol might also matter. A useful outcome records enough to decide every event and random quantity the model will discuss.

An **event** is a set of outcomes. If $A\subseteq \Omega$, then event $A$ occurs exactly when the realised outcome $\omega$ belongs to $A$. Logical operations become set operations:

- “not $A$” is $A^{c}=\Omega \setminus A$;
- “$A$ or $B$” is $A\cup B$;
- “$A$ and $B$” is $A\cap B$;
- “$A$ but not $B$” is $A\setminus B$.

This translation is not cosmetic. It exposes whether events overlap, whether one implies another, and whether a verbal partition really is exhaustive.

### Worked model contract: two draws without replacement

A bag contains red, blue, and green tokens with distinct physical identities $r,b,g$. Draw two tokens in order without replacement. A complete outcome records the ordered pair:

$\Omega =\{(r,b),(r,g),(b,r),(b,g),(g,r),(g,b)\}$.

Let $R_{1}$ be “red is drawn first” and $G$ be “green appears.” Then

$R_{1}=\{(r,b),(r,g)\}$,

$G=\{(r,g),(g,r),(b,g),(g,b)\}$.

The outcome $(r,g)$ belongs to both events, so they are not disjoint. If the physical procedure treats the six ordered pairs symmetrically, a uniform law is defensible. The set $\Omega$ alone does not force that law; a weighted drawing mechanism could assign different probabilities to the same six outcomes.

### A common modelling failure

For “the first digit appearing in a randomly selected positive integer,” there is no uniform probability distribution on all positive integers. If every integer had the same positive mass $p$, the total mass would be infinite; if each had mass zero, countable additivity would give total mass zero. The phrase “randomly selected integer” is incomplete until a sampling rule is supplied, such as uniform selection from $\{1,...,N\}$ followed by an explicit limit.

## 2. Sigma-algebras encode answerable event questions

For a finite or countable sample space, one often allows every subset as an event. On uncountable spaces, using every subset is incompatible with the standard length-like probability constructions. The theory therefore declares a family $F$ of measurable events.

A **sigma-algebra** $F$ on $\Omega$ satisfies:

1. $\Omega \in F$;
2. if $A\in F$, then $A^{c}\in F$;
3. if $A_{1},A_{2},...\in F$, then $\cup_{n\ge 1}A_{n}\in F$.

The pair $(\Omega ,F)$ is a **measurable space**. Closure under countable operations is matched to the limit operations used throughout probability.

Several consequences follow immediately. Since $\varnothing =\Omega^{c}$, the empty event is measurable. De Morgan's laws give closure under countable intersections:

$\cap_{n\ge 1}A_{n}=(\cup_{n\ge 1}A_{n}^{c})^{c}$.

Set differences are measurable because $A\setminus B=A\cap B^{c}$.

### Information coarsening

Let $\Omega =\{1,2,3,4\}$ and suppose an instrument reports only whether the outcome is odd or even. Its event family is

$F=\{\varnothing ,\{1,3\},\{2,4\},\Omega \}$.

The singleton $\{1\}$ is not measurable at this information level: the observation cannot distinguish $1$ from $3$. Enlarging $F$ means allowing more event questions. Later, conditional expectation will formalise prediction constrained by such information.

### Sigma-algebra generated by a partition

If $C_{1},...,C_{k}$ are disjoint nonempty cells whose union is $\Omega$, then all unions of these cells form a sigma-algebra. It is the smallest sigma-algebra that contains each cell. In a finite model, every sigma-algebra arises from a unique partition into its smallest nonempty events, called atoms of that sigma-algebra.

The word “atom” here describes indivisibility relative to $F$, not physical indivisibility. An atom may contain many sample points that the current information cannot separate.

## 3. A probability measure has three axioms

A **probability measure** on $(\Omega ,F)$ is a function $P:F\to [0,1]$ satisfying:

1. **normalisation:** $\mathbb{P}(\Omega )=1$;
2. **nonnegativity:** $\mathbb{P}(A)\ge 0$ for every $A\in F$;
3. **countable additivity:** if $A_{1},A_{2},...$ are pairwise disjoint measurable events, then

   $\mathbb{P}(\cup_{n\ge 1}A_{n})=\sum_{n\ge 1}\mathbb{P}(A_{n})$.

The triple $(\Omega ,F,P)$ is a **probability space**.

These axioms do not mention independence, equal likelihood, densities, or long-run frequencies. Those are constructions or interpretations added in particular models. The axioms instead say how a coherent event mass behaves once a model has been chosen.

### Why countable rather than only finite additivity?

Probability repeatedly approximates an event by an increasing or decreasing sequence of simpler events. Countable additivity makes the limiting event compatible with the limiting probabilities. If only finite additivity were required, this continuity could fail. The distinction is invisible in a finite sample space and decisive in infinite ones.

## 4. The finite event calculus is derived, not memorised

Set $A_{1}=\Omega$ and $A_{n}=\varnothing$ for $n\ge 2$. These events are disjoint, so countable additivity gives

$1=\mathbb{P}(\Omega )=\mathbb{P}(\Omega )+\sum_{n\ge 2}\mathbb{P}(\varnothing )$.

Nonnegativity forces $\mathbb{P}(\varnothing )=0$. A shorter finite argument uses $\Omega =\Omega \cup \varnothing$ disjointly.

### Complement rule

Because $\Omega =A\cup A^{c}$ disjointly,

$\mathbb{P}(A^{c})=1-\mathbb{P}(A)$.

### Monotonicity and differences

If $A\subseteq B$, then $B=A\cup (B\setminus A)$ disjointly. Hence

$\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B\setminus A)\ge \mathbb{P}(A)$

and

$\mathbb{P}(B\setminus A)=\mathbb{P}(B)-\mathbb{P}(A)$.

In particular, $0\le \mathbb{P}(A)\le 1$ follows from $\varnothing \subseteq A\subseteq \Omega$.

### Two-event inclusion–exclusion

The union decomposes as $A\cup B=A\cup (B\setminus A)$, while $B=(B\setminus A)\cup (A\cap B)$. Eliminating $\mathbb{P}(B\setminus A)$ yields

$\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)$.

Adding $\mathbb{P}(A)$ and $\mathbb{P}(B)$ double-counts the overlap; inclusion–exclusion repairs exactly that duplication. The formula does not require independence.

### Worked audit: at least one system alarm

Suppose alarm $A$ triggers with probability $0.08$, alarm $B$ with probability $0.05$, and both trigger with probability $0.02$. Then

$\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=0.08+0.05-0.02=0.11$.

The value $0.13$ would count joint triggers twice. The product $0.08\cdot 0.05$ would silently impose independence and contradict the stated overlap.

## 5. Continuity turns nested approximations into limits

Write $A_{n}\uparrow A$ when $A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq ...$ and $A=\cup_{n} A_{n}$.

**Continuity from below.** If $A_{n}\uparrow A$, then $\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\to \mathbb{P}(A)$.

**Proof.** Define disjoint increments

$B_{1}=A_{1}$, and $B_{n}=A_{n}\setminus A_{n-1}$ for $n\ge 2$.

Then $A=\cup_{n} B_{n}$ and $A_{N}=\cup_{n\le N}B_{n}$. Countable additivity gives

$\mathbb{P}(A)=\sum_{n\ge 1}\mathbb{P}(B_{n})$,

$\mathbb{P}(A_{N})=\sum_{n\le N}\mathbb{P}(B_{n})$.

The finite partial sums increase to the series, so $\mathbb{P}(A_{N})\to \mathbb{P}(A)$. `□`

Write $A_{n}\downarrow A$ when $A_{1}⊇A_{2}⊇...$ and $A=\cap_{n} A_{n}$.

**Continuity from above.** If $A_{n}\downarrow A$, then $\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\to \mathbb{P}(A)$.

**Proof.** The complements satisfy $A_{n}^{c}\uparrow A^{c}$. Continuity from below and the complement rule give

$1-\mathbb{P}(A_{n})=\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{c})\to \mathbb{P}(A^{c})=1-\mathbb{P}(A)$.

Therefore $\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\to \mathbb{P}(A)$. `□`

For a general measure, continuity from above requires the first set to have finite measure. A probability measure automatically satisfies that condition.

### Worked example: eventual success

Suppose a model assigns probability $(1-p)^{n}$ to failure on each of the first $n$ trials, where $0<p\le 1$. Let $F_{n}$ be that decreasing failure event. The event of never succeeding is $F=\cap_{n} F_{n}$. Since $(1-p)^{n}\to 0$, continuity from above gives $\mathbb{P}(F)=0$. Thus eventual success has probability one.

“Probability one” does not mean a finite deterministic deadline exists. For every fixed $n$, failure through time $n$ still has positive probability when $p<1$.

## 6. Countable subadditivity controls overlap

Events need not be disjoint for the **union bound**:

$\mathbb{P}(\cup_{n\ge 1}A_{n})\le \sum_{n\ge 1}\mathbb{P}(A_{n})$.

To prove it, disjointify the sequence:

$B_{1}=A_{1}$,

$B_{n}=A_{n}\setminus\bigcup_{k<n}A_{k}$ for $n\ge 2$.

Then the $B_{n}$ are disjoint, have the same union as the $A_{n}$, and satisfy $B_{n}\subseteq A_{n}$. Countable additivity and monotonicity give

$\mathbb{P}(\cup A_{n})=\sum \mathbb{P}(B_{n})\le \sum \mathbb{P}(A_{n})$.

The bound may exceed one; replacing its right-hand side by $\min(1,\sum \mathbb{P}(A_{n}))$ preserves validity. A bound larger than one is uninformative, not false.

### Events occurring infinitely often

For events $A_{1},A_{2},...$, define

$\limsup A_{n}=\cap_{m\ge 1}\cup_{n\ge m}A_{n}$.

An outcome lies in this event exactly when it belongs to infinitely many $A_{n}$. It survives every tail union.

**Lemma 1.1 (First Borel–Cantelli).** If $\sum_{n} \mathbb{P}(A_{n})<\infty$, then

$\mathbb{P}(\limsup A_{n})=0$.

**Proof.** For every $m$,

$\limsup A_{n}\subseteq \cup_{n\ge m}A_{n}$,

so the union bound gives

$\mathbb{P}(\limsup A_{n})\le \sum_{n\ge m}\mathbb{P}(A_{n})$.

The tail of a convergent nonnegative series tends to zero. Hence the left side, which does not depend on $m$, must be zero. `□`

No independence is needed. The converse direction will require additional dependence control.

## 7. Equal likelihood is a model theorem, not an axiom

If $\Omega$ is finite and every singleton has the same probability, normalisation forces each to have mass $1/\lvert \Omega \rvert$. Therefore

$\mathbb{P}(A)=\lvert A\rvert /\lvert \Omega \rvert$.

This ratio is valid only after equal likelihood has been justified. Physical symmetry, a randomisation mechanism, or a declared mathematical law can provide that justification. Merely listing outcomes does not.

### Worked comparison: same outcomes, different laws

Let $\Omega =\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. A fair die has $\mathbb{P}(\{i\})=1/6$. A loaded die might have

$\mathbb{P}(\{1\})=...=\mathbb{P}(\{5\})=0.1$, and $\mathbb{P}(\{6\})=0.5$.

For event $E=\{2,4,6\}$, the fair law gives $\mathbb{P}(E)=1/2$; the loaded law gives $\mathbb{P}(E)=0.7$. The event set is identical. Probability belongs to the pair of event and law, not to the event description alone.

### Symmetry through a group action

Suppose a finite group of transformations acts transitively on $\Omega$, and the law is invariant under every transformation. Transitivity means any outcome can be carried to any other. Invariance then makes all singleton masses equal, so the law is uniform. This is a rigorous route from declared symmetry to equal likelihood.

If the action has several orbits, invariance forces equality only within each orbit. It does not determine how total mass is divided between orbits.

## 8. Null events and impossible events are different

The empty event is impossible and has probability zero. The converse fails on continuous spaces. Under the uniform law on $[0,1]$, every singleton $\{x\}$ has probability zero, yet some point is realised. Countable additivity does not say

$\mathbb{P}([0,1])=\sum_{x\in [0,1]}\mathbb{P}(\{x\})$,

because that is an uncountable sum, not one of the axiom's countable unions.

An assertion holding **almost surely** may fail on a nonempty null event. This distinction becomes essential when comparing random variables, defining conditional laws, and passing to limits.

### Model boundary: mathematical probability versus empirical frequency

A probability measure is part of a mathematical model. Data may challenge or estimate that model, but the axioms alone do not say that observed frequencies must resemble assigned probabilities in a short run. A law of large numbers later proves a precise long-run statement under explicit repetition and dependence assumptions. Invoking “long-run frequency” before specifying those assumptions reverses the logical order.

## 9. An event-proof audit

Before accepting a foundational probability argument, ask:

1. Is $\Omega$ detailed enough to decide each event under discussion?
2. Is the event family closed under the countable operations the proof uses?
3. Has the law $P$ been specified independently of the event description?
4. Are the events actually disjoint before additivity is applied?
5. If equal likelihood is used, what symmetry or mechanism establishes it?
6. If a limit of events is used, is it an increasing union, a decreasing intersection, or an “infinitely often” limsup?
7. Is probability zero being confused with the empty set?
8. Is an empirical claim being attributed to the axioms without a sampling theorem?

These checks prevent most early errors before arithmetic begins.

## Further worked models

### Worked Example 1.1: information generated by two reports

Let $\Omega=\{a,b,c,d,e,f\}$ carry the uniform law. One report says whether the outcome belongs to $A=\{a,b,c\}$; another says whether it belongs to $B=\{a,d\}$. Determine the information available when both reports are observed.

The intersections of each reported event or its complement are
$A\cap B=\{a\}$, $A\cap B^c=\{b,c\}$, $A^c\cap B=\{d\}$, and $A^c\cap B^c=\{e,f\}$. They are disjoint nonempty cells covering the space. The sigma-algebra generated by both reports consists of all unions of these four cells. It has sixteen events, even though the original sample space has sixty four subsets.

For instance $\{a,d\}$ is observable, but $\{b\}$ is not: the reports never distinguish $b$ from $c$. Cell probabilities are respectively $1/6,1/3,1/6,1/3$. An observable event's mass is the sum of its cell masses, so an event with two cells need not have probability one half.

This calculation separates three levels: six underlying outcomes, four distinguishable information cells, and a probability law assigning unequal masses to those cells. Passing to a report can discard distinctions without changing the underlying outcome model.

### Worked Example 1.2: countable versus uncountable unions

On $[0,1]$ with the uniform Borel law, let $Q$ be the rational points. List them as $q_1,q_2,\ldots$; each singleton has probability zero. Countable subadditivity gives
$P(Q)\le\sum_{n\ge1}P(\{q_n\})=0$. Hence an irrational value occurs with probability one, although every interval also contains rational points.

In contrast, the union of all singletons in $[0,1]$ is the whole interval and has probability one. The axiom does not equate its probability with an uncountable sum of singleton masses. The difference is the size of the indexing family, not whether the component sets look alike.

Now let $Q_n$ contain the first $n$ listed rationals. Each has mass zero, and $Q_n\uparrow Q$, so continuity from below recovers the same result. This is a valid increasing approximation because the union is countable. Replacing it with the phrase “add all points” would erase the hypothesis that licences the calculation.

### Worked Example 1.3: a summable sequence of exceptional errors

Suppose an approximation at stage $n\ge1$ has an error event $E_n$ satisfying $P(E_n)\le2^{-n}$. No independence is supplied. For a fixed cutoff $m$,
$P(\bigcup_{n\ge m}E_n)\le\sum_{n\ge m}2^{-n}=2^{1-m}$.

The left side is the probability of at least one error after that cutoff. It need not be zero. However, the infinitely-often event lies inside every tail union, so its probability is at most $2^{1-m}$ for every $m$, forcing probability zero. Almost surely only finitely many errors occur.

The argument establishes a random eventual cutoff, which can depend on the realised outcome. It supplies no deterministic time after which every possible outcome is error-free. It also does not conclude that the total number of errors has a particular distribution. These distinctions are useful when a probability-one statement is translated back into the behaviour of one infinite sequence.

### 5. Difference probability

**Exercise 1.5**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-5"></a>

### 6. Three-event inclusion–exclusion

**Exercise 1.6**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-6"></a>

Prove

$\mathbb{P}(A\cup B\cup C)=\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)+\mathbb{P}(C)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap C)-\mathbb{P}(B\cap C)+\mathbb{P}(A\cap B\cap C)$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

First apply the two-event identity to the union of A and B together with C.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Distribute the intersection with C over the first union and apply the identity again.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** Apply two-event inclusion–exclusion to $(A\cup B)\cup C$:

$\mathbb{P}(A\cup B\cup C)=\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)+\mathbb{P}(C)-\mathbb{P}((A\cup B)\cap C)$.

Now $\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)$, and

$(A\cup B)\cap C=(A\cap C)\cup (B\cap C)$.

Applying inclusion–exclusion to the last union subtracts $\mathbb{P}(A\cap B\cap C)$ once. It is subtracted inside the outer minus, so it enters the final expression with a plus sign.


</details>

### 13. Singletons of a continuous uniform law

**Exercise 1.13**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-13"></a>

Using interval probabilities, show that a fixed singleton $\{x\}\subseteq [0,1]$ has probability zero under the uniform law.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Contain the singleton in intervals whose lengths tend to zero.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Monotonicity bounds its mass by every one of those lengths.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** For every $n$, the singleton is contained in an interval within $[0,1]$ of length at most $2/n$. Monotonicity gives $\mathbb{P}(\{x\})\le 2/n$. Since this holds for every $n$, the nonnegative number $\mathbb{P}(\{x\})$ must be zero. At endpoints one may use a one-sided interval of length $1/n$.


</details>

### 14. A nonuniform finite law

**Exercise 1.14**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-14"></a>

On $\Omega =\{a,b,c\}$, let singleton masses be proportional to $1,2,3$. Find the law and $\mathbb{P}(\{a,c\})$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Introduce one common proportionality constant.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Normalise the three weights, then add the two requested masses.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** The proportionality constant $k$ satisfies $k+2k+3k=1$, so $k=1/6$. Thus the masses are $1/6,1/3,1/2$, and

$\mathbb{P}(\{a,c\})=1/6+1/2=2/3$.

The cardinality ratio $2/3$ happens to agree here, but only accidentally. For $\{a,b\}$, the actual mass is $1/2$ while the cardinality ratio remains $2/3$.


</details>

### 15. Symmetry and orbits

**Exercise 1.15**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-15"></a>

Suppose a finite law is invariant under a transformation group with two orbits of sizes $2$ and $3$. What does invariance determine?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Invariance equates masses only within each orbit.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Leave the total probability of the first orbit as a free parameter.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** It forces equal singleton mass within each orbit. If total mass $q$ is assigned to the size-two orbit, its points each have mass $q/2$; points in the other orbit each have mass $(1-q)/3$. Invariance alone does not determine $q$, so it does not force a uniform law on all five points.


</details>

### 16. Detect an impossible assignment

**Exercise 1.16**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-16"></a>

Can events satisfy $A\subseteq B$, $\mathbb{P}(A)=0.6$, and $\mathbb{P}(B)=0.4$?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Recall the consequence of event inclusion for probability.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compare the supplied numbers with the monotonicity inequality.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** No. Monotonicity derived from nonnegativity and additivity requires $\mathbb{P}(A)\le \mathbb{P}(B)$ whenever $A\subseteq B$. The proposed values violate that theorem, so no probability measure can realise them.


</details>

### 17. Eventual success without independence language

**Exercise 1.17**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-17"></a>

Suppose directly that $\mathbb{P}(F_{n})=0.9^{n}$, where $F_{n}$ is failure on the first $n$ attempts and $F_{n+1}\subseteq F_{n}$. Prove eventual success has probability one without assuming anything else.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use the intersection of the nested failure events.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The supplied finite-time probabilities already contain all information needed for continuity.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** The event of never succeeding is $F=\cap_{n}F_{n}$. Continuity from above gives

$\mathbb{P}(F)=\lim_{n}0.9^{n}=0$.

Therefore $\mathbb{P}(F^{c})=1$. Independence may have been used elsewhere to derive the formula $0.9^{n}$, but once that formula and nesting are given, it is not needed again in this proof.


</details>

### 18. Same sample space, incompatible answers

**Exercise 1.18**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-18"></a>

Construct two probability laws on $\Omega =\{0,1\}$ giving different probabilities to event $\{1\}$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Specify a different Bernoulli parameter for each law.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Give complementary mass to zero so both assignments normalize.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** For any $p\in [0,1]$, define $\mathbb{P}_{p}(\{1\})=p$ and $\mathbb{P}_{p}(\{0\})=1-p$, then extend by additivity to $\varnothing$ and $\Omega$. Choosing $p=1/2$ and $p=9/10$ gives two laws on the same measurable space with different answers. The sample space does not determine probability.


</details>

### 19. Infinitely often versus eventually always

**Exercise 1.19**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-19"></a>

Define

$\liminf A_{n}=\cup_{m\ge 1}\cap_{n\ge m}A_{n}$.

Explain the event in words and prove $\liminf A_{n}\subseteq \limsup A_{n}$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Translate the union of tail intersections into a statement about a cutoff.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

An event occurring after every sufficiently late time necessarily occurs infinitely often.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** Membership in $\liminf A_{n}$ means there is some cutoff after which the outcome belongs to every $A_{n}$: the events occur eventually always. Such an outcome certainly belongs to infinitely many $A_{n}$, which is membership in $\limsup A_{n}$. Hence the inclusion holds.


</details>

### 20. Repair a false additivity proof

**Exercise 1.20**
<a id="event-models-and-axioms-ex-20"></a>

A student writes $\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)$ because “probabilities add under or.” Identify the missing condition and give the correct general formula.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Determine when the direct finite-additivity axiom applies.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

For an overlapping pair, subtract the mass counted in both terms.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Solution.** Direct additivity applies when $A$ and $B$ are disjoint. In general their intersection is counted twice by $\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)$, so

$\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)$.

If the intersection merely has probability zero, the numerical sum is still exact, even though the events need not be set-theoretically disjoint.


</details>

## 11. Summary and forward route

A probability space separates three choices: outcomes $\Omega$, measurable events $F$, and law $P$. Sigma-algebras make event questions stable under complements and countable limits. Normalisation, nonnegativity, and countable additivity yield complements, monotonicity, inclusion–exclusion, continuity, subadditivity, and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma. Equal likelihood and independence are not axioms; each needs its own model justification.

The next chapter, [Counting, Conditioning, and Bayes' Rule](counting-conditioning-and-bayes.md), asks how probabilities change after information is learned. Conditioning will also reveal why “independent before observation” and “independent after observation” are different claims.
