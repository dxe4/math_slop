
# Notation, Ambiguity, and Proof Conventions

## Probability spaces and ambient objects

A probability space is $(\Omega,F,P)$. The outcome space $\Omega$ contains elementary outcomes; the sigma-algebra $F$ contains events; the probability measure $P$ is defined on $F$. These roles are not interchangeable. An outcome $\omega$ need not be observable, and not every subset of an uncountable $\Omega$ is silently declared measurable.

Set complements are relative to the current ambient space. Thus $A^{c}=\Omega \setminus A$ for an event $A$, while the complement of a Borel set in $R$ is taken in $R$ unless stated otherwise. $\varnothing$ is the empty event. $\mathbf{1}_{A}$ is the indicator of $A$, equal to one on $A$ and zero elsewhere. Countable unions and intersections are indexed explicitly when the starting index matters.

When more than one law appears, subscripts identify it: $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}$, $\mathbb{E}_{\theta}$, or $\operatorname{Law}_{\theta}(X)$. A change of measure is never communicated only by reusing the symbol $P$. In a finite-population or algorithmic argument, the source of randomness—sampling, assignment, input generation, or internal random bits—is named.

Equal-probability outcomes are never inferred from a finite outcome space alone. The uniform law is a model declaration. Physical symmetry, exchangeability, or an algorithmic construction may motivate that declaration, but the corresponding mathematical assumption is still recorded.

## Random variables, values, and laws

A random variable $X$ is a measurable map from $\Omega$ to a stated measurable space. It need not be real-valued; a vector, graph, path, permutation, or dataset can be random. Uppercase letters usually denote random objects and lowercase letters their possible or realized values. This typographic convention is helpful but does not replace a declaration.

$\mathbb{P}_{X}$, $\mu_{X}$, and $\operatorname{Law}(X)$ denote the pushforward law of $X$; locally the text chooses one and defines it. For measurable $B$,

$\mathbb{P}_{X}(B)=\mathbb{P}(X \in B)=\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(B))$.

A distribution is a measure, not a formula. A cumulative distribution function is $F_{X}(x)=\mathbb{P}(X\le x)$. A probability mass function assigns $p_{X}(x)=\mathbb{P}(X=x)$ on a countable support. A density $f_{X}$ is relative to a reference measure and satisfies $\mathbb{P}(X \in B)=\int_{B} f_{X} d \lambda$ when Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ is intended. For a continuous law, $f_{X}(x)$ is not $\mathbb{P}(X=x)$.

The support of a law is declared in the sense relevant to the claim: a set of possible mass points, an essential support, or the smallest closed full-measure set. These notions need not coincide. A formula stated “for $x$ in the support” does not define behavior outside it unless the context does so.

## Three kinds of equality

The notation distinguishes:

- $X=Y$ pointwise when $X(\omega)=Y(\omega)$ for every relevant $\omega$;
- $X=Y$ almost surely when $\mathbb{P}(X=Y)=1$, always labelled “a.s.” if the exceptional null set matters;
- $X \sim Y$ or $\operatorname{Law}(X)=\operatorname{Law}(Y)$ when the two random variables have the same distribution.

Pointwise equality implies almost-sure equality, which implies equality in distribution. Neither converse holds in general. Expectations and distributional calculations may be unchanged by an almost-sure modification, while pathwise properties at a specified outcome might not be. Two random variables with the same law can have entirely different dependence on other variables.

An identity involving conditional expectation normally means equality almost surely. A regular conditional probability may be defined only up to a null set of conditioning values. Versions are chosen when pointwise formulas are needed, and conclusions do not silently depend on arbitrary behavior on that null set.

## Conditioning and information

For an event $B$ with $\mathbb{P}(B)>0$, $\mathbb{P}(A \mid B)=\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)/\mathbb{P}(B)$. The ratio is not used when $\mathbb{P}(B)=0$. Conditioning on a random variable or sigma-algebra is handled through conditional expectation or a conditional-law construction rather than by treating a density value as an event probability.

$\sigma(X)$ is the sigma-algebra generated by $X$, representing the information revealed by observing $X$. A sub-sigma-algebra $G \subseteq F$ is an information state. $\mathbb{E}[X \mid G]$ denotes a $G$-measurable version of conditional expectation and is characterized by matching integrals over every event in $G$. The expression $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]$ abbreviates $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \sigma(Y)]$; when convenient it may be represented as $g(Y)$ for a measurable $g$.

Bayes' rule is an identity under a declared joint law. “Prior” and “posterior” terminology is used only when the joint model actually gives one variable that role. Conditioning changes a law; it does not change the already realized outcome. Statements before and after conditioning name their probability measures when confusion is possible.

## Independence and dependence

Events $A$ and $B$ are independent under $P$ when $\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)$. A family of events is mutually independent when every finite subfamily satisfies the product identity. Pairwise independence is weaker. Random variables are independent when their generated sigma-algebras are independent; equivalently, their joint law factors on measurable rectangles under the hypotheses stated in the relevant chapter.

The book never uses “independent” without objects and a governing law close at hand. Separate labels, repeated notation, uncorrelatedness, or draws produced at different clock times do not establish independence. “iid” means independent and identically distributed; both components may fail separately.

Conditional independence is indexed by the conditioning information. It does not generally imply unconditional independence, nor does unconditional independence generally survive conditioning on an arbitrary third variable. Every use of a product rule states whether it follows from ordinary independence, conditional independence, or the chain rule alone.

Covariance is $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])]$ when the required second moments exist. Zero covariance is not independence except under additional structure. Correlation is undefined when either variance is zero, and its numerical magnitude does not by itself measure all forms of dependence.

## Expectation, integration, and existence

For nonnegative measurable $X$, $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is defined as an extended value in $[0,\infty]$. For a signed real variable, $\mathbb{E}[X]$ exists as a finite quantity when $\mathbb{E}[\lvert X\rvert ]<\infty$; otherwise positive and negative parts are tracked separately and the indeterminate expression $\infty-\infty$ is forbidden. Variance requires a finite second moment unless an extended convention is explicitly introduced.

Linearity of expectation for integrable variables does not require independence. Factorization such as $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ requires independence or another sufficient condition and existence of the quantities involved. Infinite sums of nonnegative terms may be exchanged with expectation by monotone convergence; signed sums require an appropriate absolute-integrability, domination, or uniform-integrability argument.

Every interchange of limit, derivative, sum, integral, conditional expectation, or supremum is treated as a mathematical step. The proof names a licence—Tonelli, Fubini, monotone convergence, dominated convergence, uniform integrability, bounded convergence, differentiation under the integral sign, or another stated result—and checks the hypotheses relevant to that use.

Norms are written $\lVert X \rVert_{p}=(\mathbb{E}\lvert X\rvert^{p})^{1/p}$ for $1\le p<\infty$ when finite, and $\lVert X \rVert_{\infty}$ is the essential supremum. Membership in $L^{p}$ concerns equivalence classes modulo almost-sure equality. Inner-product projection statements in $L^{2}$ therefore concern those equivalence classes, even when representatives are used in calculations.

## Transforms and generating functions

For a nonnegative integer-valued $X$, the probability generating function is $G_{X}(s)=\mathbb{E}[s^{X}]$ on its stated domain. The moment generating function is $M_{X}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(tX)]$ only where this expectation is finite. The characteristic function is $\varphi_{X}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(itX)]$ and exists for every real $t$.

Formal differentiation of a transform does not establish that a moment exists. The text states the neighborhood, domination, or power-series argument that licenses differentiation. Equality of transforms implies equality of laws only under the appropriate uniqueness theorem and domain conditions. A transform's algebraic expression is not silently extended beyond its convergence domain.

## Convergence and asymptotic notation

Almost-sure convergence is written $X_{n}\to_{\mathrm{a.s.}} X$, convergence in probability $X_{n}\to_{P} X$, convergence in $L^{p}$ as $\lVert X_{n}-X \rVert_{p}\to 0$, and convergence in distribution $X_{n}\to_{d} X$. These modes are never collapsed into a generic arrow when the distinction carries mathematical content.

Almost-sure convergence means that one event of probability one exists on which the numerical sequence converges. It is stronger than saying that each $n$ has its own high-probability good event. Convergence in probability controls $\mathbb{P}(\lvert X_{n}-X\rvert >\varepsilon)$ for each fixed positive $\varepsilon$. Convergence in distribution concerns bounded continuous test functions or distribution functions at continuity points; it need not occur on a common probability space.

Deterministic $a_{n}=O(b_{n})$ and $a_{n}=o(b_{n})$ are separated from stochastic $O_{P}$ and $o_{P}$. Constants hidden in bounds state their parameter dependence when it matters. An assertion “with high probability” is replaced by an explicit event and failure probability, or by a declared convention such as probability tending to one as $n\to \infty$.

Every limit states what tends to what and what remains fixed. Pointwise results fix a model parameter before the limit. Uniform results display the relevant supremum. A central limit theorem is a distributional limit, not the claim that a finite sum is normally distributed. A finite approximation uses a named metric—Kolmogorov, total variation, Wasserstein, or another defined distance—and an explicit bound when available.

$\limsup$ and $\liminf$ may refer to numerical sequences, sets, or events; the object is clear from context. The phrase “infinitely often” for events $A_{n}$ means $\limsup_{n} A_{n}=\cap_{m} \cup_{n\ge m} A_{n}$.

## Inequalities, constants, and optimization

Bounds display the event, random variable, threshold range, and parameter restrictions. A tail inequality is not extrapolated outside its stated range. Universal numerical constants may be written $C$ or $c$, but a proof does not reuse the same letter for different values within one displayed argument. Parameter-dependent constants use subscripts such as $C_{p}$.

$\sup$ and $\inf$ need not be attained; $\max$ and $\min$ assert attainment. Equality cases are distinguished from sharpness: a constant can be best possible through a limiting sequence even when no admissible object attains equality. Union bounds do not require independence. Jensen, Hölder, Minkowski, and Cauchy–Schwarz require their domains and equality conditions to be checked when those conditions matter.

## Stochastic processes, filtrations, and stopping

A stochastic process is a family $(X_{t})_{t \in T}$ on one probability space unless a family of laws is explicitly being compared. A filtration $(F_{t})$ is increasing information: $F_{s} \subseteq F_{t}$ for $s\le t$. Adaptedness means $X_{t}$ is $F_{t}$-measurable. A martingale additionally requires integrability and the conditional-mean identity; neither zero drift in an informal sense nor constant ordinary expectations is enough.

A stopping time $\tau$ satisfies $\{\tau\le t\} \in F_{t}$ in discrete time. A stopped value $X_{\tau}$ and stopped process $X_{t \land \tau}$ are distinguished. Optional-stopping conclusions list boundedness, integrability, domination, or increment conditions that make them valid. “No peeking into the future” is motivation, not a substitute for the stopping-time definition.

For a finite Markov chain, row-stochastic and column-stochastic conventions are not mixed. This volume uses the locally declared convention and checks matrix dimensions in each evolution equation. A stationary distribution, a limiting distribution, and an empirical occupation distribution are distinct objects. Irreducibility, aperiodicity, reversibility, and stationarity are separate properties.

## Couplings and distances between laws

A coupling of laws $\mu$ and $\nu$ is a joint law with those marginals. Coupled variables need not be independent; their dependence is chosen to prove a comparison. Total variation conventions differ by a factor of two across sources, so this book defines the convention before every chain of constants and uses it consistently within a chapter.

An equality or bound obtained under one coupling is separated from an intrinsic statement about marginal laws. Constructing a coupling gives an upper bound on an optimal coupling cost; proving optimality requires an additional argument. “Same distribution” never means “the same random variable on the chosen coupling.”

## Monte Carlo and computational evidence

$N$ commonly denotes a simulation budget, while $n$ commonly denotes a modeled sample size; local definitions override this convention. Monte Carlo expectation, variance, and probability refer to algorithmic randomness conditional on fixed inputs unless the target itself includes data randomness. Those two uncertainty sources are not pooled without explanation.

A simulation report records the target, estimator, pseudorandom generator or environment, seed where reproducibility permits, budget, number of independent replications, and numerical uncertainty. A standard error is not called a deterministic error bound. A confidence statement about Monte Carlo error lists the theorem and conditions supporting it. Rare-event estimates report the number of observed events rather than only a rounded proportion.

Plots have text or numerical alternatives. Color is not the sole carrier of meaning. Animation respects reduced-motion preferences. A visual match to a theoretical curve is exploratory evidence, not proof of convergence, independence, normality, or correct implementation. Whenever possible, code checks mass conservation, known moments, symmetry, monotonicity, or another invariant before novel output is interpreted.

## Proof and claim status

A **definition** establishes terminology. A **model assumption** declares the law or mechanism under study. A **theorem** states hypotheses and a mathematical conclusion. A **corollary** specializes an established theorem. A **heuristic** motivates a claim without supplying its proof. An **approximation** names a regime and, when available, an error measure. A **simulation observation** is finite computational evidence. A **counterexample** satisfies every hypothesis of the claim it refutes and violates its conclusion.

The words “exact,” “almost surely,” “independent,” “unbiased,” “concentrated,” “typical,” “negligible,” and “random” do not stand alone when several interpretations are possible. Exact under which law and arithmetic model? Independent under which joint distribution? Negligible in which limit or metric? Random according to which mechanism?

Proofs expose quantifier order. To disprove a universal claim, one admissible counterexample suffices. To prove an existence statement by the probabilistic method, positive probability of success under one valid random construction suffices; it does not necessarily provide an efficient construction. To prove two laws equal, agreement on a generating class requires the accompanying uniqueness argument.

## A final ambiguity check

Before accepting a calculation or proof, a reader should be able to expand it into the following sentence:

“On probability space **[space]**, under law **[law]**, random objects **[objects]** satisfy **[dependence and existence conditions]**; theorem or construction **[licence]** therefore establishes **[exact claim and convergence mode or error]**, with **[boundary]** not asserted.”

If one bracket cannot be filled, the result may still be true, but the argument is not yet complete. This convention is the volume's main safeguard against notation that looks familiar while its mathematical meaning shifts.
