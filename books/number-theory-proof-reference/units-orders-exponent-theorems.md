
# Units, Orders, and Finite Exponent Theorems {#section-1-units-orders-and-finite-exponent-theorems}

Exponent congruences are statements about a finite group, not permission to reduce every exponent under every modulus. This chapter builds the unit group, element order, Euler's theorem, Fermat's theorem, and the Carmichael exponent from first principles. The recurring audit question is: is the base a unit in the named modulus?

Prerequisites are linear congruences, CRT, and prime factorization. Afterward you should be able to compute orders, justify exponent reduction, separate all-residue from unit-only claims, and diagnose pseudoprime evidence.

<a id="section-5-1-the-unit-group"></a>
## 1. The unit group {#section-7-1-the-unit-group}

The invertible residue classes modulo $n$ form $U(n)$. Its size is Euler's totient $\varphi(n)$. Closure follows because a product of integers individually coprime to $n$ is coprime to $n$; Bezout supplies inverses.

If $n=\prod p^e$, inclusion-exclusion or direct counting gives

$\varphi(n)=n \prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p)$.

For a prime power, exactly the multiples of $p$ are nonunits, so $\varphi(p^e)=p^e-p^{e-1}$. CRT gives $U(mn)\cong U(m)\times U(n)$ when $\gcd(m,n)=1$, and the formula becomes multiplicative.

<a id="section-15-2-order-and-cyclic-subgroups"></a>
## 2. Order and cyclic subgroups {#section-17-2-order-and-cyclic-subgroups}

For $a \in U(n)$, its **order** $\operatorname{ord}_{n}(a)$ is the least positive $r$ with $a^r\equiv 1 \bmod n$. Such an $r$ exists because among $1,a,\ldots,a^\varphi(n)$ two powers agree; cancellation produces a positive exponent returning to one.

If $a^k\equiv 1$, division $k=qr+s$, $0\le s<r$, gives $1\equiv a^k=(a^r)^q a^s\equiv a^s$; minimality forces $s=0$. Therefore

$a^k\equiv 1 \bmod n$ exactly when $\operatorname{ord}_{n}(a)\mid k$.

<a id="section-23-worked-order"></a>
### Worked order {#section-25-worked-order}

Modulo $15$, powers of $2$ are $2,4,8,1$, so $\operatorname{ord}_{15}(2)=4$. Therefore $2^{2026}\equiv 2^{2}\equiv 4 \bmod 15$. The exponent was reduced modulo the element order, not blindly modulo $15$.

<a id="section-27-3-lagrange-euler-and-fermat"></a>
## 3. Lagrange, Euler, and Fermat {#section-29-3-lagrange-euler-and-fermat}

In a finite group $G$, the translates of the cyclic subgroup generated by $a$ partition $G$ into equal-size cosets. Hence $\operatorname{ord}(a)\mid \lvert G\rvert$. This is Lagrange's theorem.

Applied to $U(n)$:

**Theorem 5.1 (Euler's).** If $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then $a^\varphi(n)\equiv 1 \bmod n$.

For prime $p$, every nonzero class is a unit and $\varphi(p)=p-1$:

**Theorem 5.2 (Fermat's little).** If $p$ does not divide $a$, then $a^{p-1}\equiv 1 \bmod p$. For every integer $a$, including multiples of $p$, $a^p\equiv a \bmod p$.

The all-residue form works because the nonunit case modulo a prime is just zero. Euler's theorem for a composite modulus does not have that extension.

<a id="section-41-4-universal-exponents-and-carmichael-s-function"></a>
## 4. Universal exponents and Carmichael's function {#section-43-4-universal-exponents-and-carmichael-s-function}

The **exponent** of a finite group is the least positive integer divisible by every element order. For $U(n)$ it is the Carmichael function $\lambda(n)$. Thus every unit satisfies $a^\lambda(n)\equiv 1$, and no smaller positive exponent works for every unit.

For coprime $m,n$, CRT gives

$\lambda(mn)=\operatorname{lcm}(\lambda(m),\lambda(n))$.

For odd prime powers, $\lambda(p^e)=\varphi(p^e)$. For powers of two,

$\lambda(2)=1$, $\lambda(4)=2$, and $\lambda(2^e)=2^{e-2}$ for $e\ge 3$.

These prime-power formulas are proved in Sections 10 and 11 below, without assuming primitive roots; Chapter 8 uses them in the classification of cyclic unit groups. Here they may be used as named formulas with their hypotheses visible.

Example: $\varphi(15)=8$, while $\lambda(15)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,4)=4$. Euler's exponent is valid but not always sharp.

<a id="section-57-5-power-congruences-through-crt"></a>
## 5. Power congruences through CRT {#section-59-5-power-congruences-through-crt}

To determine $a^k \bmod n$, factor $n$ into coprime prime powers, compute locally, and reconstruct. This remains useful when $a$ is not a unit globally.

<a id="section-61-worked-nonunit-power"></a>
### Worked nonunit power {#section-63-worked-nonunit-power}

Find $6^{20} \bmod 35$. Modulo $5$, the result is $1$. Modulo $7$, it is $(-1)^{20}=1$. CRT gives $6^{20}\equiv 1 \bmod 35$. Here $6$ is in fact a unit. By contrast, for $6^{20} \bmod 45$, the factor modulo $9$ is zero from the second power onward, while modulo $5$ it is one; CRT gives the unique class $36 \bmod 45$.

<a id="section-65-6-fermat-tests-and-pseudoprimes"></a>
## 6. Fermat tests and pseudoprimes {#section-67-6-fermat-tests-and-pseudoprimes}

If $n$ is prime and $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then $a^{n-1}\equiv 1 \bmod n$. Failure proves compositeness. Passing for one base proves only that $n$ is a **Fermat probable prime** to that base.

For example $341=11*31$ and $2^{10}=1024\equiv 1 \bmod 341$, so $2^{340}\equiv 1 \bmod 341$. It passes the base-two Fermat test while composite.

A **Carmichael number** is composite and passes the Fermat congruence for every coprime base. Korselt's criterion says that composite squarefree $n$ is Carmichael exactly when $p-1 \mid n-1$ for every prime $p\mid n$. The criterion is a theorem, not a primality algorithm; Section 13 below proves it, and Chapter 22 compares stronger certificates.

<a id="section-73-7-exponent-audit"></a>
## 7. Exponent audit {#section-75-7-exponent-audit}

Before reducing an exponent, record:

1. the modulus and whether the base is a unit;
2. the group in which the order is taken;
3. a valid multiple of that order—element order, $\lambda(n)$, or $\varphi(n)$;
4. the special handling of prime-power factors where the base is not a unit;
5. whether the result is a correctness proof, a compositeness witness, or only probable-prime evidence.

<a id="section-83-exercises-with-complete-solutions"></a>
## 8. Cosets explain the divisibility in Lagrange's theorem {#section-371-8-cosets-explain-the-divisibility-in-lagrange-s-theorem}

All moduli in this development are integers $n\ge2$, and every exponent called universal is positive. Cancellation is permitted inside $U(n)$ because its elements have inverses. It is not a rule for arbitrary residues. The finite-group proof makes that domain visible.

Let $a$ lie in a finite group $G$, with order $r$. The powers $1,a,\ldots,a^{r-1}$ are distinct: equality of two would give an earlier positive return to one after multiplying by an inverse. They form a subgroup $H$, since exponents can be reduced modulo $r$ for products and inverses. For any $g\in G$, multiplication $h\mapsto gh$ bijects $H$ with the left coset $gH$. Thus every coset has $r$ elements.

Two cosets that meet are equal. If $gh_1=kh_2$, then $g=k(h_2h_1^{-1})$; multiplying by elements of $H$ shows $gH\subseteq kH$, and the reverse argument gives equality. Every group element lies in a coset, so these equal-size sets partition $G$. Therefore $|G|$ is an integer multiple of $r$. This is the full counting mechanism behind Lagrange, not an assertion that a short list of powers must somehow fill the group.

For $U(n)$ the conclusion is $r\mid\varphi(n)$, and hence $a^{\varphi(n)}=1$. The order can be much smaller than the group size. If a candidate order $r$ is proposed, a certificate needs both $a^r=1$ and evidence excluding its proper divisors. Once $r$ is factored, it is enough to check $a^{r/q}\ne1$ for each prime $q\mid r$: a smaller order dividing $r$ would divide at least one of those quotients.

The same reasoning proves the equality test for powers: for integers $j,k$, $a^j=a^k$ exactly when $r\mid j-k$, where negative powers use the group inverse. For nonnegative powers this gives the familiar exponent-reduction rule. For a nonunit the inverse step may not exist, so the equality proof cannot simply be copied.

This is an instance of a general proof habit. Name the finite set being partitioned, show why two pieces either coincide or are disjoint, and exhibit the equal-size bijections. Counting becomes reliable once those three claims are proved. The fact that the group operation happens to be modular multiplication does not weaken any of them.

<a id="section-383-9-element-orders-and-universal-exponents-have-different"></a>
## 9. Element orders and universal exponents have different quantifiers {#section-385-9-element-orders-and-universal-exponents-have-different}

An element order controls one base. The exponent $M=\lambda(n)$ controls every unit, so it is the least common multiple of all their orders. If an integer $E$ makes every unit satisfy $a^E=1$, each order divides $E$, and therefore $\lambda(n)\mid E$. Conversely a multiple of $\lambda(n)$ annihilates every unit. Since all orders divide $\varphi(n)$, their least common multiple also divides $\varphi(n)$.

A least common multiple is not, by its definition alone, the order of one element. In a general finite group the exponent need not be attained as an element order. The later cyclicity argument for finite subgroups of fields uses commutativity and the field root bound to construct a generator; it must not assume its desired generator from the definition of exponent. The prime-power exponent proof below avoids that circular step.

CRT supplies a second useful quantifier check. For coprime $m,n$, a unit modulo $mn$ corresponds exactly to a pair of local units. The least common multiple of the two local exponents annihilates every pair, giving one universal upper exponent. For the reverse divisibility, choose any unit in one coordinate and one in the other coordinate. Every universal exponent for the product must annihilate each such local unit, so it is divisible by both local exponents. Thus
\[
 \lambda(mn)=\operatorname{lcm}(\lambda(m),\lambda(n)).
\]
The same argument gives the lcm formula for the order of one CRT pair, but those local orders may be smaller than the local universal exponents.

For example, modulo $72=8\cdot9$ the exponent will be $\operatorname{lcm}(2,6)=6$, whereas the unit group has $\varphi(72)=24$ elements. The unit $5$ has order six: $5^2\equiv25$, $5^3\equiv53$ and $5^6\equiv1\pmod{72}$, excluding the proper divisors two and three. This is a certificate for that element, not a claim that all units have order six.

Reduction from a larger prime power to a smaller one is a surjective map on unit groups: any integer prime to $p$ represents a unit at every level. Therefore the exponent at the lower level divides the exponent at the upper level. Such reduction loses information; a base that returns to one modulo $p$ may need a larger exponent modulo $p^e$. The next two sections determine exactly how much extra exponent can be required.

<a id="section-399-10-odd-prime-power-exponents-from-a-sharp-valuation"></a>
## 10. Odd prime-power exponents from a sharp valuation {#section-401-10-odd-prime-power-exponents-from-a-sharp-valuation}

Let $p$ be odd. We first prove a valuation lemma used again in primitive-root lifting. If an integer $u$ has finite $r=v_p(u-1)\ge1$, write $u=1+cp^r$ with $p\nmid c$. In the binomial expansion of $u^p-1$, the first term is $cp^{r+1}$. Every middle term has valuation at least $1+2r\ge r+2$, since $p$ divides $\binom pj$ for $1\le j\le p-1$. The last term has valuation $pr\ge r+2$, using $p\ge3$. Thus the first term cannot cancel with the others:
\[
 v_p(u^p-1)=r+1,\qquad
 v_p(u^{p^s}-1)=r+s\quad(s\ge0).
\]
The second formula follows by induction. The case $u=1$ is separate, with all differences zero rather than a finite valuation.

Taking $u=1+p$ gives
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{p^e}(1+p)=p^{e-1}.
\]
For $e=1$ the residue is one. For $e\ge2$, its $p^{e-1}$st power is one modulo $p^e$, while the preceding $p^{e-2}$nd power is not. Its order divides $p^{e-1}$ and is therefore a power of $p$, forcing the claimed exact value.

Now let $M$ be any universal exponent modulo $p^e$. It must be divisible by $p^{e-1}$ from this element. It must also be divisible by $p-1$, without assuming a primitive root. Reduce each unit to the nonzero field residues and divide $M=q(p-1)+t$, $0\le t<p-1$. Fermat gives $a^M=a^t$ modulo $p$. If $t>0$, the nonzero polynomial $X^t-1$ would vanish on all $p-1$ nonzero residues, impossible because it has degree $t<p-1$.

For completeness, that field root bound is purely algebraic: a root $a$ allows division by the monic factor $X-a$; at another root $b\ne a$, cancellation of the nonzero scalar $b-a$ makes the quotient vanish at $b$. Induction removes distinct roots one at a time, so a nonzero polynomial has at most its degree many roots. This proof uses no exponent or primitive-root theorem. For the constant polynomial arising at $t=0$, the contradiction is not invoked.

Since $p^{e-1}$ and $p-1$ are coprime, their product divides $M$. Euler already supplies that product as a universal exponent. Hence
\[
 \lambda(p^e)=p^{e-1}(p-1)=\varphi(p^e)
 \qquad(p\text{ odd},\ e\ge1).
\]
The proof establishes an exponent formula before proving cyclicity. Later, a primitive root will be an element attaining the full product. Here two separate lower-bound requirements, together with the upper bound, are enough.

The restrictions explain two common mistakes. The sharp valuation increment need not hold for $p=2$ at the first level, because a second binomial term can have the same valuation. Also $1+p$ alone is not generally a primitive root modulo $p^e$: its order supplies only the $p$-power factor, not the factor $p-1$. It is a carefully chosen exponent witness, not a universal generator.

<a id="section-427-11-powers-of-two-and-the-role-of-five"></a>
## 11. Powers of two and the role of five {#section-429-11-powers-of-two-and-the-role-of-five}

For $e=1$, $U(2)$ is trivial, so $\lambda(2)=1$. For $e=2$, the units modulo four are one and three, giving exponent two. Assume now $e\ge3$. Every odd integer $a=2b+1$ satisfies $a^2-1=4b(b+1)$, divisible by eight because one of $b,b+1$ is even.

If $w\equiv1\pmod{2^k}$, then $w^2\equiv1\pmod{2^{k+1}}$ for $k\ge1$, by expanding $(1+2^kc)^2$. Starting with the odd-square congruence and squaring another $e-3$ times proves
\[
 a^{2^{e-2}}\equiv1\pmod{2^e}.
\]
This is a universal upper exponent, including odd bases whose first square is already one as an integer.

To show sharpness use five. Starting with $v_2(5-1)=2$, suppose $w=1+2^kc$ with $k\ge2$ and $c$ odd. Then
\[
 w^2-1=2^{k+1}c+2^{2k}c^2
\]
has exact valuation $k+1$, since the second term is divisible by $2^{k+2}$. Thus $v_2(5^{2^j}-1)=j+2$. It follows that five has order $2^{e-2}$ modulo $2^e$, and
\[
 \lambda(2^e)=2^{e-2}\qquad(e\ge3).
\]

This argument also supplies the unit-group structure needed for later examples. Every power of five is one modulo four. There are exactly $2^{e-2}$ residues of that form modulo $2^e$, so its powers are all of them. An arbitrary odd class is either one or three modulo four; negating in the second case makes it one modulo four. Consequently every unit has a unique representation
\[
 (-1)^\varepsilon5^j,\qquad
 \varepsilon\in\{0,1\},\quad j\bmod 2^{e-2}.
\]
Uniqueness follows because $-1$ is not one modulo four, so it cannot lie in the subgroup generated by five. This proves $U(2^e)\cong C_2\times C_{2^{e-2}}$, with its exceptional small levels already treated.

At $e=3$ this is a product of two order-two groups, not a cyclic group of order four. At higher levels the same separate sign choice persists. The exponent is smaller than the group size even though it is attained by five. Thus the equality of odd-prime-power exponent and totient must not be transferred to powers of two by analogy.

These proofs use exact integer valuations and group orders only. They do not require logarithms, analytic limits, or a choice of primitive root. The next chapter's lifting calculation will use the same binomial habit on polynomial values rather than on a unit raised to the prime power.

<a id="section-457-12-nonunit-powers-have-a-threshold-before-their-periodi"></a>
## 12. Nonunit powers have a threshold before their periodic part {#section-459-12-nonunit-powers-have-a-threshold-before-their-periodi}

For a nonunit, exponent reduction is not simply an incorrect theorem with an inconvenient exception; a different structure is present. Locally write $a=p^t u$ with $p\nmid u$ and $t>0$. Then
\[
 a^k=p^{tk}u^k.
\]
Modulo $p^e$ the value is zero once $tk\ge e$. Before that threshold, the unit factor need only be calculated modulo $p^{e-tk}$, because multiplying by $p^{tk}$ makes higher precision irrelevant. One may reduce the exponent in that unit calculation under a valid unit theorem, but the valuation factor and its threshold must remain unchanged.

For a general modulus, separate the prime-power factors at primes dividing the base from those coprime to it. After
\[
 k\ge\max_{p^e\parallel n,\ p\mid a}\lceil e/v_p(a)\rceil,
\]
the first group of local components is zero. The other components are unit powers and repeat with a period dividing the lcm of their exponents. CRT then gives eventual periodicity. It does not generally give periodicity starting at exponent zero.

For instance modulo twelve the powers of two become $4,8,4,8,\ldots$ from exponent two onward. Modulo four they are then zero, while modulo three they alternate. Yet replacing exponent two by zero modulo $\lambda(12)=2$ would predict one instead of four. The residue two has no multiplicative order in $U(12)$, because it is not a member of that group.

The zero base is best treated directly: positive powers are zero, whereas the zeroth power in the usual polynomial convention is one. A valuation formula with $v_p(0)$ need not be forced into an ordinary finite integer quotient. Likewise, a negative exponent requires a modular inverse and is undefined for a nonunit. Stating the exponent range prevents these boundary cases from being hidden inside a reduction formula.

The same domain issue governs primality tests. If $1<\gcd(a,n)<n$, that proper factor proves compositeness. If $\gcd(a,n)=n$, the base is a multiple of the candidate and gives no such proper-factor certificate; even a prime candidate can have this base. Fermat's unit congruence belongs only after its coprimality premise is checked. A failed necessary condition for units is not a compositeness proof when the supposed unit was never in the domain.

This separation is useful in computation and in proofs: first track the valuation-controlled components, then apply finite-group periodicity to the actual units, and finally reconstruct. An exact modular answer does not by itself justify the route used to obtain it. The local decomposition supplies that missing explanation.

<a id="section-479-13-korselt-s-criterion-is-a-complete-structural-equival"></a>
## 13. Korselt's criterion is a complete structural equivalence {#section-481-13-korselt-s-criterion-is-a-complete-structural-equival}

Let $n>1$ be composite. It is Carmichael exactly when it is squarefree and $p-1\mid n-1$ for every prime divisor $p$. We prove both directions with the exponent results already established, rather than appeal to a later primitive-root theorem.

Suppose every unit modulo $n$ has $(n-1)$st power one. If $p^e\parallel n$ with $e\ge2$, CRT supplies a unit that is $1+p^{e-1}$ modulo $p^e$ and one modulo every other prime-power factor. Its local order is exactly $p$: for $0<j<p$,
\[
 (1+p^{e-1})^j\equiv1+jp^{e-1}\not\equiv1\pmod{p^e},
\]
while the $p$th power is one. All higher binomial terms are divisible by $p^e$, including when $p=e=2$. Hence this global unit has order $p$, forcing $p\mid n-1$. But $p\mid n$, a contradiction. Thus no square factor can divide $n$.

For each prime divisor $p$, reduction from $U(n)$ onto $U(p)$ is surjective by CRT: prescribe any nonzero class at $p$ and one at all other prime factors. Every nonzero class modulo $p$ therefore has $(n-1)$st power one. Its universal exponent is $p-1$, by the field-root argument in Section 10 (the case $p=2$ is the trivial exponent one). Consequently $p-1\mid n-1$. This proves necessity without inferring a universal property from one tested base.

Conversely, suppose $n$ is squarefree and the divisibility conditions hold. If $a$ is coprime to $n$, Fermat gives $a^{p-1}=1$ at every prime divisor. Raising to the integer multiple $(n-1)/(p-1)$ gives $a^{n-1}=1$ at every local factor. Since those primes are distinct, CRT implies the congruence modulo $n$. Compositeness was part of the premise, so this proves that $n$ is Carmichael.

There is an equivalent all-residue form:
\[
 a^n\equiv a\pmod n\quad\hbox{for every integer }a.
\]
For a Carmichael number, prove it prime by prime. A residue divisible by $p$ gives zero on both sides; otherwise use the established unit congruence and multiply by $a$. Squarefreeness lets CRT combine these statements. Conversely, if the displayed identity holds for every $a$, multiply by the inverse of each unit to obtain its Fermat congruence. The composite hypothesis still distinguishes a Carmichael number from an actual prime.

This all-residue conclusion does not extend Euler's unit theorem to arbitrary composite moduli. It uses the special squarefree and divisibility structure just proved. Nor does the criterion remove the difficulty of factoring a large integer: it is a short verification once a prime factorization is supplied. Every logical quantifier is still visible: all prime factors in the criterion and all coprime bases in the definition.

<a id="section-501-14-structural-checks-distinguish-a-certificate-from-a-s"></a>
## 14. Structural checks distinguish a certificate from a successful test {#section-503-14-structural-checks-distinguish-a-certificate-from-a-s}

Korselt's criterion immediately imposes further necessary structure. A Carmichael number is odd. If it were even, squarefreeness and compositeness would give an odd prime factor $q$; the even number $q-1$ could not divide the odd number $n-1$. It also has at least three distinct prime factors. If $n=pq$ with $p>q$, the condition $p-1\mid pq-1$ would imply $p-1\mid q-1$, impossible because $0<q-1<p-1$.

These are necessary conditions, not a replacement for the full criterion. The odd squarefree integer $105=3\cdot5\cdot7$ has three prime factors but is not Carmichael: six does not divide $104$. A concrete failing unit is two, because modulo seven it has order three and $104$ is not divisible by three. Thus $2^{104}\equiv4\ne1\pmod7$, which already rules out the universal congruence modulo $105$.

For a positive example distinct from the original exercise, take $1729=7\cdot13\cdot19$. Its factors are distinct primes, and
\[
 \lambda(1729)=\operatorname{lcm}(6,12,18)=36,\qquad
 1728=48\cdot36.
\]
These exact identities certify the unit congruence for every base, without testing each of the many units individually. The prime assertions are small trial-division certificates, not deductions from the Fermat congruence being proved.

This contrast explains the strengths of different evidence. A single failed unit congruence refutes primality. A single successful congruence verifies one arithmetic property. A factorization together with the complete criterion proves a universal statement about all units and, in this special case, the all-residue identity. None of these exact finite claims establishes safe cryptographic parameters or an observed implementation's correctness. Those require their own stated inputs and verification duties.

<a id="section-516-15-coprime-orders-explain-when-an-exponent-is-attained"></a>
## 15. Coprime orders explain when an exponent is attained {#section-518-15-coprime-orders-explain-when-an-exponent-is-attained}

The least common multiple definition of exponent did not promise one element of that order. For a finite abelian group, however, such an element can be constructed. The key step is that commuting elements of coprime orders $r,s$ have product of order $rs$.

The $rs$th power of the product is one, giving an upper bound. If $(ab)^k=1$, commutativity gives $a^k=b^{-k}$. This element lies in both cyclic subgroups. Its order divides both $r$ and $s$, so coprimality forces it to be the identity. Thus $r\mid k$ and $s\mid k$, whence $rs\mid k$. These two divisibilities prove the exact product order. Without commutativity the first expansion into $a^kb^k$ is unavailable.

Let $M$ be the exponent of a finite abelian group. For each prime power $q^e$ appearing in $M$, some element has order divisible by $q^e$; otherwise the lcm would have a smaller $q$-exponent. If its order is $q^ec$ with $q\nmid c$, raising that element to $c$ gives order exactly $q^e$, by the order-of-a-power formula. Multiply the extracted elements for the distinct primes. Repeated application of the coprime-order lemma gives an element of order $M$.

For $U(n)$ multiplication is commutative, so the universal exponent is always attained. The group is cyclic precisely when this attained exponent equals its size $\varphi(n)$. When the exponent is smaller, attaining it still does not enumerate all units. For example the units modulo eight have exponent two, attained by three, but there are four units. This distinguishes the two assertions that a longest cycle exists and that one cycle covers the whole group.

As an exact certificate, modulo 45 the unit 37 has order four and 31 has order three. Indeed $37^2\equiv19$, $37^4\equiv1$, while $31^2\equiv16$ and $31^3\equiv1$. Their product is $22\bmod45$, and the lemma proves its order is twelve, attaining $\lambda(45)$. The coprime factors four and three identify why the combined order is the product, not an unsupported guess from a few powers.

This argument is the interface used by the next cyclicity chapter. The exponent bounds come first, the commuting-product construction supplies an element attaining them, and the field root bound can then force exponent equal to group size for a finite multiplicative subgroup of a field. No primitive-root existence is assumed in the exponent calculation itself.

<a id="section-530-exercises-optional-exponent-extensions"></a>
## 16. Wilson's theorem and the inverse-pair mechanism {#section-572-16-wilson-s-theorem-and-the-inverse-pair-mechanism}

**Theorem 5.3 (Wilson).** An integer $n\ge2$ is prime if and only if

$$(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod n.$$

For an odd prime $p$, each nonzero residue has a unique inverse. Inversion is an involution, so it partitions the nonzero residues into singleton fixed points and unordered pairs. A fixed point satisfies $a^2=1$ in the field; factoring $(a-1)(a+1)=0$ shows that the only fixed points are $1$ and $-1$. Each remaining pair contributes one to the product. The product of all nonzero residues is therefore $-1$. For $p=2$ the product is one, which equals minus one modulo two, so the exceptional small prime also satisfies the congruence.

Conversely, suppose the congruence holds and $n$ is composite. It has a divisor $d$ with $1<d<n$. That divisor occurs among the factors of $(n-1)!$, so reduction modulo $d$ makes the factorial zero. But the proposed congruence would make it minus one modulo $d$, a contradiction. This proves the converse without assuming that composite residue rings have the prime-field inverse structure. The proof also makes clear that the theorem concerns the entire factorial; checking a few products or a few unit inverses cannot certify it.

In fact $(n-1)!\equiv0\pmod n$ for every composite $n>4$. Write $n=ab$ with $2\le a\le b<n$. If $a<b$, both distinct integers occur in the factorial and their product is $n$. If $a=b$, then $a\ge3$ and the distinct factors $a$ and $2a$ both occur, since $2a\le a^2-1$. Their product is $2n$, so again $n$ divides the factorial. The sole missing composite is four, where $3!=6\equiv2\pmod4$. This strengthened observation is useful for hand calculations but is not needed for Wilson's converse.

<a id="section-582-worked-wilson-certificate-seven"></a>
### Worked Wilson certificate: seven {#section-584-worked-wilson-certificate-seven}

The nonzero residues modulo seven split into the fixed points $1,6$ and the inverse pairs $(2,4)$ and $(3,5)$. The pair products are one modulo seven, while the fixed-point product is six. Hence $6!\equiv6=-1\pmod7$. The displayed pairs explain the result before the factorial is multiplied; it is a structural certificate of the same equality as $720\equiv6\pmod7$.

<a id="section-586-worked-half-factorial-certificate-thirteen"></a>
### Worked half-factorial certificate: thirteen {#section-588-worked-half-factorial-certificate-thirteen}

Pair the factors $a$ and $p-a$ for $1\le a\le(p-1)/2$. With $h=(p-1)/2$, Wilson gives

$$(-1)^h(h!)^2\equiv-1\pmod p,$$

so $(h!)^2\equiv(-1)^{h+1}$. If $p\equiv1\pmod4$, the integer $h$ is even and this explicitly constructs a square root of minus one modulo $p$. At thirteen, $6!=720\equiv5$ and $5^2=25\equiv-1$. This constructs a modular root, which the Gaussian gcd argument in the sums-of-squares chapter turns into an integer representation of thirteen. The modular root alone is not the assertion $13=5^2+1$.

<a id="section-594-worked-composite-boundary-nine-and-four"></a>
### Worked composite boundary: nine and four {#section-596-worked-composite-boundary-nine-and-four}

For nine, the two distinct factors three and six already give a multiple of nine in $8!$, so the factorial is zero modulo nine. For four, $3!$ is two modulo four and still fails Wilson's congruence. A proposed proof that every composite factorial vanishes must handle four separately, although the primality characterization has no exception there.

Wilson is an exact characterization with an elementary proof, not an efficient general-purpose algorithm. The input length is approximately $\log_2 n$, whereas directly accumulating the factorial needs about $n$ multiplications. The later [Primality Tests and Certificates](primality-tests-certificates.md) chapter uses the characterization to distinguish mathematical equivalence from computational economy; recursive order certificates prove primality from much shorter structured data when that data is available.

## Summary

<a id="section-368-chapter-summary"></a>
Units form a finite multiplicative group. Element order divides every exponent returning that element to one, and Lagrange makes it divide $\varphi(n)$. Euler and Fermat are therefore group theorems with explicit unit hypotheses. $\lambda(n)$ is the sharp universal exponent, while probable-prime congruences provide compositeness witnesses on failure but not primality proofs on success.

### 1. Units modulo eighteen {#section-87-1-units-modulo-eighteen}

List $U(18)$ and compute $\varphi(18)$.

**Solution.** The classes coprime to $18$ are $1,5,7,11,13,17$; hence $\varphi(18)=6$, agreeing with $18(1-1/2)(1-1/3)=6$.

Compute $\varphi(7^{4})$.

**Hint 1.** The only nonunits modulo a prime power are multiples of that prime.

**Hint 2.** Subtract the number of multiples of seven from the total number of residues.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $\varphi(7^{4})=7^{4}-7^{3}=2401-343=2058$.

</details>

<a id="section-113-3-multiplicativity"></a>
### 3. Multiplicativity {#section-115-3-multiplicativity}

Compute $\varphi(360)$.

**Hint 1.** Factor 360 into prime powers.

**Hint 2.** Use the totient product or CRT multiplicativity.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $360=2^{3}*3^{2}*5$, so $\varphi(360)=360(1/2)(2/3)(4/5)=96$.

</details>

<a id="section-127-4-an-element-order"></a>
### 4. An element order {#section-129-4-an-element-order}

Find $\operatorname{ord}_{11}(3)$.

**Hint 1.** List successive powers until the first return to one.

**Hint 2.** No smaller positive exponent may be skipped when claiming the order.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $3^{1}=3$, $3^{2}=9$, $3^{3}=27\equiv 5$, $3^{4}\equiv 4$, $3^{5}\equiv 1$. No earlier power is one, so the order is $5$.

</details>

<a id="section-141-5-order-divides-exponent"></a>
### 5. Order divides exponent {#section-143-5-order-divides-exponent}

If $\operatorname{ord}_{n}(a)=r$, prove $\operatorname{ord}_{n}(a^k)=r/\gcd(r,k)$.

**Hint 1.** A power of the new element is one exactly when the old order divides the product of the exponents.

**Hint 2.** Divide both integers by their gcd to identify the least positive new exponent.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $(a^k)^t=1$ exactly when $r\mid kt$. Dividing by $\gcd(r,k)$ shows the least positive such $t$ is $r/\gcd(r,k)$.

</details>

<a id="section-155-6-euler-reduction"></a>
### 6. Euler reduction {#section-157-6-euler-reduction}

Find $7^{222} \bmod 20$.

**Hint 1.** Check that seven is a unit modulo twenty.

**Hint 2.** Reduce the exponent using its order or Euler, then square exactly.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $\varphi(20)=8$, and $222\equiv 6 \bmod 8$. Compute $7^{2}\equiv 9$, $7^{4}\equiv 1$, so $7^{6}\equiv 9 \bmod 20$.

</details>

<a id="section-169-7-sharper-reduction"></a>
### 7. Sharper reduction {#section-171-7-sharper-reduction}

Find $2^{2026} \bmod 15$.

**Hint 1.** The order of two modulo fifteen is four.

**Hint 2.** Reduce 2026 modulo that order.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** The order is $4$; $2026\equiv 2 \bmod 4$, so the result is $4$.

</details>

<a id="section-183-8-fermat-all-residue-form"></a>
### 8. Fermat all-residue form {#section-185-8-fermat-all-residue-form}

Prove $a^p\equiv a \bmod p$ for every integer $a$ and prime $p$.

**Hint 1.** Separate the case where the prime divides the input.

**Hint 2.** In the unit case multiply Fermat's congruence by the base.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** If $p\mid a$, both sides are zero modulo $p$. Otherwise multiply Fermat's $a^{p-1}\equiv 1$ by $a$.

</details>

<a id="section-197-9-unit-hypothesis-failure"></a>
### 9. Unit hypothesis failure {#section-199-9-unit-hypothesis-failure}

Show Euler's conclusion can fail for a nonunit.

**Hint 1.** Try a base divisible by a prime factor of the modulus.

**Hint 2.** Modulo four, the square of two is zero rather than one.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** Modulo $8$, $\varphi(8)=4$, but $2^{4}=16\equiv 0$, not $1$.

</details>

<a id="section-211-10-last-digit"></a>
### 10. Last digit {#section-213-10-last-digit}

Find the last decimal digit of $17^{1001}$.

**Hint 1.** Replace the base by its last digit.

**Hint 2.** The powers of seven modulo ten have period four.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** Modulo $10$, $17\equiv 7$ and powers cycle with order $4$. Since $1001\equiv 1 \bmod 4$, the last digit is $7$.

</details>

<a id="section-225-11-last-two-digits-by-crt"></a>
### 11. Last two digits by CRT {#section-227-11-last-two-digits-by-crt}

Find $3^{100} \bmod 100$.

**Hint 1.** Use CRT at four and twenty-five.

**Hint 2.** At twenty-five the exponent is a multiple of the unit exponent.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** Modulo $4$ the value is $1$. Modulo $25$, Euler gives exponent $20$, so it is $1$. The unique class modulo $100$ is $1$.

</details>

<a id="section-239-12-carmichael-exponent"></a>
### 12. Carmichael exponent {#section-241-12-carmichael-exponent}

Compute $\lambda(45)$.

**Hint 1.** Use the prime powers nine and five.

**Hint 2.** Their unit exponents combine by least common multiple.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $45=5*9$, so $\lambda(45)=\operatorname{lcm}(\lambda(5),\lambda(9))=\operatorname{lcm}(4,6)=12$.

</details>

<a id="section-253-13-euler-versus-carmichael"></a>
### 13. Euler versus Carmichael {#section-255-13-euler-versus-carmichael}

Explain why $a^{12}\equiv 1 \bmod 45$ for every $a$ coprime to $45$, although $\varphi(45)=24$.

**Hint 1.** A universal exponent can be smaller than the unit-group size.

**Hint 2.** The local exponents at nine and five are six and four.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** The universal exponent is $\lambda(45)=12$; every element order divides it. Euler's exponent $24$ is another, nonminimal common multiple.

</details>

<a id="section-267-14-base-two-pseudoprime"></a>
### 14. Base-two pseudoprime {#section-269-14-base-two-pseudoprime}

Verify $341$ passes the base-two Fermat test.

**Hint 1.** Compute the tenth power of two modulo 341.

**Hint 2.** Ten divides the exponent 340.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $2^{10}=1024=3*341+1$, so $2^{340}=(2^{10})^{34}\equiv 1$, while $341=11*31$ is composite.

</details>

<a id="section-281-15-a-fermat-witness"></a>
### 15. A Fermat witness {#section-283-15-a-fermat-witness}

Show $2$ witnesses that $15$ is composite.

**Hint 1.** Compute the fourteenth power modulo fifteen.

**Hint 2.** Fermat's necessary unit congruence fails.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** If $15$ were prime, $2^{14}\equiv 1$. But $2^{4}\equiv 1 \bmod 15$, so $2^{14}\equiv 2^{2}\equiv 4$, not one.

</details>

<a id="section-295-16-verify-a-carmichael-example"></a>
### 16. Verify a Carmichael example {#section-297-16-verify-a-carmichael-example}

Using Korselt's criterion, verify $561$ is Carmichael.

**Hint 1.** Factor 561 completely into distinct primes.

**Hint 2.** Check each prime predecessor divides 560.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** $561=3*11*17$ is squarefree. The numbers $2,10,16$ all divide $560$. Therefore every unit $a$ satisfies $a^{560}\equiv 1 \bmod 561$.

</details>

<a id="section-309-17-crt-order"></a>
### 17. CRT order {#section-311-17-crt-order}

If $\gcd(m,n)=1$, prove $\operatorname{ord}_{mn}(a)=\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{ord}_{m}(a),\operatorname{ord}_{n}(a))$ for $a$ coprime to $mn$.

**Hint 1.** A power is one modulo the product exactly when it is one at both coprime moduli.

**Hint 2.** Each local order must divide the exponent, so their lcm is the least possibility.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** A power is one modulo $mn$ exactly when it is one modulo both factors. Thus its exponent must be a common multiple of the two local orders, and the least is their lcm.

</details>

<a id="section-323-18-claim-strength-audit"></a>
### 18. Claim-strength audit {#section-325-18-claim-strength-audit}

A program reports $2^{n-1}\equiv 1 \bmod n$. What may it conclude?

**Hint 1.** A passing congruence is a necessary condition at that base.

**Hint 2.** The explicit base-two pseudoprime shows why a prime verdict is stronger.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** It may conclude that $n$ passed one necessary primality condition for base two, assuming the computation was exact. It may not conclude primality: base-two pseudoprimes such as $341$ exist. A proof needs a certificate or a theorem covering additional tests.

</details>

<a id="section-337-19-order-two-elements"></a>
### 19. Order-two elements {#section-339-19-order-two-elements}

Prove an element of order two is its own inverse.

**Hint 1.** Order two means the square is the identity.

**Hint 2.** Multiply by the inverse or use uniqueness of the inverse.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** Order two means $a^{2}=1$ and $a\ne 1$. The equation $aa=1$ says exactly that $a$ is an inverse of itself.

</details>

<a id="section-351-20-exponent-under-a-homomorphism"></a>
### 20. Exponent under a homomorphism {#section-353-20-exponent-under-a-homomorphism}

If a group homomorphism sends $a$ to $b$, prove $\operatorname{ord}(b)$ divides $\operatorname{ord}(a)$ when $a$ has finite order.

**Hint 1.** Apply the homomorphism to the finite-order equation.

**Hint 2.** The order of the image divides every positive exponent returning it to the identity.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** If $a^r=1$, then $b^r=\varphi(a)^r=\varphi(a^r)=1$. The order of $b$ divides every exponent returning it to one, hence divides $r=\operatorname{ord}(a)$.

</details>

<a id="section-602-exercises-wilson-and-factorial-structure"></a>
### 23. ENT05-W01. Identify every inverse pair {#section-604-ent05-w01-identify-every-inverse-pair}

Partition the nonzero residues modulo eleven into inverse pairs and fixed points, and obtain Wilson's congruence.

**Hint 1.** Inversion fixes only one and ten.

**Hint 2.** Find the inverse of each unused residue in increasing order.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The pairs are $(2,6),(3,4),(5,9),(7,8)$, with fixed points one and ten. Each pair multiplies to one modulo eleven, and the fixed points multiply to ten. These entries exhaust the ten nonzero residues without repetition, giving $10!\equiv10=-1\pmod{11}$. The partition, rather than a partial list of successful inverse equations, is what supplies the whole product.

</details>

<a id="section-616-ent05-w02-why-the-field-matters"></a>
### 24. ENT05-W02. Why the field matters {#section-618-ent05-w02-why-the-field-matters}

Find all solutions of $a^2=1\pmod8$ and explain the failure of the prime-field fixed-point argument.

**Hint 1.** Consider all four units modulo eight.

**Hint 2.** A product equal to zero in a composite quotient need not have a zero factor.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The four residues $1,3,5,7$ all square to one. For example $(3-1)(3+1)=2\cdot4=0$ modulo eight although neither factor is zero. The step from $(a-1)(a+1)=0$ to $a=\pm1$ needs a field, or at least an integral domain. Inversion still exists on the unit group, but it has more fixed points, and the product of units is a different object from the full factorial.

</details>

<a id="section-630-ent05-w03-a-converse-with-only-one-divisor"></a>
### 25. ENT05-W03. A converse with only one divisor {#section-632-ent05-w03-a-converse-with-only-one-divisor}

Suppose $(n-1)!+1$ is divisible by $n\ge2$. Prove $n$ is prime without using inverse pairs.

**Hint 1.** A proper divisor would be among the factorial factors.

**Hint 2.** Reduce the proposed divisibility modulo that divisor.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If $n$ were composite, choose $d$ with $1<d<n$ and $d\mid n$. The integer $d$ divides $(n-1)!$ and, from $n\mid((n-1)!+1)$, also divides that factorial plus one. Subtraction forces $d\mid1$, contradiction. Thus no proper nontrivial divisor exists. The proof does not assume any unproved statement about units modulo a composite candidate.

</details>

<a id="section-644-ent05-w04-the-exceptional-composite"></a>
### 26. ENT05-W04. The exceptional composite {#section-646-ent05-w04-the-exceptional-composite}

Prove the strengthened composite-factorial statement and identify its exception.

**Hint 1.** If $n=ab$ with $a<b$, select those two factors.

**Hint 2.** For $n=a^2$ with $a\ge3$, use $a$ and $2a$ instead of counting $a$ twice.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

For distinct proper factors, their product divides the factorial because each occurs once. For a square with $a\ge3$, the distinct factors $a,2a$ lie between one and $a^2-1$ and their product is a multiple of $n$. Consequently the factorial vanishes modulo $n$ in both cases. At $n=4$ the square method has no distinct second factor below $n$, and direct calculation gives $3!\equiv2$. These cover all composite integers at least four.

</details>

<a id="section-658-ent05-w05-a-square-root-from-a-factorial"></a>
### 27. ENT05-W05. A square root from a factorial {#section-660-ent05-w05-a-square-root-from-a-factorial}

Use Wilson to show that if prime $p\equiv1\pmod4$, then $((p-1)/2)!$ is a square root of minus one modulo $p$.

**Hint 1.** Pair $a$ with $p-a$ across the two halves of the factorial.

**Hint 2.** The half-length is even in this residue class.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Writing $h=(p-1)/2$ gives $(p-1)!\equiv(-1)^h(h!)^2$. Wilson makes this minus one, while even $h$ makes the sign positive. Therefore $(h!)^2\equiv-1$. This proves existence and supplies one explicit integer representative. Its negative is the other root; field factorization shows there are exactly two distinct roots because $p$ is odd.

</details>

<a id="section-672-ent05-w06-the-other-half-factorial-sign"></a>
### 28. ENT05-W06. The other half-factorial sign {#section-674-ent05-w06-the-other-half-factorial-sign}

Determine $(h!)^2\bmod p$ when $p\equiv3\pmod4$, and identify $h!$ up to sign.

**Hint 1.** Now $h$ is odd.

**Hint 2.** A square equal to one in the field has only two roots.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The same pairing gives $-(h!)^2\equiv-1$, so $(h!)^2\equiv1$. Hence $h!\equiv1$ or $-1$ modulo $p$. Wilson alone does not specify which sign occurs. For seven, $3!=6\equiv-1$, whereas for eleven, $5!=120\equiv-1$ also; examples do not add a universal sign theorem to the argument.

</details>

<a id="section-686-ent05-w07-a-factorial-missing-two-factors"></a>
### 29. ENT05-W07. A factorial missing two factors {#section-688-ent05-w07-a-factorial-missing-two-factors}

For prime $p\ge3$, show $(p-2)!\equiv1\pmod p$.

**Hint 1.** Separate the final factor of $(p-1)!$.

**Hint 2.** That factor is minus one and is a unit.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Wilson gives $(p-1)(p-2)!\equiv-1$. Since $p-1\equiv-1$, multiplying by minus one yields $(p-2)!\equiv1$. This cancellation is legal because minus one is its own inverse. The restriction $p\ge3$ keeps the factorial index at least one; with the convention $0!=1$, the conclusion also agrees at two.

</details>

<a id="section-700-ent05-w08-binomial-congruence"></a>
### 30. ENT05-W08. Binomial congruence {#section-702-ent05-w08-binomial-congruence}

For prime $p$ and $0\le k<p$, prove $\binom{p-1}{k}\equiv(-1)^k\pmod p$.

**Hint 1.** Write the numerator as $(p-1)(p-2)\cdots(p-k)$.

**Hint 2.** Every denominator factor in $k!$ is a unit modulo $p$.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

For positive $k$, reduction of the numerator gives $(-1)(-2)\cdots(-k)=(-1)^kk!$. The residue of $k!$ is nonzero and invertible because all its factors are below $p$. Cancellation yields the stated sign. At $k=0$ both sides are one. This is an instance of the same finite-field product discipline; replacing the prime by a composite may make the denominator noninvertible.

</details>

<a id="section-714-ent05-w09-prime-divisors-of-a-factorial-plus-one"></a>
### 31. ENT05-W09. Prime divisors of a factorial plus one {#section-716-ent05-w09-prime-divisors-of-a-factorial-plus-one}

If a prime $r$ divides $m!+1$ for $m\ge1$, prove $r>m$. Does this make $m!+1$ prime?

**Hint 1.** Every prime at most $m$ divides the factorial.

**Hint 2.** A restriction on prime divisors is not a count of prime divisors.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

If $r\le m$, then $r$ divides both $m!$ and $m!+1$, impossible. Thus all prime divisors exceed $m$. It does not follow that there is only one: $5!+1=121=11^2$ is composite with its sole prime divisor eleven greater than five. The argument is a divisor obstruction, while Wilson characterizes a different expression tied to the candidate itself.

</details>

<a id="section-728-ent05-w10-a-factorial-inverse"></a>
### 32. ENT05-W10. A factorial inverse {#section-730-ent05-w10-a-factorial-inverse}

For $1\le k<p$ with $p$ prime, prove $(p-k)!\,(k-1)!\equiv(-1)^k\pmod p$.

**Hint 1.** Separate the last $k-1$ factors of $(p-1)!$.

**Hint 2.** Their product is $(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!$.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Wilson gives $-1\equiv(p-k)!\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(p-j)\equiv(p-k)!(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!$. Multiplying by the same sign gives the result. This formula determines an inverse of one factorial through the other and a sign. At $k=1$ the empty product is one and the statement is exactly Wilson's congruence.

</details>

<a id="section-742-ent05-w11-product-over-a-finite-group"></a>
### 33. ENT05-W11. Product over a finite group {#section-744-ent05-w11-product-over-a-finite-group}

For a finite abelian group, prove that the product of all elements equals the product of its elements of order dividing two. Explain why the Wilson proof is a special case.

**Hint 1.** Pair each nonfixed element with its inverse.

**Hint 2.** Commutativity permits regrouping the product.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Inversion partitions the underlying set into pairs with product the identity and fixed points satisfying $g^2=1$. Since the group is abelian, these pieces can be regrouped without changing the product, leaving exactly the product of fixed points. For the multiplicative group of an odd prime field, the only fixed points are one and minus one, so the product is minus one. In a nonabelian group an unordered product is not intrinsically defined, which is why that generalization needs its commutativity hypothesis.

</details>

<a id="section-756-ent05-w12-equivalence-and-cost"></a>
### 34. ENT05-W12. Equivalence and cost {#section-758-ent05-w12-equivalence-and-cost}

Explain why Wilson gives a deterministic primality decision but direct factorial accumulation does not give a polynomial-time algorithm in the bit length of the candidate.

**Hint 1.** Distinguish the value of $n$ from the length of its binary representation.

**Hint 2.** Count even just the number of modular multiplications.

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Compute the factorial modulo $n$ and compare it with minus one; Wilson makes the answer exact for every $n\ge2$. A direct loop uses approximately $n$ modular multiplications. If the input has $b$ bits, $n$ can be nearly $2^b$, so that count is exponential in $b$. Accounting for the cost of each multiplication only increases it. This conclusion concerns the stated direct method, and does not claim that every primality algorithm or every possible factorial algorithm has that cost.

</details>

## Solutions: optional exponent extensions {#section-550-solutions-optional-exponent-extensions}

<a id="section-550-ntp-05-e21-separate-an-exponent-from-a-nonunit-threshol"></a>
### NTP-05-E21: separate an exponent from a nonunit threshold — solution {#section-552-ntp-05-e21-separate-an-exponent-from-a-nonunit-threshol}

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Since $360=8\cdot9\cdot5$, the local exponents are $2,6,4$, giving $\lambda(360)=12$. The base six is not a unit. For $k\ge3$ its power is zero modulo eight and nine, hence modulo 72, while it is one modulo five. Among $0,72,144,216,288$, the unique residue one modulo five is 216. Thus $6^k\equiv216\pmod{360}$ for all $k\ge3$. The exponent twelve controls unit components, not the valuation threshold of this base.

</details>

<a id="section-558-ntp-05-e22-certify-the-two-adic-exponent-sharply-soluti"></a>
### NTP-05-E22: certify the two-adic exponent sharply — solution {#section-560-ntp-05-e22-certify-the-two-adic-exponent-sharply-soluti}

<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The exact valuation formula gives $v_2(5^{2^j}-1)=j+2$. At $j=3$ the power is one modulo 32, while at $j=2$ it is not; its order is therefore eight. Every odd square is one modulo 8, and two further squarings make its eighth power one modulo 32. Hence the universal exponent is eight. But $|U(32)|=16$, so no element of order at most eight can enumerate all units; the group is not cyclic.

</details>
