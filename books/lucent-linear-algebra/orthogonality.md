# 10. Inner products, orthogonality, and projection

**Prerequisites:** vectors, subspaces, bases, and matrix multiplication. **Learning goals:** use inner products to measure angle; construct orthogonal complements; derive orthogonal projections; prove the closest-point property. Work through the three-dimensional example by hand before using the visual laboratories.

## From algebra to measurement

For vectors x,y in ℝⁿ, the standard inner product is x·y=Σ xᵢyᵢ. It is symmetric, linear in each argument, and positive definite: x·x≥0, with equality only for x=0. These properties give the length ‖x‖=√(x·x) and distance ‖x−y‖. A more general real inner product must satisfy the same three properties; changing the inner product changes what perpendicular and closest mean.

For nonzero vectors, their angle θ satisfies cos θ=(x·y)/(‖x‖‖y‖). This formula requires the quotient to lie in [−1,1], which is guaranteed by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality |x·y|≤‖x‖‖y‖. Zero vectors are orthogonal to every vector, but have no defined angle with another vector.

Here is a proof of the inequality that also introduces projection. If y≠0, the square of the length of x−ty is nonnegative for every real t. Completing the square gives

‖x−ty‖² = ‖y‖²(t−(x·y)/‖y‖²)² + ‖x‖²−(x·y)²/‖y‖².

Set t=(x·y)/‖y‖². Nonnegativity now gives Cauchy–Schwarz. Equality means the remaining vector is zero, so x and y are dependent. The case y=0 follows immediately. This argument works in arbitrary finite dimension.

## Projecting onto a line

Let u≠0 and let W=span{u}. The projection of b onto W is

p = ((b·u)/(u·u))u.

The coefficient tells us how far to travel along u; it is not generally a distance because u need not be a unit vector. The residual r=b−p satisfies r·u=0, since b·u−((b·u)/(u·u))(u·u)=0. Thus b splits into a component along W and a perpendicular component.

The construction depends on the line, not the chosen generator. Replacing u by cu for nonzero c multiplies the numerator by c and denominator by c², then multiplies the final vector by c. These factors cancel. Omitting the denominator u·u would destroy this invariance.

## Worked example 1: the denominator matters

Project b=(3,1) onto the line generated by u=(1,2). We have b·u=5 and u·u=5, so p=(1,2). The residual r=(2,−1) is perpendicular to u because 2−2=0. Its length is √5.

If we mistakenly used p=(b·u)u, we would obtain (5,10), whose residual (−2,−9) has dot product −20 with u. The visual impression of landing somewhere on the line is insufficient: perpendicularity is the diagnostic condition. Normalizing first would also work: q=u/√5, and p=(b·q)q.

## Orthogonal complements and subspace projection

The orthogonal complement of W is W⊥={r:r·w=0 for every w∈W}. This is a subspace: the inner product of any linear combination of two such vectors with w is still zero. To test membership, it suffices to test against a basis, because every other w is a linear combination of basis vectors.

If q₁,…,q_k are an orthonormal basis of W, define

p = Σ(b·qᵢ)qᵢ.

Then p lies in W and (b−p)·qⱼ=b·qⱼ−b·qⱼ=0 for every j. Therefore r=b−p lies in W⊥. Every finite-dimensional subspace has an orthonormal basis; chapter 12 constructs one. Completing that basis to an orthonormal basis of ℝⁿ shows dim W⊥=n−dim W.

**Theorem (Projection).** Every b has a unique decomposition b=p+r with p∈W and r∈W⊥. Existence follows from the formula. For uniqueness, suppose b=p₁+r₁=p₂+r₂. Then p₁−p₂=r₂−r₁ lies in both W and W⊥. Its inner product with itself is zero, so it is the zero vector. Thus p₁=p₂ and r₁=r₂.

There is also a geometric consequence. For any z∈W, the vectors r and p−z are perpendicular. Pythagoras gives

‖b−z‖² = ‖r‖²+‖p−z‖².

The unique minimum occurs at z=p. Projection is therefore the closest-point problem solved exactly, not just a convenient decomposition.

## Worked example 2: projection onto a plane in ℝ³

Let W={(x,y,z):x+y+z=0}. Its normal n=(1,1,1) spans W⊥. For b=(3,0,0), the normal component is r=((b·n)/(n·n))n=(1,1,1). Consequently, the plane projection is p=b−r=(2,−1,−1). Check p∈W by summing coordinates, and check r is perpendicular to every plane vector using r·(x,y,z)=x+y+z=0.

We can recover the answer with q₁=(1,−1,0)/√2 and q₂=(1,1,−2)/√6. These are orthogonal unit vectors in W. The projection formula gives (3/√2)q₁+(3/√6)q₂=(3/2,−3/2,0)+(1/2,1/2,−1)=(2,−1,−1). A flat screen cannot show every coordinate, so this higher-dimensional verification is essential.

If Q has orthonormal columns spanning W, the projection matrix is P=QQᵀ. It obeys Pᵀ=P and P²=P. A common misconception is that P²=P alone guarantees orthogonal projection. The matrix with rows (1,1), (0,0) is idempotent but sends (0,1) to (1,0); its residual (−1,1) is not perpendicular to the target x-axis. Symmetry distinguishes orthogonal projection from this oblique projection.

## Definitions and orthogonal geometry

**Definition 10.1 (Orthogonal and orthonormal lists).** A list of nonzero vectors is orthogonal if every pair of distinct members has inner product zero. It is orthonormal if, in addition, every member has norm one. The nonzero condition matters: a list containing zero cannot be independent, even though zero is perpendicular to every vector.

**Result 10.2 (Orthogonal vectors are independent).** Every finite orthogonal list of nonzero vectors in a real inner-product space is linearly independent.

*Proof.* Suppose ∑cᵢuᵢ=0. Take the inner product with uⱼ. All terms with i≠j vanish, leaving cⱼ‖uⱼ‖²=0. Since uⱼ≠0, positive definiteness gives ‖uⱼ‖²>0, hence cⱼ=0. This works for every index j. ∎

The same calculation extracts coefficients. If b=∑cᵢuᵢ lies in the span of an orthogonal list, then cⱼ=(b·uⱼ)/(uⱼ·uⱼ). For an orthonormal list the denominator is one. This is why projections have especially simple formulas in orthonormal coordinates: each coordinate can be measured independently without solving a coupled system.

**Theorem 10.3 (Triangle inequality).** For all vectors x,y in a real inner-product space, ‖x+y‖≤‖x‖+‖y‖.

*Proof.* Cauchy–Schwarz gives x·y≤‖x‖‖y‖. Therefore ‖x+y‖²=‖x‖²+2x·y+‖y‖²≤(‖x‖+‖y‖)². Both sides being compared are nonnegative, so taking square roots preserves the inequality. The proof includes zero vectors without a separate division. ∎

This result makes the norm compatible with the intuitive statement that a direct route is no longer than a route broken into two segments. Pythagoras is more precise in the perpendicular case: the cross term vanishes exactly, rather than merely being bounded. The closest-point proof depends on that exact equality.

## Worked example 3: projection as a symmetric matrix

Let W be the plane x+y+z=0, with normal n=(1,1,1). The orthogonal projection of b onto the normal line is (nᵀb)n/3, so the projection onto W is

P=I−nnᵀ/3.

Its rows are (2/3,−1/3,−1/3), (−1/3,2/3,−1/3), and (−1/3,−1/3,2/3). Multiplying by b=(2,−1,5) gives Pb=(0,−3,3), because the coordinate sum of b is six and its normal component is (2,2,2).

The result lies in W since 0−3+3=0. The residual b−Pb=(2,2,2) is perpendicular to W: its dot product with (x,y,z)∈W is 2(x+y+z)=0. Thus the distance from b to the plane is ‖(2,2,2)‖=2√3.

Check the matrix structure without multiplying nine entries laboriously. Write N=nnᵀ/3. Symmetry is immediate from the transpose rule. Also N²=n(nᵀn)nᵀ/9=3nnᵀ/9=N. Consequently P²=(I−N)²=I−2N+N²=I−N=P, and Pᵀ=P. Repeating the projection changes nothing because the first result is already in the target plane.

For a competing plane point z=(1,−2,1), the difference Pb−z=(−1,−1,2) has squared norm six. The distance identity predicts ‖b−z‖²=12+6=18. Directly, b−z=(1,1,4) also has squared norm eighteen. The residual and in-plane displacement are perpendicular, explaining the extra distance rather than merely observing it numerically.

## A full characterization of orthogonal projection matrices

**Theorem 10.4 (Symmetric idempotents).** A real n-by-n matrix P is the orthogonal projection onto its image if and only if P²=P and Pᵀ=P.

*Proof.* Suppose first P is orthogonal projection onto W. It fixes every vector in W, so applying it to Pb gives P²b=Pb for all b, hence P²=P. To prove symmetry, write arbitrary x=Px+r and y=Py+s with r,s∈W⊥. Orthogonality gives (Px)·y=(Px)·(Py)=x·(Py). Thus xᵀPᵀy=xᵀPy for all x,y. Choosing standard basis x,y shows corresponding entries of Pᵀ and P agree.

Conversely suppose P²=P and Pᵀ=P. For any b, set p=Pb and r=b−Pb. Then p belongs to im P and Pr=Pb−P²b=0. To show r perpendicular to the image, take any w=Pz. Its inner product with r is rᵀPz=(Pr)ᵀz=0, using symmetry. Therefore b=p+r is the orthogonal decomposition relative to im P, so P is its orthogonal projection. ∎

Idempotence alone supplies a decomposition but does not force perpendicularity. If P²=P, then every b splits into Pb+(I−P)b, with the first term in im P and the second in ker P. Their intersection is zero because a vector in the image is fixed by P, whereas a kernel vector is sent to zero. Symmetry ensures that these two complementary spaces are perpendicular. This is precisely what fails in the oblique example above.

## The complement and the size of projection

**Result 10.5 (Complementary projection).** If P is orthogonal projection onto W, then I−P is orthogonal projection onto W⊥, and ker P=W⊥.

*Proof.* The decomposition b=Pb+(I−P)b already places the two terms in W and W⊥. If b∈W⊥, its W component is zero by uniqueness, so Pb=0. Conversely Pb=0 makes b its own perpendicular residual. Thus ker P=W⊥. The formula for I−P follows directly from the same unique decomposition, with the two subspaces exchanged. ∎

**Result 10.6 (Projection cannot increase distances).** For orthogonal projection P and arbitrary x,y, ‖Px−Py‖≤‖x−y‖.

*Proof.* Apply the orthogonal decomposition to b=x−y. Pythagoras gives ‖b‖²=‖Pb‖²+‖(I−P)b‖²≥‖Pb‖². Linearity gives Pb=Px−Py, and square roots yield the assertion. Equality holds exactly when x−y∈W. ∎

The equality condition concerns the displacement between the points, rather than requiring both points themselves to lie in W. Two points can have identical perpendicular components away from W, making their difference lie entirely within W. Projection then preserves their mutual distance even though it moves both points.

All these statements refer to the chosen inner product. A weighted inner product produces a different perpendicular complement and a different closest point. Its projection matrix may fail ordinary Euclidean symmetry while still being symmetric relative to the weighted measurement rule. Consequently, state the inner product before testing orthogonality or interpreting a residual as a shortest distance.

The next chapter uses this geometry when an equation Ax=b is inconsistent. Rather than inventing an exact solution, one projects b onto the attainable output space and measures the remaining residual. The closest-point theorem supplies the mathematical reason this procedure is optimal in squared Euclidean distance.

## Summary

Inner products define lengths, angles, and perpendicularity. Orthogonal projection splits a vector into a subspace component and a perpendicular residual, and Pythagoras proves the unique closest-point property. Symmetric idempotent matrices characterize Euclidean orthogonal projections, whose complements project onto perpendicular spaces and whose action cannot increase distance. Least squares applies this geometry to inconsistent linear systems.
