# 12. Gram–Schmidt and QR factorization

**Prerequisites:** inner products, orthogonal projection, independent columns, and least squares.

**Learning goals:** construct an orthonormal basis; interpret a thin QR factorization; solve least squares by back substitution; recognize rank deficiency; and distinguish exact identities from floating-point behavior.

## Coordinates become simpler in an orthonormal basis

An arbitrary basis can describe a subspace, but recovering coordinates may require solving a coupled system. In an orthonormal basis q₁,…,q_n, the coordinates of a vector v in the subspace are simply q_iᵀv. Perpendicular directions do not interfere with each other's measurements. Gram–Schmidt turns independent vectors into such a basis while preserving the subspaces generated by successive vectors.

Start with independent vectors a₁,…,a_n in ℝᵐ. Set w₁=a₁ and q₁=w₁/‖w₁‖. Having constructed q₁,…,q_(j−1), subtract the component of a_j along every previous direction:

w_j = a_j − Σ from i=1 to j−1 of (q_iᵀa_j)q_i,

then normalize: q_j=w_j/‖w_j‖.

For each k<j, taking a dot product with q_k gives q_kᵀw_j=q_kᵀa_j−q_kᵀa_j=0. The other terms vanish because the previous q vectors are orthonormal. If w_j were zero, a_j would be a combination of previous vectors, contradicting independence. Thus normalization is legitimate. Because we only subtract previous components and multiply by a nonzero scalar, span(q₁,…,q_j)=span(a₁,…,a_j) at every stage.

This is an algorithm with a proof of its invariant: the first j orthonormal vectors span exactly the same subspace as the first j inputs. It does not preserve their original lengths or pairwise angles.

## Worked example in three dimensions

Take a₁=(1,1,0) and a₂=(1,0,1). First q₁=(1,1,0)/√2. Its dot product with a₂ is 1/√2, so

w₂=(1,0,1)−(1/2,1/2,0)=(1/2,−1/2,1).

Its norm is √(3/2), giving q₂=(1,−1,2)/√6. Check q₁ᵀq₂=(1−1+0)/√12=0 and ‖q₂‖²=(1+1+4)/6=1. We have also recorded the reconstruction identities

a₁=√2 q₁,     a₂=(1/√2)q₁+√(3/2)q₂.

Collect the original vectors as columns of A, the new vectors as columns of Q, and the reconstruction coefficients in R:

```text
    [1  1]         [1/√2   1/√6]         [√2    1/√2]
A = [1  0],    Q = [1/√2  −1/√6],    R = [ 0  √(3/2)].
    [0  1]         [  0    2/√6]
```

Then A=QR. Matrix R is upper triangular because each a_j uses only q₁,…,q_j. Its diagonal entries are the positive lengths ‖w_j‖. The factorization separates orthonormal directions from coordinate scaling and mixing.

## Thin QR and a common dimensional mistake

For full-column-rank A of size m×n with m≥n, **thin QR** has Q of size m×n and R of size n×n. It satisfies QᵀQ=I_n. When m>n, it generally does **not** satisfy QQᵀ=I_m. Instead, QQᵀ projects onto col(A).

For a concrete counterexample, take Q=(1,0,0)ᵀ. Then QᵀQ is the 1×1 identity, but QQᵀ=diag(1,0,0), which removes two coordinates. In our worked example, Q has two columns spanning a plane in ℝ³, so QQᵀ cannot preserve a vector normal to that plane. A full QR factorization adds orthonormal columns to complete an ambient-space basis; thin QR keeps only the columns needed here.

## Worked example: least squares using QR

Use the three-dimensional A above and b=(0,1,1). Since Q has orthonormal columns, decompose b as QQᵀb+b_perp, with b_perp perpendicular to col(Q). Therefore

‖b−QRx‖² = ‖Qᵀb−Rx‖² + ‖b_perp‖².

We used ‖Qy‖=‖y‖, which follows from QᵀQ=I. The second term is independent of x; the first vanishes exactly when Rx=Qᵀb. This triangular system is solved from its last row upward.

Here Qᵀb=(1/√2,1/√6). The last row gives √(3/2)x₂=1/√6, hence x₂=1/3. The first row gives √2 x₁+(1/√2)(1/3)=1/√2, hence x₁=1/3. Predictions are (2/3,1/3,1/3), and the residual is (−2/3,2/3,2/3), with squared norm 4/3. Dotting this residual with either original column gives zero, independently checking the result.

## Dependence, rounding, and algorithm choice

If an input belongs to the span of its predecessors, its exact remainder is zero. Do not normalize it. Skip dependent inputs when constructing a basis; when factoring matrices for computation, rank-revealing methods such as column-pivoted QR help identify suitable independent columns. A numerical rank decision requires a tolerance related to scale and uncertainty, not an exact comparison with zero.

Classical Gram–Schmidt computes each q_iᵀa_j from the original a_j. **Modified Gram–Schmidt** updates a working remainder after every subtraction and computes the next projection from that updated remainder. In exact arithmetic the results agree. In floating-point arithmetic, modified Gram–Schmidt usually preserves orthogonality better, but nearly dependent inputs can still require reorthogonalization. Householder QR is a standard robust choice for general dense least-squares problems. None of these algorithms removes the inherent sensitivity of nearly dependent data.

## Why the positive-diagonal thin factorisation is unique

The existence proof earlier in the chapter constructs QR one column at a time. A second issue is whether different exact constructions can produce incompatible results. Without a sign convention they can: replacing qⱼ by −qⱼ and the corresponding row of R by its negative preserves A. Requiring positive diagonal entries removes precisely that freedom.

**Theorem 12.1 (Uniqueness of thin QR).** Let A∈ℝᵐ×ⁿ have independent columns in a fixed order. If A=QR=Q′R′ are thin QR factorizations with positive diagonal entries in both upper-triangular factors, then Q=Q′ and R=R′.

*Proof.* Both sets of orthonormal columns span col(A). Thus Q′=QU with U=QᵀQ′. Since QQᵀ acts as the identity on that common space, UᵀU=Q′ᵀQQᵀQ′=I. Multiplying QR=QU R′ by Qᵀ and by R′⁻¹ gives U=R R′⁻¹, which is upper triangular and has positive diagonal.

An upper-triangular orthogonal matrix has diagonal entries ±1 and all off-diagonal entries zero. Indeed its first column has only its first entry potentially nonzero, so that entry is ±1. Orthogonality with all remaining columns makes the rest of the first row zero. Repeating on the remaining square block proves the claim by induction. Positivity now forces U=I, giving both asserted equalities. ∎

This result concerns exact QR with a fixed order and sign convention. Rounding can lead two numerical implementations to slightly different entries, and pivoting changes column order. Neither fact contradicts the theorem. If A is rank deficient, the nonzero-remainder argument used for existence fails, so this particular full-column-rank uniqueness statement is inapplicable.

### Worked example 3: a rank boundary with an explicit sign

Let a₁=(1,1,0) and a₂=(1,1,ε). Their first orthonormal direction is q₁=(1,1,0)/√2. The projection coefficient of a₂ on q₁ is √2, leaving w₂=(0,0,ε). For ε≠0 we get q₂=sign(ε)(0,0,1) and

R=[[√2,√2],[0,|ε|]].

All diagonal entries are positive, including when ε is negative. Multiplying QR reconstructs a₂ because |ε|sign(ε)=ε. At ε=0 the columns coincide, w₂ vanishes and normalization is forbidden. One can still describe the rank-one column space, but a two-column thin Q with the preceding positive-diagonal R no longer exists.

This family also distinguishes exact dependence from sensitivity. Every nonzero ε gives exact rank two. Nevertheless, very small |ε| leaves one feature direction weakly constrained. A numerical decision to treat it as zero requires a scale and an uncertainty model; the algebra does not provide a universal threshold.

## What orthogonal coordinates preserve in a fit

For any w∈ℝᵐ, orthogonal decomposition gives w=QQᵀw+(I−QQᵀ)w and

‖w‖²=‖Qᵀw‖²+‖(I−QQᵀ)w‖².

In particular ‖Qᵀw‖≤‖w‖, although Qᵀ is rectangular. Applying this with w=b−QRx establishes the least-squares splitting already used in the worked calculation. It says why removing perpendicular observation information loses no information relevant to choosing the coefficients: that perpendicular part contributes a constant squared error for every x.

For fixed A=QR, changed data b+δb change the unique coefficient vector by δx=R⁻¹Qᵀδb. Orthogonal conversion cannot increase the norm of the observation perturbation, but solving with R may amplify it. QR avoids the unnecessary squaring caused by forming AᵀA; it cannot make a genuinely small independent remainder large. Conditioning and algorithmic representation remain separate considerations.

There is a useful determinant connection as well. Since AᵀA=RᵀR, determinant multiplicativity gives det(AᵀA)=det(R)². The positive diagonal of R therefore yields √det(AᵀA)=∏rⱼⱼ. For two independent columns this is their parallelogram area, the first length times the perpendicular second remainder. This gives an area for rectangular A without pretending that A itself has a determinant. In the earlier three-dimensional example, AᵀA=[[2,1],[1,2]], so the area is √3, agreeing with √2·√(3/2).

## Summary

Gram–Schmidt preserves each successive column span while constructing perpendicular unit directions. Thin QR has QᵀQ=I, while QQᵀ is generally a projection. Independent columns and a fixed positive-diagonal convention give a unique exact factorisation. Least squares then separates a fixed perpendicular residual from a triangular coefficient problem. Small remainders signal sensitivity, and a zero remainder requires rank-deficient treatment rather than division by zero. The next chapters turn from orthogonal representations to invariant directions and characteristic polynomials.
