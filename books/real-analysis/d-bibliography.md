# Bibliography and source notes

This book's exposition, examples, organization, and figures are original. The
following works are recommended for alternate treatments, historical context, and
deeper study. Publication details are supplied so a reader can locate a suitable
edition; no passage in this book is reproduced from them.

## Core real analysis

- Abbott, Stephen. *Understanding Analysis*. Springer, second edition, 2015.
  Especially approachable for counterexamples and the transition from calculus.
- Apostol, Tom M. *Mathematical Analysis*. Addison–Wesley, second edition, 1974.
  A broad classical route with metric spaces and multivariable topics.
- Pugh, Charles Chapman. *Real Mathematical Analysis*. Springer, second edition,
  2015. Geometric motivation alongside rigorous proofs.
- Rudin, Walter. *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*. McGraw–Hill, third edition,
  1976. A concise and demanding standard reference.
- Tao, Terence. *Analysis I* and *Analysis II*. Hindustan Book Agency, third
  edition, 2016. A careful construction-oriented development.

## Measure, Fourier, and functional analysis

- Axler, Sheldon. *Measure, Integration & Real Analysis*. Springer, 2020. A modern,
  legally available reference for measure and integration.
- Folland, Gerald B. *Real Analysis*. Wiley, second edition, 1999. Measure,
  functional analysis, and advanced topics.
- Kreyszig, Erwin. *Introductory Functional Analysis with Applications*. Wiley,
  1978. An application-oriented first course.
- Stein, Elias M., and Rami Shakarchi. *Fourier Analysis: An Introduction*.
  Princeton University Press, 2003. Fourier series and transforms in a larger
  analytic programme.
- Wheeden, Richard L., and Antoni Zygmund. *Measure and Integral*. CRC Press,
  second edition, 2015. Detailed classical measure theory.

## Multivariable, Sobolev, and differential-equation continuations

- Evans, Lawrence C. *Partial Differential Equations*. American Mathematical
  Society, second edition, 2010. Sobolev spaces, weak solutions, and variational
  methods at graduate level.
- Leoni, Giovanni. *A First Course in Sobolev Spaces*. American Mathematical
  Society, second edition, 2017. Detailed one- and higher-dimensional Sobolev
  theory.
- Royden, H. L., and P. M. Fitzpatrick. *Real Analysis*. Pearson, fourth edition,
  2010. Lebesgue measure, product measures, $L^p$ spaces, and differentiation.
- Teschl, Gerald. *Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems*.
  American Mathematical Society, 2012. An analysis-driven route from local
  existence to qualitative dynamics.
- Zorich, Vladimir A. *Mathematical Analysis II*. Springer, second edition, 2016.
  Multivariable differentiation, implicit functions, integration, and geometric
  applications.
- Brezis, Haim. *Functional Analysis, Sobolev Spaces and Partial Differential
  Equations*. Springer, 2011. Banach and Hilbert space structure through weak PDE
  methods.
- Conway, John B. *A Course in Functional Analysis*. Springer, second edition,
  1990. Operator theorems, weak topologies, and spectral theory.
- Durrett, Rick. *Probability: Theory and Examples*. Cambridge University Press,
  fifth edition, 2019. Conditional expectation, martingales, and convergence.
- Lieb, Elliott H., and Michael Loss. *Analysis*. American Mathematical Society,
  second edition, 2001. Inequalities, rearrangement, Fourier analysis, and
  variational methods.
- Rudin, Walter. *Functional Analysis*. McGraw–Hill, second edition, 1991.
  Concise treatment of locally convex spaces, distributions, and operators.

## Probability, transport, dynamics, harmonic analysis, and operators

- Bogachev, Vladimir I. *Measure Theory*, Volumes I and II. Springer, 2007.
  Polish spaces, kernels, regular conditional measures, and deep measure theory.
- Kallenberg, Olav. *Foundations of Modern Probability*. Springer, third edition,
  2021. Standard Borel spaces, disintegration, processes, and convergence laws.
- Villani, Cédric. *Topics in Optimal Transportation*. American Mathematical
  Society, 2003. Kantorovich duality, Wasserstein geometry, and displacement
  interpolation.
- Ambrosio, Luigi, Nicola Gigli, and Giuseppe Savaré. *Gradient Flows in Metric
  Spaces and in the Space of Probability Measures*. Birkhäuser, second edition,
  2008. Wasserstein spaces and variational evolution.
- Walters, Peter. *An Introduction to Ergodic Theory*. Springer, 1982. Measure
  preservation, recurrence, ergodic theorems, and symbolic dynamics.
- Petersen, Karl. *Ergodic Theory*. Cambridge University Press, 1983. A broad
  continuation from Koopman operators to mixing and entropy.
- Karatzas, Ioannis, and Steven E. Shreve. *Brownian Motion and Stochastic
  Calculus*. Springer, second edition, 1991. Brownian paths, stochastic
  integration, Itô calculus, and diffusion theory.
- Revuz, Daniel, and Marc Yor. *Continuous Martingales and Brownian Motion*.
  Springer, third edition, 1999. A deeper martingale route through continuous
  stochastic processes.
- Grafakos, Loukas. *Classical Fourier Analysis*. Springer, third edition, 2014.
  Interpolation, maximal operators, singular integrals, and multiplier theory.
- Stein, Elias M. *Singular Integrals and Differentiability Properties of
  Functions*. Princeton University Press, 1970. The classical
  Calderón–Zygmund framework and its geometric consequences.
- Reed, Michael, and Barry Simon. *Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics I:
  Functional Analysis*. Academic Press, revised edition, 1980. Unbounded
  operators, spectral measures, and functional calculus.
- Schmüdgen, Konrad. *Unbounded Self-adjoint Operators on Hilbert Space*.
  Springer, 2012. Domains, extensions, forms, and detailed spectral theory.

## Vector integration, Fredholm theory, and convex representation

- Atkinson, Kendall E., and Lawrence F. Shampine.
  [*Solving Fredholm Integral Equations of the Second Kind in Matlab*](https://homepage.math.uiowa.edu/~atkinson/ftp/Fie_paper.pdf),
  author manuscript, 2007. Sections 2–4 distinguish nodal data, interval
  reconstruction, convergence, conditioning, and quadrature across a kernel's
  diagonal derivative jump. Chapter 46 derives its own exact certificate for
  the two displayed Green kernels rather than importing a general error bound.

- Diestel, Joseph, and J. J. Uhl Jr. *Vector Measures*. American Mathematical
  Society, 1977. Bochner and Pettis integration, vector measures, and the
  Radon–Nikodym property.
- Hytönen, Tuomas, Jan van Neerven, Mark Veraar, and Lutz Weis. *Analysis in
  Banach Spaces, Volume I: Martingales and Littlewood–Paley Theory*. Springer,
  2016. A modern treatment of Banach-valued measurability and integration.
- Kress, Rainer. *Linear Integral Equations*. Springer, third edition, 2014.
  Compact integral operators, the Fredholm alternative, and convergent
  discretisation.
- Megginson, Robert E. *An Introduction to Banach Space Theory*. Springer,
  1998. Weak topologies, reflexivity, uniform convexity, and operator theory.
- Phelps, Robert R. *Lectures on Choquet's Theorem*. Springer, second edition,
  2001. Extreme points, barycentres, simplexes, and Choquet representation.
- Baggett, Lawrence W. [*Topological Vector Spaces and Continuous Linear
  Functionals*, Chapter III](https://spot.colorado.edu/~baggett/funcchap3.pdf).
  Theorem 3.11 states the metrizable Choquet representation, including
  measurability of the extreme set.
- Hochman, Michael. [*Lectures on fractal geometry*](https://math.huji.ac.il/~mhochman/courses/fractals-2023/notes.pdf),
  2023, Section 1.3. Distinguishes a set of full measure from the closed
  topological support; Chapter 47 makes that distinction explicit in its compact
  metric setting.

## Historical landmarks

The least-upper-bound viewpoint is associated with nineteenth-century
arithmetization of analysis, particularly Dedekind cuts and Cantor's constructions
of the real numbers. Cauchy's work systematized limits, though modern epsilon
language was refined later. Riemann's 1854 habilitation work shaped the interval
integral; Lebesgue's 1902 thesis introduced his wider integral. Fourier's early
nineteenth-century heat work promoted trigonometric expansions, and Banach's 1932
monograph consolidated complete normed spaces.

Historical attribution is more complicated than a list of named theorems suggests.
Results often evolved across several authors, and modern hypotheses or formulations
may differ from originals. The names in the text are navigation conventions, not
claims of sole invention.

## Suggested reading routes

For a second pass through elementary analysis, read Abbott or Pugh while re-proving
results without notes. For a terse challenge, use Rudin and expand every omitted
step. For measure, continue with Axler before Folland. For Fourier analysis, the
Stein–Shakarchi volume connects kernels, convergence, and the transform. For
functional analysis, pair a first text with concrete work in $C([a,b])$, $\ell^p$,
and $L^p$ rather than reading structural theorems in isolation.

For Chapters 15–22, a measure-focused continuation through Axler, Royden–
Fitzpatrick, and then Folland keeps construction ahead of abstraction. For Chapter
23, begin with Leoni before using Evans as a PDE reference. For Chapter 24, work
Teschl's examples while reproducing the contraction and Grönwall estimates by hand.
For Chapters 25–32, Brezis provides the most direct bridge across functional
analysis, variational methods, and PDE; pair it with Conway for operators, Durrett
for martingales, and Stein–Shakarchi for harmonic analysis. These routes are
suggestions, not sources silently imported into the exposition.

For Chapters 33–38, use Bogachev for Radon measures, Brezis for convex and
monotone methods, Teschl or Evans for evolution, Kallenberg for weak probability,
and a geometric-measure or nonlinear-functional-analysis text matching the
reader's intended continuation.

For Chapters 39–44, take distinct but intersecting routes. Bogachev and
Kallenberg extend the Polish-space and kernel foundations of Chapter 39.
Villani follows Chapter 40, with Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré as the route toward
gradient flows. Walters or Petersen continues Chapter 41. Karatzas–Shreve
continues Chapter 42 after the martingale material in Chapter 30. Grafakos and
Stein extend Chapter 43. Reed–Simon or Schmüdgen should be read alongside
Chapter 44 with special attention to domains and Hilbert-space conventions.
These texts use varying Fourier normalizations, inner-product conventions, and
definitions of regular conditional probability; translate those choices
explicitly rather than combining formulas verbatim.

For Chapters 45–47, use Diestel–Uhl or Hytönen–van Neerven–Veraar–Weis for
vector integration, Kress for the bridge from compact operators to numerical
integral equations, and Phelps for the representation theory beyond
Krein–Milman. Megginson supplies a common Banach-space route through all three
chapters. These sources differ in whether scalar fields are real or complex and
in their weak-measurability conventions, so check those choices before moving a
formula between treatments.
