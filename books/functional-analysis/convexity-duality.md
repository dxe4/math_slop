
# Convexity, Separation, and Duality

A convex optimization problem can be described by its points or by the affine inequalities supporting it. Hahn–Banach connects those descriptions. Separation produces continuous affine minorants, conjugation records all such minorants at once, and biconjugation recovers a proper lower-semicontinuous convex function exactly. In Hilbert space, the same inequalities characterize proximal minimizers and prove their stability.

Preparation is [Hahn–Banach](hahn-banach.md), [Hilbert projection](hilbert-spaces.md), and [weak lower semicontinuity](weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity.md). Work over real scalars. A complex space can be treated as its underlying real space, with real parts of complex pairings; the convex order comparisons are real comparisons. The final separation statement also applies to real Hausdorff locally convex spaces, whose topology has a base of convex balanced neighbourhoods of zero. That extension will be used for compact convex sets in the next chapter.

## Convex sets and gauges

A set \(C\) is convex when \((1-t)x+ty\in C\) for \(x,y\in C\), \(0\le t\le1\). A function \(f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]\) is convex when its epigraph \(\{(x,r):r\ge f(x)\}\) is convex. It is proper when it is never minus infinity and is finite somewhere, and lower semicontinuous when its epigraph is closed, equivalently all its sublevel sets are closed.

**Lemma 14.1 (Gauge separation of an open convex set).** If \(U\) is open, convex and contains zero in a locally convex space, its gauge
\[
 p(x)=\inf\{t>0:x\in tU\}
\]
is finite, sublinear and continuous, with \(U=\{p<1\}\). If \(z\notin U\), some continuous real linear functional \(\phi\) has \(\phi(u)<1\le\phi(z)\) for all \(u\in U\).

*Proof.* Openness at zero makes \(U\) absorbing, so the infimum is finite. Positive homogeneity follows by rescaling. If \(x\in aU\), \(y\in bU\), convexity puts \((x+y)/(a+b)\) in \(U\), proving subadditivity by approaching the infima. If \(x\in U\), openness along its ray puts \((1+\varepsilon)x\in U\) for small positive \(\varepsilon\), giving \(p(x)<1\). Conversely \(p(x)<1\) puts \(x\in tU\subseteq U\) for some \(t<1\).

Choose a balanced neighbourhood \(V\subseteq U\cap(-U)\). On \(\varepsilon V\), both \(p(h)\) and \(p(-h)\) are below \(\varepsilon\); subadditivity then bounds both signs of \(p(x+h)-p(x)\), proving continuity. On the line generated by \(z\), define \(f(tz)=tp(z)\). This is dominated by \(p\), including negative \(t\) since \(-p(z)\le p(-z)\). Real Hahn–Banach extends it to \(\phi\le p\). Bounds by \(p(h)\) and \(p(-h)\) make \(\phi\) continuous. Finally \(p(z)\ge1\), while \(\phi(u)\le p(u)<1\). ∎

**Theorem 14.2 (Strict point separation).** If \(C\) is a nonempty closed convex set and \(z\notin C\), there is a continuous linear functional with \(\phi(z)>\sup_{c\in C}\phi(c)\).

*Proof.* Choose an open convex balanced neighbourhood \(V\) of zero with \((z+V)\cap C=\varnothing\). Balancedness makes \(z\notin C+V\). Translate that open convex set by one of its points and apply Lemma 14.1 to separate it from \(z\). The resulting nonzero functional has \(\phi(c+v)<\phi(z)\) for all \(c,v\). Select one \(v\in V\) with \(\phi(v)>0\), possible by continuity and nonzeroness. Then \(\sup_C\phi\le\phi(z)-\phi(v)<\phi(z)\), proving a strict gap. ∎

This proof uses locally convex neighbourhoods and real Hahn–Banach, not norm completeness. It therefore applies to weak and weak-star topologies, whose basic neighbourhoods are finite intersections of inverse images of scalar intervals. In particular a norm-closed convex set is weakly closed, because separation expresses it as an intersection of weakly closed affine half-spaces.

## Conjugates and all affine lower bounds

**Definition 14.3 (Conjugate and biconjugate).** For proper \(f\), define on the continuous dual
\[
 f^*(\phi)=\sup_x[\phi(x)-f(x)],\qquad
 f^{**}(x)=\sup_\phi[\phi(x)-f^*(\phi)].
\]
An infinite \(f^*(\phi)\) contributes minus infinity in the second supremum. No infinity-minus-infinity occurs because \(\phi(x)\) is finite. The conjugate is convex and weak-star lower semicontinuous as a supremum of continuous affine functions. Directly from its definition,
\(f(x)+f^*(\phi)\ge\phi(x)\), the Fenchel–Young inequality.

**Theorem 14.4 (Fenchel–Moreau).** Every proper lower-semicontinuous convex function on a real locally convex Hausdorff space satisfies \(f=f^{**}\).

*Proof.* Fenchel–Young gives \(f^{**}\le f\). Fix \(a<f(x_0)\); the point \((x_0,a)\) is outside the closed convex epigraph. Strict separation in the product space gives a continuous functional \(\phi(x)-br\), with \(b\ge0\) because the epigraph extends upward, such that
\[
 \phi(x_0)-ba>\alpha:=\sup_{(x,r)\in\operatorname{epi}f}[\phi(x)-br].
\]
If \(b>0\), the affine function \(\ell(x)=(\phi(x)-\alpha)/b\) lies below \(f\) and has \(\ell(x_0)>a\).

If \(b=0\), then \(\phi(x_0)>\sup_{\operatorname{dom}f}\phi\). First obtain some continuous affine minorant \(\ell_0\) by separating below an epigraph point whose first coordinate lies in the finite domain; there \(b=0\) is impossible since the epigraph includes that same first coordinate. Put \(c=\sup_{\operatorname{dom}f}\phi<\phi(x_0)\). The affine functions \(\ell_0(x)+N(\phi(x)-c)\) remain below \(f\) on its domain and automatically below its infinite values elsewhere. Their values at \(x_0\) tend to infinity, so one exceeds \(a\).

Thus every point value is approached from below by continuous affine minorants. Such a minorant \(\psi(x)-d\) satisfies \(d\ge f^*(\psi)\), so it is bounded above by the corresponding term in \(f^{**}\). Hence \(f^{**}(x_0)>a\) for every \(a<f(x_0)\), including arbitrarily large \(a\) when the value is infinite. This proves equality. ∎

**Worked example 14.5 (A squared norm's conjugate).** For \(f(x)=\|x\|^2/2\) on a Banach space, \(f^*(\phi)=\|\phi\|^2/2\). The upper bound follows by maximizing the scalar expression \(\|\phi\|t-t^2/2\) over \(t\ge0\). For the lower bound choose unit vectors whose functional values approach \(\|\phi\|\), changing signs if necessary, and scale them by \(\|\phi\|\). Their values approach that maximum. A norm-attaining vector is not required. In a real Hilbert space Riesz converts this into the familiar self-conjugacy of half the squared Hilbert norm.

## Subgradients are exact dual equality conditions

**Definition 14.6 (Subgradient).** For \(x\) in the finite domain, \(\phi\in\partial f(x)\) means
\[
 f(y)\ge f(x)+\phi(y-x)\quad\text{for every }y.
\]
Rearranging proves the exact equivalence
\[
 \phi\in\partial f(x)\iff f(x)+f^*(\phi)=\phi(x).
\]
Indeed either condition says \(x\) attains the supremum defining the conjugate. A subgradient is therefore a supporting affine function, not a generalized derivative whose existence is automatic at every domain point.

**Worked example 14.7 (Subgradients of a norm).** At nonzero \(x\), the norm subgradients are exactly the unit dual functionals satisfying \(\phi(x)=\|x\|\). Such functionals exist by Hahn–Banach and their norm inequality gives the supporting inequality. Conversely testing a subgradient at zero and at \(2x\) forces \(\phi(x)=\|x\|\), after which the supporting inequality implies \(\phi(y)\le\|y\|\) for every \(y\), hence \(\|\phi\|\le1\); the nonzero equality forces norm one. At zero the subgradient set is the whole dual unit ball.

For a differentiable convex function, its derivative is its unique subgradient. The supporting inequality follows from increasing one-dimensional secant slopes along a segment. Conversely apply a subgradient inequality to positive and negative small multiples of a direction and pass to the derivative, forcing equality with that derivative on every direction.

## Weak duality and an explicit optimality certificate

For bounded linear \(B:X\to Y\), the problem of minimizing \(f(x)+g(Bx)\) has the dual lower bound
\[
 \inf_x[f(x)+g(Bx)]\ge
 \sup_{\psi\in Y^*}[-f^*(-B^*\psi)-g^*(\psi)].
\]
Apply Fenchel–Young to each term; the two linear pairings cancel. If some \(x,\psi\) satisfy \(-B^*\psi\in\partial f(x)\) and \(\psi\in\partial g(Bx)\), both inequalities are equalities, proving that pair is primal/dual optimal with no gap. Existence of such a pair is a separate question. A general strong-duality theorem requires a qualification condition and is not inferred from this inequality alone.

**Worked example 14.8 (Soft thresholding and a dual certificate).** Minimize \((x-b)^2/2+\lambda|x|\), where \(\lambda>0\). The first subgradient is \(x-b\); the second is \(\lambda\operatorname{sgn}x\) off zero and the interval \([-\lambda,\lambda]\) at zero. Thus the unique minimizer is
\[
 x=\operatorname{sgn}(b)\max(|b|-\lambda,0).
\]
Its dual certificate is \(\psi=b-x\), the clipping of \(b\) to \([-\lambda,\lambda]\). Indeed the conjugates are \(f^*(\theta)=b\theta+\theta^2/2\) and \(g^*(\psi)=0\) on that interval, infinity outside. The dual maximizes \(b\psi-\psi^2/2\) there, giving the same clipping and equality of the two objective values. Strict convexity of the quadratic proves uniqueness of the primal minimizer.

## Proximal minimizers in Hilbert space

**Theorem 14.9 (Proximal existence and characterization).** For proper lower-semicontinuous convex \(f\) on a real Hilbert space and \(\tau>0\), the function \(f(y)+\|y-x\|^2/(2\tau)\) has one unique minimizer \(p\), and
\[
 (x-p)/\tau\in\partial f(p).
\]

*Proof.* Fenchel–Moreau supplies a continuous affine lower bound for \(f\). The quadratic therefore makes minimizing sequences norm bounded. Their values are bounded below and some finite competitor gives a finite upper bound. A bounded Hilbert sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence, proved in chapter 8. Convex norm-closed sublevel sets are weakly closed by separation, so \(f\) is weakly lower semicontinuous; the squared norm is too. Taking lower limits along the subsequence produces a minimizer. Strict convexity of the quadratic on the convex domain proves uniqueness.

For its characterization, compare \(p\) with \(p+t(y-p)\), use convexity to bound the function value there by the convex combination, expand the quadratic, divide by positive \(t\), and let \(t\downarrow0\). The result is exactly \(f(y)\ge f(p)+\langle(x-p)/\tau,y-p\rangle\). Conversely that supporting inequality plus expansion of the quadratic leaves the nonnegative term \(\|y-p\|^2/(2\tau)\), proving minimality. ∎

The proximal map is nonexpansive. If \(p,q\) correspond to \(x,y\), adding their two subgradient inequalities gives monotonicity of the subgradients, hence
\(\|p-q\|^2\le\langle x-y,p-q\rangle\le\|x-y\|\|p-q\|\). Divide when nonzero. Indicator functions of closed convex sets reduce this theorem to orthogonal convex projection; absolute value reduces it to soft thresholding.

## Equality constraints and actual multipliers

A multiplier is not justified merely by writing a formal Lagrangian. Its existence requires a reason that a derivative annihilating feasible directions comes from the constraint's adjoint.

**Proposition 14.10 (Surjective linear constraints).** Let \(B:X\to Y\) be a bounded surjection between Banach spaces and let convex \(f\) be differentiable at a feasible point \(x_0\), where \(Bx_0=b\). Then \(x_0\) minimizes \(f\) on that affine constraint exactly when some \(\lambda\in Y^*\) satisfies \(f'(x_0)=B^*\lambda\).

*Proof.* At a constrained minimum, differentiating along both signs of every \(h\in\ker B\) makes \(f'(x_0)h=0\). Define \(\lambda(Bh)=f'(x_0)h\). Annihilation of the kernel makes this well defined, and linearity is immediate. The quotient isomorphism from open mapping bounds a coset's norm by a constant times its image norm. Choosing representatives arbitrarily close to that coset norm therefore bounds \(|\lambda(y)|\) by a constant times \(\|y\|\), proving continuity and the adjoint identity. Conversely convex support gives \(f(x)\ge f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)\); the adjoint identity makes the last term zero for every feasible \(x\), proving minimality. ∎

In Hilbert spaces the minimum-norm solution of \(Bx=b\) is consequently \(B^*\lambda\). The normal equation is \(BB^*\lambda=b\). It has a unique solution for surjective \(B\): the quotient bound and dual norm detection give \(\|B^*y\|\ge c\|y\|\); thus \(\langle BB^*y,y\rangle\ge c^2\|y\|^2\). Cauchy–Schwarz makes \(BB^*\) bounded below, so its range is closed. Its self-adjoint kernel is zero, making that range dense and hence all. Therefore the bounded right inverse selecting minimum norms is
\[
 S=B^*(BB^*)^{-1}.
\]
This construction is specific to Hilbert geometry and surjective constraints; it is not a universal formula for arbitrary underdetermined Banach equations.

**Worked example 14.11 (A constrained quadratic certificate).** Minimize \((s^2+t^2)/2\) subject to \(s+t=1\). Here \(B(s,t)=s+t\), \(B^*\lambda=(\lambda,\lambda)\), and \(BB^*\lambda=2\lambda\). The multiplier is \(1/2\), giving the unique minimizer \((1/2,1/2)\) with objective \(1/4\). The dual maximizes \(\lambda-\lambda^2\), whose maximum is the same \(1/4\) at \(\lambda=1/2\). The equality is certified by the adjoint stationarity relation and feasibility, rather than asserted from a solver output or from differentiation that ignores the constraint.

**Worked example 14.12 (Biconjugation repairs a missing boundary).** Let \(f\) be the indicator of the open interval \((0,1)\). Its conjugate is \(f^*(a)=\max(a,0)\): for positive slope the supremum approaches the right endpoint, and for negative slope it approaches the left endpoint, whether or not that supremum is attained. Therefore
\[
 f^{**}(x)=\sup_{a\in\mathbb R}[ax-\max(a,0)].
\]
For \(0\le x\le1\), every term is at most zero and \(a=0\) gives zero. For \(x>1\), taking \(a\to+\infty\) makes the expression infinite; for \(x<0\), taking \(a\to-\infty\) does the same. Thus the biconjugate is the indicator of the closed interval \([0,1]\), differing from the original precisely at its missing boundary. The original function was convex and proper but not lower semicontinuous, so the equality theorem's hypothesis was absent.

For comparison, the indicator of the two-point set \(\{-1,1\}\) has conjugate \(|a|\) and biconjugate the indicator of \([-1,1]\). Here the missing hypothesis is convexity: affine lower bounds cannot distinguish the two endpoints from their convex combinations. Conjugation faithfully records every continuous affine minorant, so applying it twice recovers the function determined by those minorants, not an arbitrary discontinuous or nonconvex input unchanged. These examples explain why both regularity assumptions appear in Fenchel–Moreau, rather than being technical additions to an otherwise universal identity.

## The two proximal minimizers in Moreau's decomposition

The proximal theorem has an exact companion involving the conjugate. Write Pτ,f(x) for the unique minimizer of f(y)+||y−x||²/(2τ), where f is proper lower-semicontinuous convex on a real Hilbert space and τ>0. Put p=Pτ,f(x) and v=(x−p)/τ. Its optimality condition says v belongs to the subgradient of f at p. Fenchel equality is symmetric under conjugation, so this is equivalent to p belonging to the subgradient of f* at v.

The proximal condition for f* with parameter 1/τ and input x/τ is precisely x−τv belonging to the subgradient of f* at v. That vector is p, so uniqueness gives v=P1/τ,f*(x/τ). Consequently

\[
 P_{\tau,f}(x)+\tau P_{1/\tau,f^*}(x/\tau)=x.
\]

All parameters and scalings are essential. This identity follows from the already proved subgradient characterization and conjugate equality, not from differentiability of f or f*. It includes nonsmooth and extended-valued examples.

For f(y)=λ||y||, λ>0, the conjugate is the indicator of the closed radius-λ ball. If ||v||≤λ, Cauchy–Schwarz makes <v,y>−λ||y||≤0 and y=0 attains zero. If ||v||>λ, choosing positive multiples of v makes the supremum infinite. Thus the conjugate proximal map is ordinary projection onto that ball. Moreau's identity gives p=(1−τλ/||x||)x when ||x||>τλ and p=0 otherwise. This is vector shrinkage along the radial direction, extending the scalar soft-threshold example without assuming a derivative at zero.

There is also an exact cone decomposition. Let f be the indicator of a closed convex cone C. Its conjugate is the indicator of the polar cone C-polar={v:<v,c>≤0 for all c∈C}: a positive pairing can be scaled to infinity, while otherwise zero is the maximum. At τ=1 the identity becomes x=PCx+PC-polar x. These two projected vectors are perpendicular. To prove it, the projection optimality condition gives v=x−p in the normal cone at p. Test its supporting inequality at zero and at 2p, both in C, to obtain <v,p>≥0 and ≤0, hence equality.

For a subspace the polar cone is its ordinary orthogonal complement, reproducing the earlier linear orthogonal decomposition. For a genuine cone both projections can be nonlinear. The same identity still splits a vector into two uniquely specified nearest-point components, but it does not assert that arbitrary convex sets have linear complements or that their projections add in this fashion. The conic scaling was the property that turned the conjugate into the indicated polar constraint.

## Exercises

### Exercise 14.1 {#convexity-duality-ex-1}

Prove the indicator of a nonempty closed convex set is proper, lower semicontinuous and convex.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Its value is zero on the set and infinity outside.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Its epigraph is the product of the set with the nonnegative half-line.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The function is finite somewhere and never minus infinity, hence proper. Its epigraph is \(C\times[0,\infty)\), closed and convex because both factors are. That proves lower semicontinuity and convexity directly, including the extended-value convention that excludes infeasible points from minimization.
</details>

### Exercise 14.2 {#convexity-duality-ex-2}

Find the conjugate of \(f(x)=\lambda|x|\) on the real line.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Compare the scalar slope magnitude with \(\lambda\).
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
If it is larger, scale \(x\) with matching sign without bound.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
If \(|\phi|\le\lambda\), then \(\phi x-\lambda|x|\le0\), with equality at zero, so the conjugate is zero. If \(|\phi|>\lambda\), choose \(x=t\operatorname{sgn}\phi\); the expression is \(t(|\phi|-\lambda)\to\infty\). Thus the conjugate is the indicator of \([-\lambda,\lambda]\).
</details>

### Exercise 14.3 {#convexity-duality-ex-3}

Derive Fenchel–Young directly and characterize its equality case.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
One candidate in the conjugate supremum is the chosen input.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Equality means that candidate dominates all the others.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The definition gives \(f^*(\phi)\ge\phi(x)-f(x)\), hence the inequality. Equality is equivalent to \(\phi(y)-f(y)\le\phi(x)-f(x)\) for every \(y\). Rearranging yields exactly the subgradient inequality at \(x\). This proves both necessity and sufficiency without a differentiability assumption.
</details>

### Exercise 14.4 {#convexity-duality-ex-4}

Find every subgradient of \(|x|\) at zero and at a nonzero real input.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
At zero the supporting inequality is \(|y|\ge ay\) for all real \(y\).
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
At a nonzero point the ordinary derivative exists.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
At zero, positive and negative \(y\) force \(-1\le a\le1\), and every such value satisfies the inequality. At positive inputs the unique subgradient is one; at negative inputs it is minus one, either by differentiability or by testing nearby points on the same side. A kink produces a set of supporting slopes rather than one derivative.
</details>

### Exercise 14.5 {#convexity-duality-ex-5}

Prove norm-closed convex sets are weakly closed.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Separate any outside point by a continuous linear functional.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The separating half-space is weakly closed.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For each point outside the closed convex set, strict separation supplies a functional and a closed affine half-space containing the set but excluding that point. Intersect all such half-spaces. The intersection equals the set and is weakly closed because the defining functionals are weakly continuous. Convexity is needed for the separation step.
</details>

### Exercise 14.6 {#convexity-duality-ex-6}

Find the conjugate of the indicator of \([-1,2]\).

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
It is the supremum of \(ax\) over the interval.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The maximizing endpoint depends on the sign of \(a\).
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For \(a\ge0\), the maximum is \(2a\), attained at two. For \(a\le0\), it is \(-a\), attained at minus one. The value at zero is zero in either formula. This is the interval's support function, an example of a convex function obtained from a constraint set by conjugation.
</details>

### Exercise 14.7 {#convexity-duality-ex-7}

Explain why the zero vertical coefficient in Fenchel–Moreau's separation proof requires a separate argument.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
One cannot divide the separating inequality by that coefficient.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Use one affine minorant already obtained at a finite-domain point.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
With zero vertical coefficient, separation only distinguishes the input point from the finite domain; it gives no direct affine lower bound by division. Obtain a genuine affine minorant by separating below a point in the finite domain, where a zero coefficient is impossible. Adding large positive multiples of the domain-separating functional minus its domain supremum preserves the minorant and raises its value at the outside input arbitrarily. This repairs precisely the missing case.
</details>

### Exercise 14.8 {#convexity-duality-ex-8}

Compute soft thresholding for \(b=3\), \(\lambda=1\), and exhibit the dual certificate.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
The positive solution satisfies \(x-b+\lambda=0\).
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Use \(\psi=b-x\).
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The minimizer is \(x=2\), with certificate \(\psi=1\). The primal value is \((2-3)^2/2+|2|=5/2\). The dual value is \(3\cdot1-1^2/2=5/2\), and \(\psi\) is feasible in \([-1,1]\). Equal values and the two subgradient conditions prove optimality, while strict convexity proves uniqueness.
</details>

### Exercise 14.9 {#convexity-duality-ex-9}

Show subgradients are monotone: if \(\phi\in\partial f(x)\), \(\psi\in\partial f(y)\), then \((\phi-\psi)(x-y)\ge0\).

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Evaluate each supporting inequality at the other point.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Add them and cancel the two function values.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The inequalities are \(f(y)\ge f(x)+\phi(y-x)\) and \(f(x)\ge f(y)+\psi(x-y)\). Adding gives \(0\ge-(\phi-\psi)(x-y)\), which is the desired nonnegativity. No differentiability or strict convexity is required; the conclusion is a consequence of convex support alone.
</details>

### Exercise 14.10 {#convexity-duality-ex-10}

For an indicator of a closed convex set, identify the proximal minimizer and its dependence on \(\tau\).

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Outside the set the objective is infinite.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Inside, scaling squared distance by a positive constant does not change its minimizer.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The minimizer is exactly the nearest-point projection onto the set. Its location is independent of \(\tau>0\), because the finite objective is squared distance multiplied by one positive scalar. The subgradient characterization becomes the normal-cone inequality defining convex projection. This recovers the earlier projection theorem from the general proximal statement.
</details>

### Exercise 14.11 {#convexity-duality-ex-11}

Why does a proper lower-semicontinuous convex function plus a positive Hilbert quadratic have bounded minimizing sequences?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Fenchel–Moreau's proof supplies a continuous affine minorant.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
A positive quadratic dominates a linear norm bound at infinity.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Write \(f(y)\ge\phi(y)+c\ge-\|\phi\|\|y\|+c\). The distance quadratic grows like a positive multiple of \(\|y\|^2\), up to linear and constant terms depending on the fixed center. Therefore the total objective tends to infinity as \(\|y\|\to\infty\). Any sequence whose objective approaches a finite infimum must remain bounded.
</details>

### Exercise 14.12 {#convexity-duality-ex-12}

Prove that a primal/dual pair satisfying the two displayed subgradient conditions is optimal without assuming a general strong-duality theorem.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Each subgradient condition is equality in its own Fenchel–Young inequality.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The opposite linear terms cancel.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
The conditions give \(f(x)+f^*(-B^*\psi)=-\psi(Bx)\) and \(g(Bx)+g^*(\psi)=\psi(Bx)\). Adding makes the primal value equal the dual lower bound. Weak duality puts every primal value at least this dual value and every dual value at most this primal value. Hence both are optimal and the gap is zero for this certified pair.
</details>

## Summary

Gauges turn open convex geometry into a sublinear domination problem. Hahn–Banach gives strict separation, which recovers closed convex functions from all their affine minorants. Conjugation records those bounds, and subgradients identify exact equality in Fenchel–Young. Hilbert quadratics then produce unique stable proximal minimizers. The next chapter studies compact convex sets through their extreme points, using the locally convex separation theorem just proved.
