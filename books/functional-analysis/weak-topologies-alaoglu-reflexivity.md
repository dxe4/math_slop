
# Weak Topologies, Banach–Alaoglu, and Reflexivity

Norm convergence asks every bounded measurement to become small at one uniform rate. Weak convergence asks each fixed measurement to converge, allowing the rate to depend on that measurement. Weak-star convergence applies the same idea to functionals tested on vectors. These weaker topologies can make unit balls compact even when Riesz's lemma rules out norm compactness.

Preparation is the first five chapters: [duals](bounded-operators-duals.md), [Hahn–Banach and the bidual embedding](hahn-banach.md), [uniform boundedness](uniform-boundedness.md), and [closed graphs](open-mapping-closed-graph.md). The compactness proof below includes the product-topology argument needed for Banach–Alaoglu. It uses Zorn's lemma through ultrafilter extension, explicitly identifying the choice input rather than replacing general compactness with an unjustified subsequence argument.

The main outcomes are a full weak-star compactness theorem, a finite-approximation proof of Goldstine's theorem, and the equivalence between reflexivity and weak compactness of the unit ball for Banach spaces. We will also identify when a compact weak-star ball is metrizable, so that sequence methods become legitimate.

## Weak and weak-star neighbourhoods

**Definition 6.1 (Weak topology).** The weak topology on a normed space \(X\), written \(\sigma(X,X^*)\), is the coarsest topology making every \(\phi\in X^*\) continuous. A basic neighbourhood of \(x\) specifies finitely many inequalities
\[
 |\phi_j(y-x)|<\varepsilon\quad(1\le j\le m).
\]
Allowing different positive tolerances gives the same topology by taking their minimum.

A sequence converges weakly precisely when \(\phi(x_n)\to\phi(x)\) for every \(\phi\in X^*\). This follows from the finite-neighbourhood definition in both directions. Norm convergence implies weak convergence by the functional bound, but the converse can fail.

**Definition 6.2 (Weak-star topology).** On \(X^*\), the topology \(\sigma(X^*,X)\) is generated by the evaluations \(\phi\mapsto\phi(x)\), for \(x\in X\). Its basic neighbourhoods constrain finitely many vectors. It may be weaker than the weak topology \(\sigma(X^*,X^{**})\), whose testing functionals include every member of the bidual.

Both topologies are Hausdorff. Distinct vectors in \(X\) are distinguished by Hahn–Banach, and distinct members of \(X^*\) differ on some input by the definition of distinct functions. Finite-dimensional weak and norm topologies agree because coordinate functionals recover the Euclidean coordinates. In infinite dimensions a basic weak neighbourhood need not control the norm ball in that way.

**Worked example 6.3 (Weak convergence with fixed norm).** In \(c_0\), the coordinate unit vectors \(e_n\) converge weakly to zero. Every functional is given by \(a\in\ell^1\), so \(\phi_a(e_n)=a_n\to0\). Yet \(\|e_n\|_\infty=1\) for all \(n\), so they do not converge to zero in norm. This calculation checks every continuous functional through the proved dual representation; checking just the coordinate evaluations would not by itself prove weak convergence in an arbitrary space.

## A compactness tool beyond sequences

We need compactness for a product indexed by all vectors of \(X\), potentially an uncountable family. Sequential compactness cannot simply be substituted for open-cover compactness in such a product. The following short construction supplies the needed general theorem.

**Definition 6.4 (Filters).** A proper filter on a set is a nonempty family of subsets closed under finite intersections and passage to supersets, and containing no empty set. An ultrafilter is a maximal proper filter. It converges to a point of a topological space when every neighbourhood of that point belongs to it.

Every proper filter extends to an ultrafilter by Zorn: the union of a chain of proper filters is again a proper filter and supplies the required upper bound. An ultrafilter has the alternative property that for every subset \(A\), exactly one of \(A,A^c\) belongs to it. They cannot both belong because their intersection is empty. If \(A\) is not already in it, adjoining \(A\) would destroy properness by maximality; hence some existing filter set misses \(A\), forcing \(A^c\) into the filter.

**Lemma 6.5 (Ultrafilter compactness criterion).** A topological space is compact if and only if every ultrafilter on it converges to at least one point. In a Hausdorff space that limit is unique.

*Proof.* In a compact space the closures of the sets belonging to an ultrafilter have the finite-intersection property. Compactness, equivalently the closed-set finite-intersection principle obtained by complementing an open cover, gives a point in all those closures. Every open neighbourhood of that point belongs to the ultrafilter: otherwise its complement belongs, and that closed complement would have to contain the point, a contradiction. Thus the ultrafilter converges.

Conversely suppose an open cover has no finite subcover. Complements of cover members have the finite-intersection property and generate a proper filter. Extend it to an ultrafilter. Any proposed limit lies in some cover member; this neighbourhood must belong to the ultrafilter, while its complement already does, impossible. Thus failure of compactness produces a nonconvergent ultrafilter. In a Hausdorff space two distinct limits have disjoint neighbourhoods, which cannot both lie in one proper filter. ∎

**Theorem 6.6 (Compact Hausdorff product).** A product of compact Hausdorff spaces is compact in the product topology.

*Proof.* For an ultrafilter on the product, its pushforward under any coordinate projection is an ultrafilter on that factor: a subset of the factor belongs exactly when its inverse image belongs to the original ultrafilter. The filter axioms and alternative property pass through inverse images. Compactness gives a limit in each factor, unique by Hausdorffness. Assemble these limits into a product point. A basic neighbourhood of it restricts only finitely many coordinates. Each corresponding inverse-image neighbourhood belongs to the original ultrafilter; their finite intersection therefore belongs too. Thus the ultrafilter converges in the product topology. Lemma 6.5 gives compactness. The product is Hausdorff because distinct points differ in a coordinate where disjoint factor neighbourhoods can separate them. ∎

This is the form of product compactness we use. Zorn's lemma is the explicit foundational choice principle, just as it was in Hahn–Banach. No assumption of separability has entered the argument.

## Banach–Alaoglu as a closed subset of a product

**Theorem 6.7 (Banach–Alaoglu).** For any normed space \(X\), the closed unit ball \(B_{X^*}\) is compact in its weak-star topology.

*Proof.* For each \(x\in X\), let \(K_x\) be the closed scalar disk of radius \(\|x\|\), compact in the ordinary finite-dimensional topology. Embed the dual ball in
\[
 K=\prod_{x\in X}K_x
\]
by sending a functional to all its values. The product is compact by Theorem 6.6. The image consists exactly of those coordinate families \((a_x)\) satisfying
\[
 a_{x+y}=a_x+a_y,\qquad a_{\lambda x}=\lambda a_x
\]
for all vectors and scalars. Each is a closed equation involving finitely many continuous coordinates, so their common solution set is closed. Conversely such a family defines a linear functional, and the disk bounds give \(|a_x|\le\|x\|\), so its norm is at most one. Thus the image is exactly that closed subset.

The product subspace topology on the image is generated by finitely many evaluations, precisely the weak-star topology. A closed subset of a compact space is compact, proving the theorem. ∎

Domain completeness is unnecessary. The theorem concerns the full continuous dual and its weak-star topology, not norm compactness of that ball. If the dual is infinite dimensional, its norm unit ball is not compact by the first chapter's separation theorem. Compactness has been gained by changing the topology.

## Norms and weak closedness

**Proposition 6.8 (Norm lower semicontinuity).** Closed norm balls in \(X\) are weakly closed. More generally, if \(x_n\to x\) weakly, then \(\|x\|\le\liminf_n\|x_n\|\).

*Proof.* Hahn–Banach gives
\[
 \{x:\|x\|\le r\}
 =\bigcap_{\|\phi\|\le1}\{x:|\phi(x)|\le r\}.
\]
Each set on the right is weakly closed, proving the first statement. For the second, every unit functional satisfies \(|\phi(x)|=\lim|\phi(x_n)|\le\liminf\|x_n\|\). Taking the supremum over those functionals gives the claim. ∎

For a linear subspace, norm closure and weak closure agree: norm closure is contained in weak closure because the weak topology is weaker, while Hahn–Banach separates every point outside the norm closure by a continuous functional vanishing on the subspace. Its kernel is weakly closed and excludes that point. This assertion is about subspaces; arbitrary subsets need not have equal closures.

## Goldstine: every finite set of measurements can be approximated

**Theorem 6.9 (Goldstine).** The canonical image \(J(B_X)\) is weak-star dense in \(B_{X^{**}}\). Explicitly, for \(F\in X^{**}\) with \(\|F\|\le1\), finitely many \(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_m\in X^*\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(x\in X\), \(\|x\|\le1\), such that
\[
 |\phi_j(x)-F(\phi_j)|<\varepsilon\quad(1\le j\le m).
\]

*Proof.* Let \(A:X\to\mathbb F^m\) send \(x\) to the vector of those functional values. The closure \(C=\overline{A(B_X)}\) is bounded, closed and convex in a finite-dimensional real space, viewing complex coordinates as pairs of real coordinates. Hence it is compact. Put \(z=(F(\phi_1),\ldots,F(\phi_m))\). We claim \(z\in C\).

If not, choose a nearest point \(c\in C\), possible by compactness. With the Euclidean inner product on real coordinates, the vector \(d=z-c\) strictly separates \(z\) from \(C\). Indeed for any \(w\in C\), the segment \(c+t(w-c)\) belongs to \(C\); minimality of distance at \(t=0\) gives \(d\cdot(w-c)\le0\). Thus \(d\cdot z=d\cdot c+\|d\|^2>\sup_{w\in C}d\cdot w\).

Any real linear scalar function on \(\mathbb F^m\) has form \(w\mapsto\operatorname{Re}\sum_j\alpha_jw_j\), with real coefficients in the real case. Define \(\phi=\sum_j\alpha_j\phi_j\). The strict separation would give
\[
 \operatorname{Re}F(\phi)
 >\sup_{\|x\|\le1}\operatorname{Re}\phi(x)=\|\phi\|.
\]
The equality follows by changing the sign or complex phase of unit-ball inputs to turn functional values into nonnegative real numbers. But \(\|F\|\le1\) gives \(\operatorname{Re}F(\phi)\le|F(\phi)|\le\|\phi\|\), a contradiction. Hence \(z\in C\). Approximation in the finite-dimensional closure supplies an input in the unit ball with all coordinate errors below \(\varepsilon\), proving the explicit statement and therefore weak-star density. ∎

The result promises a vector for each finite list of measurements. It does not assert that one sequence works for every functional simultaneously, and it does not make \(J\) surjective. Those stronger statements require additional hypotheses.

## Reflexivity and weak compactness

**Definition 6.10 (Reflexive space).** A normed space is reflexive if its canonical map \(J:X\to X^{**}\) is onto. Since \(J\) is isometric and the bidual is Banach, a reflexive space is automatically Banach.

**Theorem 6.11 (Reflexive unit-ball criterion).** A Banach space is reflexive if and only if its closed unit ball is weakly compact.

*Proof.* If \(J\) is onto, it maps \(B_X\) bijectively to \(B_{X^{**}}\). Its weak topology corresponds exactly to the weak-star topology there, because both are generated by the same functionals \(\phi\in X^*\). Banach–Alaoglu applied to the normed space \(X^*\) gives compactness of the bidual ball, hence weak compactness of \(B_X\).

Conversely, if \(B_X\) is weakly compact, its image under the weak-to-weak-star continuous map \(J\) is compact. In the Hausdorff weak-star bidual ball it is therefore closed. Goldstine says that image is dense in the whole ball, so it equals the ball. Scaling gives surjectivity of \(J\) on the whole bidual, proving reflexivity. ∎

**Worked example 6.12 (The missing vector in the bidual of \(c_0\)).** The dual identifications from chapter 2 give \((c_0)^*=\ell^1\) and \((c_0)^{**}=\ell^\infty\), with \(J\) the ordinary inclusion of a sequence tending to zero into bounded sequences. The constant sequence one belongs to the bidual but not to that image, so \(c_0\) is not reflexive and its unit ball is not weakly compact.

The finite initial all-ones sequences \(u_n\in c_0\) have norm one. Against any \(a\in\ell^1\), their pairings tend to \(\sum a_j\), the pairing with the constant-one element of the bidual. They therefore converge weak-star in the bidual to a point outside \(J(c_0)\). They cannot converge weakly to any vector of \(c_0\): coordinate evaluations would force every coordinate of the limit to be one. This illustrates the distinction between compactness in a dual ball and surjectivity of a particular canonical embedding.

## Separable preduals permit sequence methods on bounded balls

**Proposition 6.13 (Weak-star metrizability on the ball).** If \(X\) is separable, the weak-star topology of \(B_{X^*}\) is metrizable and the ball is sequentially compact.

*Proof.* Choose a countable dense subset \((x_j)\) of the unit ball and put
\[
 d(\phi,\psi)=\sum_{j\ge1}2^{-j}
 \frac{|\phi(x_j)-\psi(x_j)|}{1+|\phi(x_j)-\psi(x_j)|}.
\]
The scalar function \(t/(1+t)\) is increasing and subadditive for nonnegative arguments: split \((a+b)/(1+a+b)\) into its two numerator terms and bound their denominators separately by \(1+a\) and \(1+b\). Thus the sum gives a pseudometric. Zero distance forces agreement on the dense set and hence on all of \(X\) by continuity, giving a metric. The tail weights make its topology exactly convergence in the countably many listed coordinates: finitely many small coordinate errors control a sum up to a small tail, and small metric distance controls each fixed coordinate.

On the dual unit ball, any omitted unit input can be approximated by some \(x_j\), and the evaluation error of \(\phi-\psi\) is at most \(2\|x-x_j\|\). This turns control on the dense set into continuity of every evaluation in the metric topology. Conversely the metric is built from weak-star continuous coordinates and uniformly small tails, so its topology is no stronger than weak-star. Thus they agree. Banach–Alaoglu gives a compact metric ball, hence sequential compactness. ∎

Outside a bounded ball the approximation estimate lacks the common bound two. Separability alone does not make this particular argument a metric description of the entire unbounded dual. Always state the bounded set on which a countable testing family is being used.

**Worked example 6.14 (Evaluation moves weak-star but not in norm).** On \(C([0,1])^*\), let \(\delta_t(f)=f(t)\). If \(t_n\to t\), continuity of each fixed input function gives \(\delta_{t_n}(f)\to\delta_t(f)\). Therefore the evaluations converge weak-star. Each has norm one, consistent with the boundedness needed to compare different evaluations.

For distinct \(s,t\), however, \(\|\delta_s-\delta_t\|=2\). The upper bound follows from \(|f(s)-f(t)|\le2\|f\|_\infty\). To attain it, choose a continuous piecewise linear function taking value one at \(s\), minus one at \(t\), linear between them and constant outside, with signs interchanged if their order requires it. Its uniform norm is one and the evaluation difference has modulus two. Thus a sequence with \(t_n\ne t\) can converge weak-star while staying exactly distance two from its limit in the dual norm.

The weak-star statement fixes an input function before taking the limit. The norm statement lets the maximizing function depend on both evaluation points, with a steeper transition as the points approach. That changed order of choices explains the two conclusions without a contradiction.

## Exercises

### Exercise 6.1 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-1}

Show norm convergence implies weak convergence and explain why the reverse implication fails in \(c_0\).

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Apply the functional norm bound to the input difference.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Use the coordinate unit vectors and the known dual of \(c_0\).
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For each fixed \(\phi\), \(|\phi(x_n-x)|\le\|\phi\|\|x_n-x\|\to0\). Thus norm convergence implies weak convergence. In \(c_0\), every dual functional has summable coefficients, so it sends \(e_n\) to a coefficient tending to zero. Therefore \(e_n\to0\) weakly, while their norms remain one and no norm convergence to zero occurs.
</details>

### Exercise 6.2 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-2}

Prove every weakly convergent sequence is norm bounded.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Regard its canonical images as operators on \(X^*\).
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The dual is Banach even if \(X\) is incomplete.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For every \(\phi\in X^*\), the scalars \((Jx_n)(\phi)=\phi(x_n)\) converge and are bounded. The operators \(Jx_n:X^*\to\mathbb F\) are thus pointwise bounded on a Banach domain. Uniform boundedness gives \(\sup_n\|Jx_n\|<\infty\). Since \(J\) is isometric, this is exactly \(\sup_n\|x_n\|<\infty\). Completeness of \(X\) itself is not required.
</details>

### Exercise 6.3 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-3}

If \(X\) is Banach and \(\phi_n\to\phi\) weak-star, prove the functional norms remain bounded. Explain the completeness requirement.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Weak-star convergence bounds the outputs on every fixed vector.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Use uniform boundedness with input space \(X\).
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For each \(x\), the sequence \(\phi_n(x)\) converges and is bounded. Uniform boundedness on the Banach domain \(X\) gives \(\sup_n\|\phi_n\|<\infty\). For an incomplete counterexample take \(X=c_{00}\) with supremum norm and \(\phi_n(x)=nx_n\). Every fixed finite sequence eventually gives zero, so the functionals converge weak-star to zero, while their norms equal \(n\). The missing hypothesis is precisely domain completeness.
</details>

### Exercise 6.4 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-4}

Why does Banach–Alaoglu not contradict noncompactness of infinite-dimensional norm unit balls?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
The same underlying set can carry different topologies.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Weak-star neighbourhoods constrain finitely many evaluations rather than a norm supremum.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Riesz's lemma rules out compactness for the norm topology of an infinite-dimensional normed space. Banach–Alaoglu proves compactness of a dual ball for the weaker weak-star topology. A separated norm sequence need not be separated in that weaker topology. Compactness is a property of a set together with its topology, not of the point set alone.
</details>

### Exercise 6.5 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-5}

Prove the linearity conditions in the product proof of Banach–Alaoglu define closed sets.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Each condition concerns finitely many coordinate projections.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Take an inverse image of the closed scalar set zero.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For fixed \(x,y\), the function \((a_z)\mapsto a_{x+y}-a_x-a_y\) is continuous in the product topology, so its zero set is closed. For fixed scalar \(\lambda\) and vector \(x\), the same applies to \(a_{\lambda x}-\lambda a_x\). The intersection over all such equations is closed because arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed. Countability of the equations is unnecessary.
</details>

### Exercise 6.6 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-6}

Show that weakly compact subsets of a normed space are norm bounded.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Each fixed continuous functional is bounded on the compact set.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Apply uniform boundedness to all canonical images of its points, acting on \(X^*\).
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
If \(K\) is weakly compact, every \(\phi\in X^*\) has bounded values on \(K\), since it is continuous for that topology. The family \(\{Jx:x\in K\}\) is pointwise bounded on the Banach space \(X^*\). Uniform boundedness gives a common norm bound, and isometry of \(J\) converts it to \(\sup_{x\in K}\|x\|<\infty\). This does not assert that every bounded set is weakly compact.
</details>

### Exercise 6.7 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-7}

Verify weak lower semicontinuity of the norm using one norming functional at the limit point.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
If the limit is nonzero, choose \(\phi(x)=\|x\|\), \(\|\phi\|=1\).
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Compare the limiting scalar values with the input norms.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For nonzero weak limit \(x\), choose the stated norming functional. Weak convergence gives \(\phi(x_n)\to\|x\|\). Since \(|\phi(x_n)|\le\|x_n\|\), taking the lower limit gives \(\|x\|\le\liminf\|x_n\|\). If \(x=0\), the inequality is automatic from nonnegativity. The conclusion is an inequality rather than convergence of norms.
</details>

### Exercise 6.8 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-8}

Explain the finite-dimensional separation step in Goldstine's proof using a nearest point of a compact convex set.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Move from the nearest point a small positive fraction toward an arbitrary set point.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Differentiate the squared Euclidean distance at that fraction equal to zero.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
If \(c\) is nearest to \(z\notin C\), then \(c+t(w-c)\in C\) for \(0\le t\le1\). Minimality implies the right derivative of \(\|z-c-t(w-c)\|^2\) at zero is nonnegative, so \((z-c)\cdot(w-c)\le0\). The real functional with coefficient vector \(z-c\) therefore has its value on every \(w\in C\) at most its value at \(c\), while its value at \(z\) is larger by \(\|z-c\|^2>0\). This is the strict separation used in the contradiction.
</details>

### Exercise 6.9 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-9}

Identify the canonical embedding of \(c_0\) into its bidual and prove it is not onto.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Use \((c_0)^*=\ell^1\) and \((\ell^1)^*=\ell^\infty\).
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Compare the pairings with summable coefficient sequences.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
For \(x\in c_0\), its bidual value on \(a\in\ell^1\) is \(\sum a_nx_n\), exactly the functional on \(\ell^1\) represented by the bounded sequence \(x\). Thus the canonical embedding is the ordinary inclusion into \(\ell^\infty\). The constant sequence one is bounded but does not tend to zero, so it is outside the image. Hence \(c_0\) is not reflexive.
</details>

### Exercise 6.10 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-10}

Why does a compact weak-star dual ball need not have a convergent subsequence from every sequence without further hypotheses in this proof?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Open-cover compactness and sequential compactness coincide for metric spaces, not all spaces.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
The separability proposition provides a metric specifically on a bounded ball.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
Banach–Alaoglu proves topological compactness by a product argument that may use uncountably many coordinates. General compact spaces need not be sequentially compact, so a subsequence conclusion cannot be inferred solely from that proof. With a separable predual, Proposition 6.13 supplies a metric for the weak-star ball; compact metric spaces are sequentially compact. The extra hypothesis licenses the sequence method rather than changing the meaning of compactness.
</details>

### Exercise 6.11 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-11}

Show that a normed space whose canonical bidual map is onto must be complete.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
The bidual is Banach because it is a continuous dual.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Use the surjective isometry to transport a Cauchy sequence and its limit.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
If \((x_n)\) is Cauchy, isometry makes \((Jx_n)\) Cauchy in the Banach bidual, with limit \(F\). Surjectivity gives \(F=Jx\) for some \(x\). Then \(\|x_n-x\|=\|Jx_n-Jx\|\to0\). Thus every Cauchy sequence converges in \(X\). Completeness follows from reflexivity rather than being an additional assumption in its definition.
</details>

### Exercise 6.12 {#weak-topologies-alaoglu-reflexivity-ex-12}

In the metric proof, explain why agreement on a dense set determines a bounded functional, and why the uniform norm bound is needed to recover the topology of all evaluations.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Continuity gives uniqueness from density.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Estimate \(|(\phi-\psi)(x-x_j)|\) for two unit-ball functionals.
</details>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>
If two bounded functionals agree on a dense subset, continuity makes them agree on limits from that subset and hence everywhere. For topology, two functionals in the dual unit ball satisfy \(|(\phi-\psi)(x-x_j)|\le2\|x-x_j\|\). Choosing one nearby listed point reduces arbitrary evaluation control to that listed coordinate plus a uniformly small error. Without a common norm bound, the approximation error could be magnified arbitrarily and the countable-coordinate argument would not establish continuity of every evaluation.
</details>

## Summary

Weak and weak-star topologies retain specified scalar measurements while relaxing norm control. Product compactness gives Banach–Alaoglu for every dual unit ball. Hahn–Banach gives weak closedness of norm balls, and finite-dimensional separation gives Goldstine's dense canonical image. Reflexivity is exactly the condition making a Banach unit ball weakly compact. With a separable predual the weak-star ball becomes metrizable, allowing subsequence arguments. The next chapter adds an inner product, whose orthogonality supplies stronger geometry and explicit minimizers.
