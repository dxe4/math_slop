
# Reader Guide and Notation {#section-1-reader-guide-and-notation}

<a id="section-1-why-this-book-exists"></a>
## Why this book exists {#section-3-why-this-book-exists}

Mathematics is not only proved; it is read, written, typeset, searched, shared, reviewed, presented, funded, and lived. Each of these activities requires skill, and each can be taught. This book is for students, early researchers, and working mathematicians who want to improve the practical disciplines that surround theorems and proofs. It is not a textbook of any single mathematical subject; it is a professional toolkit.

The manuscript is self-contained at the foundation level. Every chapter introduces its own notation, states its assumptions explicitly, and ends with solved practice. If a chapter feels dense, use the notation guide and diagnostic before concluding that the material is inaccessible. Many of the skills here---writing a clear proof, managing a collaborative project, giving a conference talk---compress years of experience into a short text; expanding that compression is a learnable skill.

<a id="section-7-three-reading-routes"></a>
## Three reading routes {#section-9-three-reading-routes}

<a id="section-9-the-continuous-route"></a>
### The continuous route {#section-11-the-continuous-route}

Read the chapters in reading order for a sustained curriculum: active reading, mathematical writing, LaTeX and typesetting, literature search, collaboration tools, peer review, conference presentations, grant writing, ethics, and career paths. This route suits a semester-long course or a self-study program over several months.

<a id="section-13-the-communication-route"></a>
### The communication route {#section-15-the-communication-route}

Read Chapters 1--3 for reading, writing, and typesetting, then Chapter 7 for conference presentations and Chapter 8 for grant writing. Add Chapter 6 for peer review if you are preparing a submission. This route suits a student preparing a first paper or thesis.

<a id="section-17-the-professional-practice-route"></a>
### The professional-practice route {#section-19-the-professional-practice-route}

Read Chapters 4--6 for literature search, collaboration, and peer review, then Chapters 9--10 for ethics and career planning. Return to Chapters 7--8 when you are preparing a talk or proposal. This route suits an early-career researcher building sustainable habits.

<a id="section-21-how-to-work-a-chapter"></a>
## How to work a chapter {#section-23-how-to-work-a-chapter}

First read the opening question and write down the exact skill, convention, or decision at stake. On a second pass, cover each worked example and predict the next step before reading it. Work at least four exercises before reading their solutions: one definition or convention check, one practical application, one reasoning or judgment exercise, and one repair or counterexample task. Compare your solution by structure rather than by wording. If your reasoning differs, ask whether it establishes the same conditions and uses no hidden hypothesis.

Maintain three notebooks:

1. a **convention notebook** recording notation, formatting rules, and citation standards you intend to adopt;
2. a **decision log** recording why you chose one tool, strategy, or presentation over another;
3. a **repair notebook** recording errors you made, how you caught them, and what process change would prevent recurrence.

<a id="section-31-exercise-markers-used-across-chapters"></a>
## Exercise markers used across chapters {#section-33-exercise-markers-used-across-chapters}

Exercises are numbered within each chapter. Markers indicate the expected reasoning demand:

- **R** --- retrieval or notation check;
- **F** --- routine fluency or practical application;
- **P** --- proof, justification, or reasoning;
- **C** --- counterexample, repair, or misconception diagnosis;
- **T** --- transfer to a new surface form or context;
- **I** --- investigation or project requiring extended reasoning across sections.

A single exercise may carry multiple markers. Solutions show the decisive reasoning, not only the final answer. When an exercise tests model choice, strategy, or ethical judgment, the solution explains why that choice is warranted.

<a id="section-44-how-to-report-a-suspected-error"></a>
## How to report a suspected error {#section-46-how-to-report-a-suspected-error}

Report a suspected error with the chapter identifier, the smallest questionable claim, and, when possible, a counterexample or proposed repair. For an exercise, include the exact step at which the supplied answer and your reasoning diverge. This makes mathematical and notation issues reproducible.

<a id="section-48-scope-and-deliberate-boundaries"></a>
## Scope and deliberate boundaries {#section-50-scope-and-deliberate-boundaries}

This book reaches a first treatment of active reading, mathematical writing, LaTeX typesetting, literature search, collaboration infrastructure, peer review, oral presentation, grant writing, professional ethics, and career planning. It does not attempt advanced topics in any of these areas: copy editing, journal management, large-scale software engineering, advanced research design, or clinical professional counseling. Those are separate volumes because each needs its own prerequisites, examples, and evidence standards.

The next component is a prerequisite diagnostic. The notation and convention guide after it is designed for repeated reference rather than one-time reading.
