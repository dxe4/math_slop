
# Glossary and Index {#section-1-glossary-and-index}

This is a retrieval aid. Compact entries cannot replace the full definitions or hypotheses in their chapters. Chapter numbers follow reading order.

## Glossary {#section-5-glossary}

**Active reading.** The habit of expanding compressed mathematical prose into its full logical structure, checking hypotheses, quantifiers, and dependencies. Ch. 1.

**Branch (Git).** An independent line of development in a version-controlled repository, allowing parallel work and review before merging. Ch. 5.

**BibTeX / BibLaTeX.** Bibliography formats for LaTeX documents, ensuring consistent citation styling and persistent identifiers. Chs. 3, 4.

**Broader impacts.** The societal, educational, and disciplinary benefits of a research project, required in many grant proposals. Ch. 8.

**Catastrophic cancellation.** Loss of relative accuracy when subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers. Ch. 1.

**Collaboration protocol.** An agreed set of conventions for communication, decision-making, and conflict resolution in a mathematical team. Ch. 5.

**Commit (Git).** A snapshot of a repository at a point in time, accompanied by a message explaining what changed and why. Ch. 5.

**Commensurability.** Sufficient similarity of constructs, measures, and contrasts to justify quantitative pooling across studies. Ch. 4.

**Conflict of interest.** A situation in which a reviewer's personal or professional interests may compromise their impartiality. Ch. 6.

**Contrapositive.** The logically equivalent statement formed by negating and reversing the hypotheses and conclusion of an implication. Chs. 1, 2.

**Counterexample.** An object that satisfies the hypotheses of a conjecture but violates its conclusion, disproving the conjecture. Ch. 1.

**Display math.** LaTeX mathematics set on its own line, appropriate for important equations and multi-line calculations. Ch. 3.

**Five-mark system.** An active-reading annotation method tracking objects, hypotheses, quantifiers, conclusion, and dependencies. Ch. 1.

**Floating-point reliability.** The discipline of choosing numerical expressions that avoid catastrophic cancellation, overflow, and underflow. Ch. 1.

**Gift author.** A person listed as an author who did not make a substantial intellectual contribution to the work (also called an honorary author). Ch. 9.

**Git.** A distributed version control system designed for text-based sources such as LaTeX, Markdown, and program code. Ch. 5.

**Grant proposal.** A persuasive document that convinces a funder that a research project is important, feasible, and likely to succeed. Ch. 8.

**Inline math.** LaTeX mathematics enclosed in single dollar signs, flowing with the text for short expressions. Ch. 3.

**Issue tracker.** A tool for recording concrete, actionable tasks in a collaborative project, creating an auditable decision history. Ch. 5.

**LaTeX.** A typesetting system designed to encode mathematical structure explicitly, producing documents where visual hierarchy matches logical hierarchy. Ch. 3.

**Lemma.** A stepping-stone result used to prove something larger; named honestly to guide the reader. Ch. 2.

**Literate programming.** An approach that interleaves code, output, and exposition, requiring discipline for version control. Ch. 5.

**Local observation.** Something seen, heard, or recorded in a specific setting without a controlled design. Ch. 4.

**Mathematical result.** A statement true by definition, deduction, or calculation within stated axioms. Ch. 1.

**MathSciNet.** A specialist bibliographic and review database for mathematics, maintained by the American Mathematical Society. It points to primary works but is itself a secondary discovery source. Ch. 4.

**Mean Value Theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then there exists $c \in (a, b)$ with $f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$. Ch. 2.

**Merge request / pull request.** A proposal to integrate one Git branch into another, accompanied by review and discussion. Ch. 5.

**Monovariant.** A quantity that changes in a single direction (usually strictly decreasing or increasing) and is used to prove termination. Ch. 1.

**MSC.** Mathematics Subject Classification, a hierarchical scheme for indexing mathematical literature. Ch. 4.

**Nonexample.** An object that fails a definition, used to clarify boundaries. Ch. 1.

**Peer review.** The quality-control mechanism in which independent readers check correctness, clarity, and novelty before publication. Ch. 6.

**Preliminary data.** Evidence from pilot work or unpublished calculations that demonstrates feasibility for a proposed project. Ch. 8.

**Proof by contrapositive.** A proof strategy that establishes $P \Rightarrow Q$ by proving $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$. Chs. 1, 2.

**Quantifier.** A logical symbol ($\forall$ or $\exists$) specifying whether a statement applies to all objects of a type or asserts the existence of at least one. Ch. 1.

**Reproducibility.** The property that a computational claim can be independently verified by running the same code with the same data and environment. Ch. 9.

**Requirements.txt.** A Python dependency list whose version constraints help reconstruct an environment. Exact package pins alone do not fix the operating system, hardware, system libraries, or external data. Ch. 5.

**Response document.** A structured reply to peer-review comments, quoting each comment and stating the author's response or revision. Ch. 6.

**Scope mistake (reviewing).** Rejecting or accepting a paper based on the reviewer's interests rather than the venue's standards. Ch. 6.

**Signal word.** A word such as "Proof:" that tells the reader verification mode begins. Ch. 2.

**Single-source fallacy.** Relying on one paper or one author for an entire topic, producing a narrow and potentially biased view. Ch. 4.

**Subitizing.** Rapid recognition of a small quantity without serial counting. Not used in this book; retained for cross-reference compatibility.

**Theorem.** A major result, stated with all hypotheses and the exact conclusion, separated from its proof. Ch. 2.

**Tombstone.** The end-of-proof marker $\square$ or QED. Ch. 2.

**Transfer task.** A problem whose surface form differs from worked examples, testing whether structure is recognized. Ch. 1.

**Version control.** A systematic record of changes to text-based documents, enabling collaboration, rollback, and audit. Ch. 5.

**Well-ordering principle.** Every nonempty set of positive integers has a least element; equivalent to induction for proving properties of $\mathbb{N}$. Ch. 5.

**zbMATH Open.** An open specialist bibliographic and review database for mathematics. Ch. 4.

## Index of conventions {#section-129-index-of-conventions}

**Citation policy.** Paraphrase sources; cite the job they perform; do not copy distinctive examples; record disagreements and unknown provenance. Front matter; Chs. 2, 4, 9.

**LaTeX source rules.** No raw Unicode math symbols; brace multi-character subscripts and superscripts; standard `amsmath` and `amssymb` only; no `\hline` in math arrays. Ch. 3.

**Notation discipline.** Introduce every symbol before use; respect type; avoid unnecessary decoration. Ch. 2.

**Theorem--proof pair.** State the result first; signal the proof; end with $\square$ or QED. Ch. 2.
