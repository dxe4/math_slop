
# Grant Writing

Mathematical research requires resources: time, travel, equipment, students, and access to data or computation. Grants are one important mechanism for securing these resources. A grant proposal is not a research paper; it is a persuasive document that must convince reviewers that the proposed work meets the funder's published criteria.

This chapter introduces the structure, style, and strategy of effective grant proposals. The audience is a panel of experts with limited time and a pile of competing proposals.

## Understanding the funder

Different funders have different goals:

- **Government agencies** (NSF, EPSRC, DFG) publish agency- and programme-specific criteria. For example, NSF evaluates Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts; EPSRC and DFG use their own published criteria.
- **Private foundations** (Simons, Sloan, Clay) publish programme-specific aims that may emphasize particular areas, investigator stages, institutions, or kinds of risk.
- **Industry sponsors** publish or negotiate their own aims, deliverables, timelines, intellectual-property terms, and disclosure conditions.

Read the call for proposals carefully. Address every required section. Use the funder's language. If they ask for "broader impacts," do not write "applications."

## The core argument

Every grant proposal makes three implicit claims:

1. **The problem matters:** It advances knowledge, solves a practical need, or opens a new area.
2. **The approach is sound:** The methods are appropriate, the timeline is realistic, and the team can execute.
3. **The investigator is capable:** The PI has the expertise, track record, and institutional support to deliver.

A weak proposal fails on at least one of these. A strong proposal supports all three with evidence.

The allocation below shows one way to organize an argument; it is not a funder rule or a submission checklist. Component names, forms, placement, and page limits vary by funder, programme, and solicitation. Build the actual submission from the current call and official instructions. For NSF, consult the current version of the *Proposal & Award Policies & Procedures Guide* (PAPPG), any applicable policy notices, and the specific solicitation.

### Summary

The summary is often the most-read component of a proposal. Write it last, but polish it most. It should stand alone: a reader who sees only the summary should understand the problem, the approach, and the expected outcome.

Open with the problem, not with background. State the main question in one sentence. Describe the approach in two sentences. End with the expected impact.

### Background and significance

Establish the intellectual context. What is known? What is open? Why does the open problem matter? Cite the key papers, including your own prior work. Show that you understand the field.

**Common error:** Writing a literature review that is comprehensive but directionless. Every citation should support the claim that the proposed work fills a specific gap.

### Research plan

This is the technical core. For each aim or objective:

1. State the precise question.
2. Describe the method, with enough detail that an expert could assess its feasibility.
3. Identify risks and contingency plans.
4. State the expected outcome and how you will know if you have achieved it.

Use timelines, milestones, and deliverables. A proposal that says "we will study X" is weaker than one that says "by month $12$ we will prove Y or determine that Z is the obstruction."

### Societal, educational, or disciplinary contribution

Explain the kinds of benefit requested by the funder in its own terms. Be specific. "Training students" is vague; "mentoring two PhD students from underrepresented groups in computational topology" is concrete. NSF calls one of its two merit-review criteria **Broader Impacts** and requires a separately headed Broader Impacts section within the Project Description under PAPPG 24-1. Do not transfer that label or its current placement rules to another funder without checking its call.

### Budget and justification

Every dollar must be justified. Explain why each item is necessary for the project. Do not pad the budget; reviewers notice. Do not underbudget; an unrealistic budget suggests poor planning.

### Evidence that the team can deliver

Use preliminary work, a feasible plan, facilities, and relevant expertise to show that the team can execute the project. Personnel documents are separate, format-controlled components for many funders, so do not invent a free-form biosketch. Under NSF PAPPG 24-1, each senior/key person's Biographical Sketch uses the Common Form and is prepared and certified in SciENcv; the current PAPPG and solicitation control the final requirements.

## Writing style

Grant proposals are read quickly. Optimize for clarity and persuasion.

- **Use active voice:** "We will develop" is stronger than "It is hoped that development will occur."
- **Be specific:** "We will classify all graphs with property P up to $n = 20$" is stronger than "We will study graphs with property P."
- **Avoid jargon:** Define technical terms on first use. Reviewers may be from adjacent fields.
- **Use visual structure:** Headings, lists, and white space guide the reader through dense material.

## Common errors in grant writing

### The solution in search of a problem

A proposal that says "I have a hammer; let me find nails" fails the significance test. Start with the problem, then introduce the method.

### The overambitious timeline

Promising $5$ major theorems in $2$ years suggests naivety or dishonesty. Reviewers prefer a focused plan with realistic milestones.

### The missing preliminary work

A proposal that claims "we will develop a new theory of X" with no evidence that the PI has thought about X is unconvincing. Include preliminary results, even if they are partial or negative.

### The generic broader impacts

"This work will benefit humanity" is empty. "We will develop undergraduate modules on topological data analysis and test them at three institutions" is evidence-based.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise 1
You are writing a $15$-page NSF Project Description under a solicitation that uses that limit. Allocate its internal page budget and explain which proposal components must remain separate. Always check the current solicitation and PAPPG before submission.

### Exercise 2
Rewrite the following weak aim as a strong aim: "We will study the properties of prime numbers."

### Exercise 3
A reviewer asks why you need $3$ months of summer salary instead of $1$. Draft a one-paragraph response.

### Exercise 4
List three concrete broader impacts for a project in pure algebraic geometry, avoiding generic claims.

### Exercise 5
Explain why "preliminary data" from an unpublished calculation is valuable in a grant proposal, even if it does not constitute a theorem.

### Exercise 6
A colleague's proposal is rejected with the comment "the significance is unclear." Diagnose the likely problem and suggest a revision strategy.

## Solutions

**Solution 1.** One defensible allocation within a $15$-page **Project Description** is:

| Section | Pages | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Background | $3$ | Enough to establish gap and significance. |
| Research plan | $7$ | The technical core; needs hypotheses, methods, and risk controls. |
| Prior results and feasibility | $2$ | Connects evidence to the proposed work. |
| Broader impacts | $2$ | Integrates concrete, assessable activities. |
| Timeline | $1$ | Visual; milestones and deliverables. |

The Project Summary, References Cited, Budget and Budget Justification, and other required forms are separate proposal components rather than slices of those $15$ pages. The exact required components and limits come from the current solicitation and PAPPG.

**Solution 2.** Strong aim:

> "We will use a segmented sieve to enumerate every twin-prime pair $(p,p+2)$ with $p\leq 10^{9}$. We will publish the implementation and the resulting list, and verify the count with an independently written second implementation."

This states a finite question, a suitable method, concrete outputs, and a reproducible success criterion. The computation would establish the complete list only up to the stated bound; it would not decide whether infinitely many twin primes exist.

**Solution 3.** Response:

> "The proposed project requires sustained, uninterrupted focus on technical proof development that is incompatible with a full teaching load. Three months of summer support will allow the PI to work intensively on Aim 2, which involves classifying exceptional cases in a $20$-dimensional parameter space. Preliminary work during a one-month pilot last summer confirmed that meaningful progress requires blocks of at least six uninterrupted weeks."

**Solution 4.**
1. Develop interactive visualizations of algebraic surfaces for undergraduate calculus courses, tested at two partner institutions with pre- and post-assessments.
2. Mentor two PhD students and one postdoc, with explicit professional development plans and placement targets.
3. Organize a public lecture series on the mathematics of symmetry, with recordings archived and distributed to high school teachers.

**Solution 5.** Preliminary data demonstrates feasibility. It shows the PI has engaged with the problem, identified obstacles, and developed tools. Even negative preliminary data is valuable: it shows the PI understands why naive approaches fail and has a plausible alternative. A proposal with no preliminary work is a promise; a proposal with preliminary work is evidence.

**Solution 6.** The likely problem is that the proposal describes the technical work without explaining why anyone should care. The colleague probably opened with background rather than with the problem's importance.

Revision strategy:
1. Rewrite the opening paragraph to state the problem and its consequences in one sentence each.
2. Add a paragraph explicitly connecting the technical result to a broader question or application.
3. Include a sentence in the summary that a non-specialist could understand and find compelling.
4. Ask a colleague from a different subfield to read the opening and state what they think the problem is.
