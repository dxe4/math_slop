
# Conference Presentations

A conference presentation is not a paper read aloud. It is a distinct medium with its own constraints: limited time, a mixed audience, no ability to rewind, and the need to convey both results and motivation. A good talk gives the audience a reason to care, a path to follow, and a clear destination.

This chapter treats the conference presentation as a design problem. The constraints are fixed; the goal is to produce the maximum understanding per minute.

## Know your audience

Before designing a talk, determine who will hear it. A specialized session on algebraic topology allows dense notation and rapid proof sketches. A general mathematics colloquium requires motivation, definitions, and examples that a non-specialist can follow.

Ask:
- What background can I assume?
- What do they care about? (New techniques? Applications to their field? Open problems?)
- How much time do I have, and how many slides or board pages fit in that time?

## Structure of a mathematical talk

A standard $20$-minute talk has approximately three parts:

### The opening ($2$--$3$ minutes)

State the problem in plain language. Why is it interesting? What is known? What is new? Do not start with notation. Start with a question.

> "How many colors are needed to color a planar map so that adjacent regions receive different colors? The answer, known for over a century, is four. But what if we restrict the class of maps?"

### The body ($12$--$14$ minutes)

Introduce one key definition, one key example, and one key theorem. Resist the urge to prove everything. Sketch the main idea, highlight the difficult step, and show why standard methods fail.

If you must include a proof, present the structure, not the algebra. Say: "The proof proceeds by induction on the dimension. The base case is a direct calculation. The inductive step uses a decomposition lemma that I will state without proof."

### The closing ($2$--$3$ minutes)

Restate the main result. Mention one open problem or one direction for future work. Thank the audience and invite questions.

## Slide design

### One idea per slide

A slide crowded with $10$ equations and $3$ paragraphs forces the audience to choose between reading and listening. They will do neither well.

### Readable fonts and sizes

Use at least $24$-point font for body text and $32$-point for headings. Test your slides on the projector you will use, not just your laptop.

### Equations as objects

Display important equations centered and large. Number only equations you will refer to later. Use color sparingly: one highlight color for the current focus, neutral colors for context.

### Use sentences selectively

Slides are not speaker notes. A concise sentence can state a theorem or preserve an exact qualification, but paragraphs of narration compete with the speaker. Prefer:
- The statement of the theorem.
- A diagram or graph.
- A key equation.
- A one-phrase reminder of where you are in the argument.

The spoken text provides the connective tissue.

## Board talks

Some venues prefer chalk or whiteboard. Board talks have different constraints:

- **Write large.** The back row must read your symbols.
- **Plan your board space.** Divide the board into sections and erase strategically.
- **Face the audience.** Do not talk to the board. Write, then turn and speak.
- **Slow down.** Writing takes time; budget for it.

## Rehearsal and timing

Rehearse out loud, standing up, with a timer. A $20$-minute talk rehearsed in $15$ minutes will run long on stage because adrenaline speeds delivery but questions and fumbles consume time. Aim to finish $1$--$2$ minutes early.

Practice the opening until it is automatic. Nerves are highest at the start; a memorized opening builds confidence.

## Handling questions

Questions are not attacks. They are opportunities to clarify and engage.

- **Listen fully.** Do not interrupt the questioner.
- **Repeat the question.** This ensures everyone heard it and gives you time to think.
- **Answer directly.** If you do not know, say so. "That is an interesting question I have not considered" is honest and professional.
- **Defer if necessary.** For questions that require a long answer, offer to discuss afterward.

## Common errors in presentations

### The data dump

Presenting every theorem from a $30$-page paper in $20$ minutes. The audience remembers nothing.

### The notation avalanche

Introducing $15$ symbols in the first $2$ minutes. The audience spends its working memory tracking notation instead of following ideas.

### The proof marathon

Reading a $5$-page proof line by line. Proofs are for papers; talks need structure and intuition.

### The unreadable slide

Equations in $12$-point font, $4$ colors, and a busy background. The audience squints and gives up.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise 1
You have $15$ minutes to present a paper containing three theorems, two lemmas, and four examples. Select what to include and justify your choices.

### Exercise 2
Design a single slide that states a theorem, illustrates it with a diagram, and highlights the hypothesis that distinguishes it from prior work. Describe the layout.

### Exercise 3
You are asked a question you cannot answer. Write three possible responses, ranging from fully honest to evasive. Evaluate which is most professional.

### Exercise 4
A colleague puts $8$ bullet points of text on every slide. Write a constructive suggestion for improving their next talk.

### Exercise 5
You have $20$ minutes for a board talk. Estimate how many complete theorem statements you can write legibly, and explain your calculation.

### Exercise 6
Explain why starting a talk with a slide full of notation definitions is usually ineffective, and suggest an alternative opening.

## Solutions

**Solution 1.** Include:
- The main theorem (the strongest or most surprising result).
- One example that motivates the theorem and one that shows why the hypothesis matters.
- The proof idea in two sentences, focusing on the novel technique.
- One open problem.

Omit: the two lemmas (mention them as "technical tools"), the weaker theorems (mention them as corollaries), and two of the examples (they are for the paper, not the talk).

Justification: A talk is a trailer, not the full film. The audience should leave wanting to read the paper.

**Solution 2.** Layout description:
- **Top third:** Theorem statement in $28$-point font, centered.
- **Middle third:** A simple diagram (e.g., a graph, a geometric figure, or a commutative diagram) illustrating the theorem's conclusion.
- **Bottom third:** A highlighted box in a contrasting color containing the distinguishing hypothesis, with an arrow pointing to the part of the diagram where it matters.
- **Left margin:** A small breadcrumb indicating where we are in the talk (e.g., "Step 3 of 5").

**Solution 3.**
1. Honest: "That is an excellent question, and I do not know the answer. I would need to think about it further."
2. Partially honest: "That is related to work by Smith, though I have not checked whether their methods apply here."
3. Evasive: "That is addressed in the full paper."

The first is most professional. It respects the questioner, admits limits without shame, and invites follow-up discussion.

**Solution 4.** Constructive suggestion:

> "Your content is strong, but the slides may be hard to follow at speed. Consider reducing each slide to one idea: a theorem, a diagram, or a key point. Move supporting text to your speaking notes. The audience will retain more if they listen to you rather than reading paragraphs."

**Solution 5.** A legible theorem statement on a board takes approximately $90$--$120$ seconds to write, including pauses for explanation. In $20$ minutes, allowing $3$ minutes for opening and closing, you have $17$ minutes of board time. At $2$ minutes per theorem, you can write approximately $8$ complete theorem statements if you say nothing else. Realistically, with explanations and diagrams, plan for $2$--$3$ theorem statements per talk.

**Solution 6.** A notation slide forces the audience to memorize symbols before they know why the symbols matter. Working memory is limited; spending it on notation leaves none for concepts. An alternative opening presents a concrete problem first, then introduces notation as needed to solve it. The symbols acquire meaning from context rather than from a dictionary.
