## Common traps and corrective principles

**Ordinary magnitude.** A large integer can be *p*-adically small. Translate
every size statement through $v_p$ before reasoning (Chapters 1 and 4).

**Digit direction.** Stable digits begin at low powers and expansions extend
toward larger powers (Chapter 5).

**Invisible cancellation.** Equal-valuation terms may cancel beyond known
precision. Return a ball, not a guessed zero (Chapters 1 and 6).

**Convergence analogy.** For series in a complete ultrametric field, terms
tending to zero are sufficient (Chapter 7).

**The prime 2.** Exponential domains, derivatives, and square classes often need
stricter hypotheses at $2$ (Chapters 8–10).

**Hensel converse.** A singular derivative does not prove no lift exists
(Chapter 9).

**Finite versus exact.** A root or periodic orbit modulo $p^N$ need not extend
compatibly through every precision (Chapters 9 and 13).

**Uniform distribution.** Normalized Haar probability exists on compact
$\mathbb Z_p$, not on all of $\mathbb Q_p$ (Chapter 11).

**Valuation normalization.** In an extension, decide whether the value group is
$\mathbb Z$ or $(1/e)\mathbb Z$ before comparing formulas (Chapter 12).

**Local-to-global logic.** Local failure always obstructs a rational solution;
local success implies global success only under an appropriate theorem
(Chapter 14).

**Plots.** Coordinates in the figures encode finite arithmetic data. They are
not Euclidean embeddings of $\mathbb Q_p$ (front matter and figure captions).

**Lower-hull sign.** Newton-polygon root valuations are negatives of segment
slopes, and points above the lower hull do not create segments (Chapter 19).

**Completeness hierarchy.** Algebraic closedness, metric completeness, and
spherical completeness are distinct properties (Chapter 21).

**Measure normalization.** Fourier and zeta formulas depend on the chosen
additive character and on additive versus multiplicative Haar measure
(Chapter 22).

**Geometric points.** A Berkovich point may be a disc seminorm rather than a
classical coordinate (Chapter 23).

**Period structures.** Period rings matter because of filtration, Frobenius,
monodromy, topology, and invariants, not merely because they are large fields
(Chapter 24).

**Witt coordinates.** Witt addition is not coordinatewise. Perform operations
through the universal ghost-polynomial identities (Chapter 25).

**Frobenius convention.** Arithmetic and geometric Frobenius are inverses.
State the choice before using reciprocity or a Galois exact sequence (Chapters
26–27).

**Formal versus convergent.** A formal logarithm can satisfy an identity before
it converges at any chosen point. Check the analytic domain separately
(Chapters 28–29).

**Finite slope.** A plotted valuation of coefficients is not automatically a
$U_p$-slope or an eigencurve computation. Identify the operator and Banach
space (Chapter 30).

**Eventual tower growth.** A few class numbers cannot certify Iwasawa invariants.
The structure and control theorems justify the eventual formula (Chapter 31).

**Tilting addition.** The sharp map is multiplicative, not additive. Never
transport a polynomial coefficientwise and call it the tilting equivalence
(Chapter 32).

**Period implications.** Crystalline implies semistable, de Rham, and
Hodge–Tate in that direction. Reverse implications fail without extra
hypotheses (Chapter 33).

**Tate uniformization.** The quotient $K^\times/q^{\mathbb Z}$ describes split
multiplicative reduction under the stated hypothesis; it is not a universal
coordinate model for every elliptic curve (Chapter 34).

**Cocycles and coboundaries.** A cohomology class is an equivalence class of
cocycles, not an individual function. Continuous local Galois cohomology also
depends on the module topology (Chapter 35).

**Selmer conditions.** A Selmer group is cut out by named local images. Passing
every local test need not by itself produce a rational point (Chapter 36).

**Commuting semilinear actions.** A $(\varphi,\Gamma)$-module carries compatible
Frobenius and group actions; neither ordinary linear algebra nor one action
alone captures the object (Chapter 37).

**Deformation functors.** A tangent-space count predicts first-order directions,
not automatically a smooth universal ring. Obstructions and representability
hypotheses must be checked (Chapter 38).

**Certified local-field algorithms.** A printed polynomial or digit string is
not a certificate without its base field, representation, precision, and a
verifiable residual or congruence bound (Chapter 39).

**Two kinds of trees.** A Berkovich tree uses disc seminorms; a Bruhat–Tits
tree uses homothety classes of lattices. Similar drawings do not identify their
vertices (Chapters 23 and 40).

**Two kinds of representations.** Smooth representations of $GL_2(K)$ use open
vector stabilizers, while $p$-adic Galois representations use continuity into a
$p$-adic matrix group (Chapters 26, 33, and 41).

**Hecke normalization.** Adjacency, normalized Satake operators, and Haar-scaled
indicators have rescaled eigenvalues. State the convention before comparing them
(Chapter 41).

**Étale terminology.** Finite étale algebras, étale cohomology, and étale
$(\varphi,\Gamma)$-modules are related but different structures (Chapters 37
and 42).

**Igusa indexing.** Tail volumes $A_m$, exact masses $p_m$, and multiplicative
Tate shells have different generating functions (Chapters 22 and 43).

**Height normalization.** A $p$-adic height depends on logarithm and splitting
choices and has no positivity. A determinant ball containing zero does not
certify regulator vanishing (Chapter 44).

**Language dependence.** Quantifier elimination and definability are statements
in a named first-order language. Uniformity may exclude finitely many small
primes (Chapter 45).

**Almost mathematics.** "Almost zero" means annihilated by a named ideal; it
does not mean numerically negligible (Chapter 32).

**Computed digits.** Output precision is a theorem-backed residue ball. More
printed digits are not evidence when cancellation or division has lost
precision (Appendix F).

These are dependency shortcuts, not prerequisite lists. A ground-up reader can
follow the concise primer and numbered chapters in order; every advanced chapter
identifies and repairs the specific background it reuses just in time.

## Executable Python route

The [exact $p$-adic Python laboratory](appendix-h-python-laboratory.md) begins
with valuations and digit lists, then develops certified reconstruction,
carries, inverse lifting, Hensel lifting, exhaustive roots modulo $p^N$,
ultrametric matrices, Mahler differences, Legendre valuations, linear
congruences, logarithm and exponential ledgers, Newton polygons, and singular
root counts. Five further formula-to-code studies cover geometric-series
precision, exponential-term valuations, Teichmüller lifting, Haar ball measures,
and finite residue dynamics. All twenty programs use exact integers for every
certificate and make prime, precision, and singular-root assumptions explicit.
