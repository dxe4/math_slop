
# Van Kampen as a Pushout

## The theorem statement

Let $X$ be a topological space written as $X=U \cup V$ where $U$, $V$, and $U \cap V$ are open and path-connected. Let $x_0 \in U \cap V$ be a basepoint. The **Seifert--van Kampen theorem** states that $\pi_1(X,x_0)$ is the pushout of the diagram

$$\pi_1(U \cap V,x_0) \xrightarrow{i_{U*}} \pi_1(U,x_0) \qquad \pi_1(U \cap V,x_0) \xrightarrow{i_{V*}} \pi_1(V,x_0)$$

in the category of groups. Concretely, this means

$$\pi_1(X,x_0) \cong \pi_1(U,x_0) *_{\pi_1(U \cap V,x_0)} \pi_1(V,x_0),$$

where the right-hand side is the **amalgamated free product**: start with the free product $\pi_1(U) * \pi_1(V)$ and impose the relations $i_{U*}(g)=i_{V*}(g)$ for all $g \in \pi_1(U \cap V)$.

The hypotheses are essential. If $U \cap V$ is not path-connected, loops in different components of the intersection cannot be identified, and the theorem fails in this form. The fundamental groupoid version, which tracks basepoints in all components, is the correct generalization.

## Worked example 1: the torus

Consider the torus $T^2$ as a CW complex with one 0-cell $v$, two oriented 1-cells $a$ and $b$, and one 2-cell attached along $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$. Let $U$ be the complement of a point in the interior of the 2-cell, and $V$ a small disk in the interior of the 2-cell. Then $U \cap V$ is an annulus, homotopy equivalent to $S^1$.

The space $U$ deformation retracts onto the 1-skeleton, which is $S^1 \vee S^1$, so $\pi_1(U) \cong \langle a,b \rangle$, the free group on two generators. The space $V$ is contractible, so $\pi_1(V)=0$. The intersection $U \cap V$ has $\pi_1(U \cap V) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the loop $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ that bounds the 2-cell.

Applying van Kampen, $\pi_1(T^2)$ is the free group on $a,b$ modulo the normal closure of $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$. This is the presentation

$$\pi_1(T^2) \cong \langle a,b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1}=e \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}^2.$$

The relation says $a$ and $b$ commute, giving the free abelian group of rank 2.

## Worked example 2: the projective plane

The real projective plane $\mathbb{RP}^2$ has a CW structure with one 0-cell, one 1-cell $a$, and one 2-cell attached by the map $z \mapsto z^2$ on $S^1$, i.e., along $a^2$. Let $U$ be the complement of a point in the 2-cell and $V$ a disk in the 2-cell interior. Then $U$ deformation retracts onto the 1-skeleton $S^1$, so $\pi_1(U) \cong \langle a \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$. The intersection $U \cap V$ has $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$, with generator mapping to $a^2$ in $\pi_1(U)$.

Van Kampen gives

$$\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) \cong \langle a \mid a^2=e \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.$$

This group has torsion, unlike the torus. Attaching maps matter: two spaces with the same number of cells can have different invariants.

## Wedge sums

For based spaces $(X,x_0)$ and $(Y,y_0)$, the **wedge sum** $X \vee Y$ is the quotient of $X \sqcup Y$ identifying $x_0 \sim y_0$. If small neighborhoods of the basepoints deformation retract onto the basepoints, van Kampen with $U$ a neighborhood of $X$ and $V$ a neighborhood of $Y$ gives

$$\pi_1(X \vee Y) \cong \pi_1(X) * \pi_1(Y),$$

the free product. For example, $\pi_1(S^1 \vee S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z} = F_2$.

**Common error:** applying the wedge formula without the neighborhood deformation retraction hypothesis. The Hawaiian earring is the subspace union of tangent circles whose radii shrink to zero; its topology is not the CW wedge topology on countably many circles. It does not satisfy this hypothesis, and its fundamental group is not the free product of countably many copies of $\mathbb Z$.

## Cell attachments

Let $X$ be path-connected and attach an $n$-cell along $f:S^{n-1} \to X$. For $n \geq 3$, the inclusion $X \hookrightarrow X \cup_f e^n$ induces an isomorphism on $\pi_1$ because $S^{n-1}$ is simply connected. For $n=2$, the effect is to kill the normal closure of $[f]$ in $\pi_1(X)$.

**Proposition 4.1.** If $X$ is a connected CW complex, then $\pi_1(X) \cong \pi_1(X^{(2)})$, where $X^{(2)}$ is the 2-skeleton.

**Proof.** For a finite sequence of attachments, apply the preceding attachment calculation successively. For an arbitrary CW complex, use relative cellular approximation, stated with its source in the CW chapter: every loop is homotopic into the 1-skeleton and every homotopy between such loops is homotopic relative its boundary into the 2-skeleton. These give surjectivity and injectivity respectively. This uses the geometric approximation theorem, not an unjustified infinite succession of finite computations. $\square$

This reduces fundamental group computations to the 2-skeleton, a powerful simplification.

## Common errors

**Error: forgetting the normality hypothesis.** When attaching a 2-cell along $f$, the resulting fundamental group is $\pi_1(X)/\langle\langle [f] \rangle\rangle$, where $\langle\langle [f] \rangle\rangle$ is the **normal closure** of $[f]$, the smallest normal subgroup containing $[f]$. The subgroup generated by $[f]$ alone need not be normal.

**Error: applying van Kampen when the intersection is not path-connected.** If $U \cap V$ has multiple components, loops in different components cannot be canonically identified. The fundamental groupoid version tracks all basepoints and gives the correct general statement.

**Error: assuming van Kampen gives abelian groups.** The amalgamated free product of abelian groups need not be abelian. The free product $\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ is nonabelian, even though both factors are abelian.

## Worked example 3: the Klein bottle

The Klein bottle $K$ has a CW structure with one 0-cell, two 1-cells $a,b$, and one 2-cell attached along $aba^{-1}b$. Applying van Kampen as for the torus gives

$$\pi_1(K) \cong \langle a,b \mid aba^{-1}b=e \rangle = \langle a,b \mid ab=b^{-1}a \rangle.$$

This group is nonabelian. For an explicit witness, let $b$ act on $\mathbb R$ by $b(x)=x+1$ and let $a(x)=-x$. Then $aba^{-1}=b^{-1}$, so this action respects the presentation, while $ab(0)=-1$ and $ba(0)=1$. More precisely, $\langle b\rangle\cong\mathbb Z$ is normal and conjugation by $a$ sends $b$ to $b^{-1}$, so the group is $\mathbb Z\rtimes\mathbb Z$ with the $a$-factor acting by inversion on the $b$-factor. Its abelianization is $\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: imposing $ab=ba$ gives $b=b^{-1}$, hence $b^2=e$.

## A complete proof of the two-open-set theorem

The algebraic target is the group

$$P=(\pi_1(U,x_0)*\pi_1(V,x_0))/N,$$

where $N$ is the normal subgroup generated by all words $i_{U*}(a)i_{V*}(a)^{-1}$ for $a\in\pi_1(U\cap V,x_0)$. Both inclusions into $X$ give homomorphisms into $\pi_1(X,x_0)$ and agree on intersection loops, so they define a homomorphism $\Phi:P\to\pi_1(X,x_0)$. We shall construct an inverse. The definition of $P$ does not require either intersection homomorphism to be injective. In particular, the phrase amalgamated free product here names a pushout even when a geometric inclusion kills some loop classes.

For every point $x\in X$, choose a path $\lambda_x$ from $x_0$ to $x$ as follows. If $x\in U\cap V$, choose it in the intersection. If $x$ lies only in $U$, choose it in $U$, and similarly for $V$. Let $\lambda_{x_0}$ be constant. Such choices exist by the path-connectedness hypotheses; making all choices at once is a use of ordinary set-theoretic choice. Only finitely many are used in any single calculation. The paths are bookkeeping devices, not a claimed continuous assignment depending on $x$.

For a path segment $\gamma:[a,b]\to W$, where $W$ is either $U$ or $V$, define its contribution to be the image in $P$ of the based loop

$$\lambda_{\gamma(a)}*\gamma*\bar\lambda_{\gamma(b)}$$

in $W$. Both connecting paths lie in $W$ by the rule above. If the whole segment lies in the intersection, the contribution is independent of whether its label is $U$ or $V$, by the defining relations of $P$.

**Lemma 4.2 (Subdivision).** A path has a finite subdivision into segments each lying in $U$ or $V$. The ordered product of their contributions is independent of subdivision and labels.

*Proof.* The inverse images of $U,V$ are an open cover of the compact parameter interval. A Lebesgue number for this cover gives a finite subdivision with each closed subinterval contained in one inverse-image set. If one segment is subdivided at a point $z$, its two contributions multiply to the original contribution because the adjacent paths $\bar\lambda_z*\lambda_z$ cancel relative endpoints in the same open set. Changing the label of an unchanged segment is legitimate only when that segment lies in both open sets, in which case the intersection relation gives equality. Any two subdivisions have a common refinement. On that refinement a segment that had different labels in the two original subdivisions lies in both sets. The two preceding observations therefore compare all the contributions and prove the assertion. ∎

For a based loop $\gamma$, write $\Psi(\gamma)$ for this product. The constant loop contributes the identity, reversal gives the inverse product, and concatenating two based loops concatenates their products. The remaining issue is independence of the based homotopy class. An invariance claim based only on the interval subdivision would be incomplete: a homotopy moves entire two-dimensional families of paths.

**Lemma 4.3 (Grid).** If $H:I^2\to X$ is a based path homotopy, the products for its bottom and top edges agree in $P$.

*Proof.* Pull back the open cover to the compact metric square. Choose a rectangular grid fine enough that the image of each closed small rectangle lies in $U$ or $V$. Give every oriented grid edge its contribution, using its image path and the globally chosen connecting paths. If the two adjacent rectangles have different labels, the edge image lies in their intersection. Its contribution is therefore the same from either rectangle. Reversing an edge inverts its contribution.

For each rectangle all its boundary paths, including the connecting paths at its vertices, lie in one chosen open set. The product of its four edge contributions is the identity because the boundary map extends over the rectangle; the connecting paths cancel and the boundary loop contracts inside that rectangle's image. Equivalently, the product along its lower edge followed by its right edge equals the product along its left edge followed by its upper edge, with coherent orientations. Sweep a monotone path across the grid one rectangle at a time, replacing those two-edge products. Each replacement preserves the ordered product. This proves equality between the bottom-then-right outer route and the left-then-top outer route. No factors are commuted, so the argument is valid in nonabelian groups. On the two vertical outer edges the homotopy is the constant basepoint path, whose contributions are identities. The bottom and top products are therefore equal. ∎

The grid lemma makes $\Psi$ a well-defined homomorphism from $\pi_1(X,x_0)$ to $P$. Applying $\Phi$ to a subdivided product cancels every neighboring connecting path, leaving the original loop class in $X$. Thus $\Phi\Psi$ is the identity. Conversely, if a based loop lies wholly in $U$ or wholly in $V$, take a one-piece subdivision. The product is exactly its canonical image in $P$, because the connecting path at the basepoint is constant. Since these two images generate $P$, the composite $\Psi\Phi$ is the identity. This proves the theorem in full. The same proof shows that the final isomorphism is canonical even though the temporary paths were chosen: its inverse is determined by its values on the two generating groups.

## What the pushout assertion means

**Result 4.1 (Universal property).** Suppose homomorphisms $a:\pi_1(U)\to G$ and $b:\pi_1(V)\to G$ agree after composition with the two intersection maps. There is a unique homomorphism $c:\pi_1(X)\to G$ whose restrictions are $a$ and $b$.

*Proof.* The free product admits a unique homomorphism extending $a$ and $b$. Every defining generator of $N$ is sent to the identity because the intersection images agree, so this homomorphism descends to $P$. Compose with the inverse of $\Phi$. Uniqueness follows because $P$ is generated by the two factor images. ∎

This property is often a better description than a presentation. A presentation requires generators and relations for the factor groups and the two images of every relevant intersection generator. Forgetting one of the two maps loses information. For example, the intersection of the punctured-cell neighborhood and the disk in a surface is an annulus. Its abstract group is always infinite cyclic. The torus and projective-plane answers differ because the generator maps to a commutator in one case and a square in the other.

## Result 4.2: attaching a disk adds a normal relation

Let $X$ be a path-connected CW complex, and attach a disk along a loop $f:S^1\to X$. Choose a path in $X$ from the fixed basepoint to the image of the marked boundary point. The induced based attaching word is defined using that path. Changing the path conjugates the word, so its normal closure is independent of the choice.

To apply the open-set theorem, use the complement $U$ of the disk center in the attachment space and an open smaller disk $V$ around that center. Radial motion in the punctured characteristic disk gives a deformation retraction of $U$ to $X$, compatible with the boundary identifications because points already on the boundary remain fixed. The overlap is an annulus. Its generator maps to the attaching word in $X$ and to the identity in $V$. The pushout consequently identifies the whole normal closure of that word with the identity. It cannot impose only the cyclic subgroup generated by the word, because a kernel is always normal.

For an $n$-cell with $n\ge3$, the overlap retracts to $S^{n-1}$. The relevant sphere is simply connected, so both no new generator and no new relation are contributed to the existing fundamental group. The same radial argument proves this assertion for a single attachment. Passing to an infinite CW complex uses the approximation theorem described above, rather than assuming a loop or a homotopy finishes after an unexplained infinite list of cell operations.

## Worked example 4: why spheres of dimension at least two are simply connected

For $n\ge2$, let $U$ and $V$ be $S^n$ with the north and south poles respectively removed. Each is homeomorphic to $\mathbb R^n$ by stereographic projection and therefore contractible. Their intersection is homeomorphic to $S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R$ and is path connected because $n-1\ge1$. The pushout of the two trivial groups over any group is trivial, so $\pi_1(S^n)=0$. We did not need to assume that the intersection itself was simply connected. For $n=2$ it is an annulus with nontrivial fundamental group, yet the theorem still gives the trivial group because both inclusion-induced maps have trivial targets.

For $S^1$, the analogous intersection has two components. The theorem in the form proved here is unavailable. Applying it anyway would falsely predict a trivial circle group. This is a compact nonexample that isolates the exact hypothesis, rather than treating every failure of a gluing calculation as a mysterious property of the spaces.

Compute $\pi_1$ of the space obtained by attaching a 2-cell to $S^1 \vee S^1$ along the word $a^2b^3$.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Keep the attaching word as a relation.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Map the generators to integers so that the relation has image zero.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** The fundamental group is $\langle a,b \mid a^2b^3=e \rangle$. It is infinite because the assignment $a\mapsto3$, $b\mapsto-2$ respects the relation $2(3)+3(-2)=0$ and defines a surjection onto $\mathbb Z$. Thus the presentation is nontrivial without requiring us to identify the one-relator group more explicitly. $\square$

</details>

### Exercise 3.2
<a id="van-kampen-as-a-pushout-ex-2"></a>

Show that $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2 \vee \mathbb{RP}^2)$ is infinite.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use the free-product normal form.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The alternating word never cancels.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** By the wedge formula, $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2 \vee \mathbb{RP}^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. This free product is infinite: the element $ab$ has infinite order, where $a$ and $b$ are the generators of the two $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ factors. Indeed, $(ab)^n$ cannot equal the identity for $n>0$ because reduced words in a free product have unique normal forms. $\square$

</details>

### Exercise 3.3
<a id="van-kampen-as-a-pushout-ex-3"></a>

Let $X$ be the complement of $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Compute $\pi_1(X)$.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Choose disjoint small disks around the punctures and connecting arcs.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

Retract the resulting planar region to a graph.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** By deformation retraction, $X$ is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of $n$ circles. Therefore $\pi_1(X) \cong F_n$, the free group on $n$ generators. Each generator corresponds to a loop encircling one of the punctures. $\square$

</details>

### Exercise 3.4
<a id="van-kampen-as-a-pushout-ex-4"></a>

Prove that if $X$ is a connected CW complex and $f:S^1 \to X$ is any map, then attaching a 2-cell along $f$ kills the normal closure of $[f]$ in $\pi_1(X)$.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Choose open sets adapted to the characteristic disk.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The disk kills its boundary word and therefore all its conjugates.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

**Solution.** Write $Y=X \cup_f e^2$. Let $U$ be the complement of the center of the 2-cell and $V$ the interior of the 2-cell. Then $U \cap V$ is homotopy equivalent to $S^1$, with generator mapping to $[f]$ in $\pi_1(U) \cong \pi_1(X)$. Van Kampen gives $\pi_1(Y) \cong \pi_1(X)/\langle\langle [f] \rangle\rangle$. $\square$

</details>

### Exercise 3.7
<a id="van-kampen-as-a-pushout-ex-7"></a>

Explain exactly why covering the circle by two open arcs cannot prove that its group is trivial using the two-set theorem.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Inspect the intersection rather than just the two arcs.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The intersection has more than one path component.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

Two proper arcs that cover the circle overlap in two disjoint arcs. Their intersection is not path connected, so there is no single family of connecting paths inside the intersection as required in the proof. The theorem does not apply. A groupoid calculation or the earlier winding argument gives the correct infinite cyclic group.

</details>

### Exercise 3.8
<a id="van-kampen-as-a-pushout-ex-8"></a>

Give an example where the intersection maps in van Kampen are not injective.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use the two-pole cover of the two-sphere.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The overlap retracts to a circle, while both pieces are contractible.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

For $S^2$, the punctured-sphere open sets each have trivial group, but their annular overlap has group $\mathbb Z$. Both intersection maps are zero and are not injective. The pushout theorem remains valid because it was formulated as a quotient, not as a statement that an intersection subgroup embeds in both factors.

</details>

### Exercise 3.9
<a id="van-kampen-as-a-pushout-ex-9"></a>

Compute the abelianization of $\langle a,b\mid a^2b^3=1\rangle$.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Abelianization replaces words by exponent sums.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The vector $(2,3)$ is primitive in $\mathbb Z^2$.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The answer is $\mathbb Z^2/\langle(2,3)\rangle\cong\mathbb Z$. The homomorphism $(m,n)\mapsto3m-2n$ is surjective and has kernel generated by $(2,3)$: $3m=2n$ implies $m=2k,n=3k$. Thus no torsion remains in the abelianization.

</details>

### Exercise 3.10
<a id="van-kampen-as-a-pushout-ex-10"></a>

Explain why attaching a three-cell to a path-connected CW complex leaves its fundamental group unchanged.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>

Apply the cell-attachment open cover.

</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>

The overlap retracts to the simply connected sphere $S^2$.

</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>

The complement of the cell center retracts to the old complex, the interior disk is contractible, and the overlap retracts to $S^2$. Its fundamental group is trivial, so the pushout is the old group. The induced isomorphism is the one supplied by inclusion of the old complex, not just an abstract group coincidence.

</details>

## Summary

Van Kampen identifies the group of a suitable union as a pushout, with both intersection homomorphisms retained. Interval subdivision generates the group; a grid subordinate to a homotopy proves that the construction is independent of choices. Attaching a two-cell kills a normal closure, while cells of dimension at least three leave the fundamental group unchanged. Open sets, path-connected intersections and basepoint paths are hypotheses used in the proof, not optional features of a picture. Continue to [coverings and monodromy](coverings-and-monodromy.md).
