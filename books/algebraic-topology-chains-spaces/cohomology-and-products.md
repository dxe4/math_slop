
# Cohomology and Products

## Cohomology

For a space $X$ and coefficient ring $R$, the **singular cochain complex** is $C^k(X;R)=\operatorname{Hom}_R(C_k(X;R),R)$. The **coboundary** $\delta^k:C^k(X;R) \to C^{k+1}(X;R)$ is the dual of the boundary:

$$(\delta \varphi)(\sigma) = \varphi(d\sigma).$$

Since $d^2=0$, we have $\delta^2=0$. The **cohomology** is

$$H^k(X;R) = \ker \delta^k / \operatorname{im} \delta^{k-1}.$$

A continuous map $f:X \to Y$ induces $f^*:H^k(Y;R) \to H^k(X;R)$ by precomposition: $(f^*\varphi)(\sigma)=\varphi(f_\#\sigma)$. Cohomology is contravariantly functorial.

## The cup product

For a commutative ring $R$, the **cup product** is a map

$$\smile: H^p(X;R) \times H^q(X;R) \to H^{p+q}(X;R)$$

defined at the cochain level by

$$(\varphi \smile \psi)(\sigma) = \varphi(\sigma|_{[v_0,\ldots,v_p]}) \cdot \psi(\sigma|_{[v_p,\ldots,v_{p+q}]}).$$

This makes $H^*(X;R)$ into a **graded ring**. The cup product is **graded-commutative**:

$$\alpha \smile \beta = (-1)^{pq} \beta \smile \alpha$$

for $\alpha \in H^p(X;R)$ and $\beta \in H^q(X;R)$.

## The torus vs. the wedge

The torus $T^2$ and the wedge $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$ have isomorphic integral homology groups: $H_0=\mathbb{Z}$, $H_1=\mathbb{Z}^2$, $H_2=\mathbb{Z}$. Their integral cohomology groups are also isomorphic additively. However, their cup product structures differ.

For $T^2$, let $a,b \in H^1(T^2;\mathbb{Z})$ be the duals of the meridional and longitudinal cycles. Then $a \smile b$ generates $H^2(T^2;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, while $a \smile a = b \smile b = 0$ by graded-commutativity (since $(-1)^{1 \cdot 1}=-1$, so $a \smile a = -a \smile a$, hence $2(a \smile a)=0$, and torsion-freeness gives $a \smile a=0$).

For $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$, the cohomology ring is generated by $a,b \in H^1$ and $c \in H^2$ with all products of positive-degree classes from different wedge summands equal to 0. In particular, $a \smile b = 0$ because $a$ and $b$ live in different $S^1$ summands. Thus $T^2$ and $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$ are not homotopy equivalent, even though their homology groups agree.

## The universal coefficient theorem

For any space $X$ and abelian group $G$, there is a natural short exact sequence

$$0\to \operatorname{Ext}^1_{\mathbb Z}(H_{k-1}(X;\mathbb Z),G)\to H^k(X;G)\to
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(H_k(X;\mathbb Z),G)\to0.$$

It splits as a sequence of abelian groups, but not naturally in $X$. Merely choosing a field as the target group does not kill the integral Ext term: for example, $\operatorname{Ext}^1_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z/2,\mathbb F_2)\cong\mathbb F_2$. Instead, if chains and cochains are taken over a field $\mathbb F$, linear algebra gives

$$H^k(X;\mathbb F)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb F}(H_k(X;\mathbb F),\mathbb F).$$

This identifies cohomology with the dual vector space, not canonically with homology itself; for infinite-dimensional spaces even their vector-space dimensions need not agree.

## Common errors

**Error: assuming cohomology is just the dual of homology.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$, $H^k(X;\mathbb{Z})$ is not simply $\operatorname{Hom}(H_k(X;\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Z})$. For a finite CW complex, or more generally when $H_{k-1}(X;\mathbb Z)$ is finitely generated, each finite cyclic torsion summand of $H_{k-1}(X;\mathbb Z)$ contributes an isomorphic torsion summand through $\operatorname{Ext}^1_{\mathbb Z}(-,\mathbb Z)$. Without finite-generation hypotheses, no such blanket implication from torsion in homology to torsion in cohomology is valid.

**Error: forgetting graded-commutativity.** The cup product satisfies $\alpha \smile \beta = (-1)^{pq}\beta \smile \alpha$. For odd-degree classes, this implies only $2(\alpha \smile \alpha)=0$. The square vanishes over a field of characteristic different from $2$, or over $\mathbb Z$ when the target group has no 2-torsion; in integral cohomology it can otherwise be a nonzero 2-torsion class. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, graded-commutativity is ordinary commutativity, and $\alpha \smile \alpha$ may be nonzero.

**Error: computing cup products on the wrong representative.** The cup product is defined on cohomology classes, not cochains. One must verify that the product of cocycles is a cocycle and that changing by a coboundary does not affect the cohomology class.

## Worked example: $\mathbb{RP}^n$ with $\mathbb{F}_2$ coefficients

The cohomology ring $H^*(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb{F}_2)$ is a truncated polynomial algebra:

$$H^*(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb{F}_2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^{n+1}),$$

where $x \in H^1(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb{F}_2)$. The generator $x$ satisfies $x^k \neq 0$ for $k \leq n$ and $x^{n+1}=0$. This ring structure encodes the fact that $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is obtained by attaching cells in increasing dimension with nontrivial attaching maps.

## The chain identities behind the ring

The definitions above require proofs before they descend to cohomology. We work over a commutative coefficient ring R. For a singular simplex sigma, the notation sigma[i,...,j] means restriction to that ordered face with its induced parametrization, not an independently chosen simplex.

### Theorem 14.1 — Leibniz, associativity, and naturality

For alpha of degree p and beta of degree q,
$\delta(\alpha\smile\beta)
=\delta\alpha\smile\beta+(-1)^p\alpha\smile\delta\beta.$
The cup operation is associative on cochains, has the constant-one degree-zero cocycle as unit, and commutes with pullback.

*Proof.* Evaluate the proposed Leibniz identity on an ordered (p+q+1)-simplex. Expand its boundary as the alternating sum of deleted vertices. Deleting a vertex among the first p+1 positions contributes to the coboundary of the front alpha face. Deleting one after the shared vertex contributes to the back beta face with the factor (-1)^p. The two terms at the shared transition cancel. This leaves exactly the two displayed sums, with no unpaired face.

For associativity, both (alpha cup beta) cup gamma and alpha cup (beta cup gamma) evaluate on the three consecutive faces
$[0,\ldots,p]$, $[p,\ldots,p+q]$, and $[p+q,\ldots,p+q+r]$.
Multiplication in R is associative. The unit evaluates to one at the initial or terminal vertex. Finally restriction to a face commutes with composition by a continuous map f, proving f*(alpha cup beta)=f*alpha cup f*beta. $\square$

If alpha and beta are cocycles, Leibniz makes their product a cocycle. If alpha changes by delta u while beta is closed, its product changes by delta(u cup beta); changing beta gives a coboundary with the corresponding sign. This establishes independence of representatives. The same argument, with one variable at a time, handles both changing simultaneously.

## Why commutativity holds only after passing to classes

Define the diagonal chain map
$\Delta(\sigma)=\sum_{p=0}^n
\sigma[0,\ldots,p]\otimes\sigma[p,\ldots,n].$
It is Alexander–Whitney applied to the geometric diagonal. Expanding its tensor boundary gives Delta partial: internal deleted-face terms pair across adjacent split positions, and the remaining outer terms are precisely the faces of sigma. Thus it is a chain map.

On homogeneous tensors let T(a tensor b)=(-1)^{|a||b|}b tensor a. The tensor differential verifies T is a chain map. Both Delta and T Delta preserve augmentation.

**Theorem 14.2 (graded commutativity).**
$[\alpha]\smile[\beta]=(-1)^{pq}[\beta]\smile[\alpha].$

*Proof.* Use the filling principle proved in [Coefficients and Kunneth](coefficients-and-kunneth.md). On a universal n-simplex, the two diagonal maps land in
$C_*(\Delta^n)\otimes C_*(\Delta^n)$,
an augmented-contractible complex. Their face restrictions are compatible. Induction therefore constructs a natural degree-one map K with
$\Delta-T\Delta=\partial K+K\partial.$
The fillers are chosen in the universal standard model and then pushed forward; no contractibility of the simplex image is assumed.

For cocycles alpha,beta, the tensor functional alpha tensor beta kills tensor boundaries. Evaluate the chain-homotopy identity on it. The partial K term vanishes, and the K partial term is the coboundary of the degree-(p+q-1) cochain (alpha tensor beta)K. The left side is alpha cup beta minus (-1)^{pq} beta cup alpha. Their difference is therefore a coboundary, proving the claim. $\square$

The operation is generally not graded-commutative as a literal cochain formula. The chain homotopy is the mechanism that removes the discrepancy on cohomology. For odd-degree alpha, graded commutativity gives 2alpha²=0, not automatically alpha²=0.

## Cap products with all signs specified

For a p-cochain alpha and an n-simplex sigma, n>=p, define
$\alpha\frown\sigma
=\alpha(\sigma[0,\ldots,p])\,\sigma[p,\ldots,n],$
and extend linearly, with value zero when n<p. This is the convention used in the duality chapter.

### Theorem 14.3 — Cap boundary and pairing identities

For a homogeneous p-cochain and chain c,
$\partial(\alpha\frown c)
=(-1)^p\bigl(\alpha\frown\partial c-(\delta\alpha)\frown c\bigr).$
Moreover,
$(\alpha\smile\beta)\frown c
=\beta\frown(\alpha\frown c),$
and
$\langle\beta,\alpha\frown c\rangle
=\langle\alpha\smile\beta,c\rangle$
when the degrees match.

*Proof.* Expand alpha cap partial sigma using the alternating deleted-vertex faces. The terms deleting one of the first p+1 vertices match the coboundary expansion of delta alpha on the initial (p+1)-face. Subtracting cancels them. The remaining terms are the boundary faces of the back simplex, with the common factor (-1)^p, giving the first identity.

For the second, the first cap removes the front p-face and the second removes the following q-face. Both sides multiply alpha on [0,...,p] and beta on [p,...,p+q], leaving [p+q,...,n]. Commutativity of scalar multiplication allows the written order. Evaluating the remaining chain by beta is exactly the same front/back expression, proving the pairing identity. $\square$

If alpha is closed and c a cycle, the first formula makes alpha cap c a cycle. Replacing c by a boundary changes it by a boundary; replacing alpha by a coboundary also does so after the displayed sign is used. Hence cap descends to the stated homology-cohomology operation.

Naturality follows directly from faces:
$f_\#(f^*\alpha\frown c)=\alpha\frown f_\#c.$
This is the identity required when comparing local fundamental classes, not a claim that homology and cohomology have the same variance.

## A complete mod-two projective ring model

The ring calculation for RPn retained above needs more than knowing its additive cellular groups. Here is a separate construction of the multiplication, independent of the Borsuk–Ulam proof.

Let G={1,t}. Its simplicial classifying model BG has normalized n-simplices [g1|...|gn] with every gi nonidentity. For G of order two there is exactly one such word in each degree: all entries are t. In its boundary, an internal face multiplies adjacent t entries into 1 and is degenerate; the two end faces are equal and cancel over F2. Thus its normalized F2 chain differential is zero.

The front/back diagonal splits a word into its first p entries and last q entries. If x is the degree-one cochain taking value one on [t], its kth cup power takes value one on the unique degree-k word. It is therefore a nonzero generator in every degree. This proves H*(BG;F2)=F2[x], including the multiplication.

To identify this model with RP infinity, the homogeneous model EG has simplices (g0,...,gn). Inserting 1 at the front is an extra degeneracy that contracts it; its geometric realization is contractible and the diagonal G-action is free. On the other hand the infinite sphere S infinity is contractible. One explicit homotopy moves a finite-support unit vector to its coordinate shift by normalized straight interpolation, which never vanishes because a finite-support vector cannot be a negative scalar multiple of its shift. The shifted sphere is perpendicular to e0 and contracts to e0 by another normalized interpolation.

Both are free contractible G-CW spaces. Construct equivariant maps between them by extending on one cell from each free orbit; every boundary map extends into a contractible target. The same procedure extends homotopies between composites and identities. Descending gives BG homotopy equivalent to RP infinity. This proves the identification rather than inferring the ring from cell counts.

The finite RPn is its n-skeleton. All mod-two cellular differentials are zero, so restriction to that skeleton is an isomorphism in each degree at most n and the higher groups vanish. Naturality carries x and its powers to the finite skeleton. Consequently
$H^*(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb F_2)
=\mathbb F_2[x]/(x^{n+1}).$
The simplicial/normalized chain comparison used here removes degenerate simplices by their standard degeneracy contraction; equivalently one may use the simplicial CW realization, whose normalized simplices are its cells. The cellular theorem supplies that homology comparison.

The familiar CP2 ring in legacy Exercise 9.4 is an additional standard projective-space cup calculation, cited to Hatcher's cohomology chapter. Its conclusion is retained; we do not claim that the additive cell counts alone prove the nonzero square.

## Worked examples with multiplication visible

### Worked example 1 — A torus cellular diagonal

For the interval, a cellular approximation to the diagonal runs along two sides of its square, with chain expression v0 tensor e+e tensor v1. It is homotopic to the geometric diagonal relative endpoints by linear interpolation inside the square. Quotient endpoints to form a circle, giving Delta a=v tensor a+a tensor v.

For the product of two circles, tensor the two expressions and reorder factors with the tensor sign. The two-cell c has diagonal
$\Delta c=v\otimes c+a\otimes b-b\otimes a+c\otimes v.$
Thus dual one-cochains a*,b* satisfy (a* cup b*)(c)=1 and (b* cup a*)(c)=-1. Their product generates top cohomology. This supplies the actual multiplication used in the torus comparison.

### Worked example 2 — Why the wedge product vanishes

For W=S1 wedge S1 wedge S2, restriction $H^2(W)\to H^2(S^2)$ is an isomorphism, by the wedge chain decomposition. The two degree-one classes restrict to zero on S2. Hence their cup product restricts to zero there by naturality, and the isomorphism forces it to vanish in W. On each circle there is no degree-two group either. The product difference from the torus is therefore a ring obstruction, not an additive-group obstruction.

### Worked example 3 — A cochain discrepancy that disappears only for classes

On an ordered triangle choose alpha to evaluate one on edge 01 and zero on 12 and 02, and beta one on 12 and zero on 01 and 02. Then (alpha cup beta)(012)=1, while (beta cup alpha)(012)=0. These particular cochains are not cocycles: their coboundaries evaluate nontrivially on the triangle. The example makes it impossible to interpret graded commutativity as an automatic pointwise equality of the defining formulas.

### Worked example 4 — Torsion in integral cohomology

For RP2, H1=Z/2 and H2=0. Integral UCT gives H1 cohomology zero and H2 cohomology Z/2 through Ext. Over F2 the projective generator x has nonzero square in degree two. It is not obtained by squaring an integral degree-one generator, because no such nonzero integral generator exists. Coefficient changes affect both additive groups and their possible products.

## Using products to distinguish spaces

A homotopy equivalence has a homotopy inverse, and pullback preserves products. The prism chain homotopy from the earlier homotopy-invariance chapter dualizes to a cochain homotopy, so the two inverse composites induce identities on cohomology. This proves ring invariance directly, supplementing the UCT route in the retained exercise.

The cap pairing will identify the orientation class with complementary-dimensional chains in the next chapter. The order and signs already fixed here are carried through that proof; silently switching a cap convention would alter intermediate boundary formulas even though the final isomorphism remains true.

## Worked example — A torus map and its degree

The ring determines a two-dimensional effect from a one-dimensional calculation. Orient the torus by its two ordered circle factors. Let their oriented fundamental loops be $u,v$, and choose dual integral one-classes $a,b$. Fix the top generator $c=a\smile b$, which evaluates to one on the fundamental two-cycle by the cellular diagonal above.

Suppose a continuous self-map induces

$$f_*u=mu+nv,\qquad f_*v=pu+qv$$

on first homology, with integer coefficients. Evaluation is natural: evaluating a pulled-back cohomology class on a cycle equals evaluating the original class on the pushed-forward cycle. Consequently

$$f^*a=ma+pb,\qquad f^*b=na+qb.$$

Notice the positions of the coefficients. They follow from evaluating on each of the two loops, rather than guessing whether a matrix should be transposed. Since the two squares vanish and reversing the order of the one-classes changes sign, multiplication gives

$$f^*c=(mq-pn)c.$$

The degree of the map is the integer multiplying the top fundamental class under pushforward. Pairing with the top cohomology generator proves that this same integer multiplies that generator under pullback. Thus the degree is the determinant of the displayed action on first homology. This argument uses the ring and the evaluation pairing; the additive cohomology groups alone would not relate these two degrees.

Every integer matrix in this calculation actually occurs. View the torus as the real plane modulo the integer lattice and apply the linear map whose columns are the images of the two standard basis vectors. It sends integer vectors to integer vectors, so it descends to a continuous map on the quotient. Changing a representative by an integer vector changes its image by another integer vector, proving that the quotient formula is well-defined. Its action on the two coordinate loops is exactly the prescribed one.

For a concrete map, use columns $(2,1)$ and $(1,1)$. The determinant is one, and the inverse matrix again has integer entries. The quotient map is therefore a homeomorphism with a continuous inverse induced by that matrix. By contrast, doubling both coordinates has determinant four. It has four preimages of each point, obtained by choosing independently either half-angle lift in each circle factor. All local linear orientations are positive, in agreement with degree four. A matrix with zero determinant gives degree zero; it can collapse the torus onto a circle, so there is no contradiction with having a nontrivial action on first homology.

This calculation also strengthens the torus-versus-wedge obstruction. Any map from the wedge into the torus pulls both degree-one generators back to classes whose product is zero. Naturality forces the pullback of the torus top generator to be zero. Hence such a map cannot induce an isomorphism in degree two. One does not need to classify all maps between the spaces to obtain this obstruction. The particular multiplication relation already rules out the proposed behavior.

## Why field duality does not need a chosen basis

There is a useful elementary explanation of the field version of universal coefficients. Work with a chain complex of vector spaces over a field. A cocycle of degree $k$ is a linear functional on chains that kills boundaries in degree $k$. Restricting it to cycles therefore gives a well-defined functional on homology. If the cocycle changes by a coboundary, this restriction stays the same, since a coboundary evaluates on a cycle by applying a functional to its zero boundary. Thus restriction defines a canonical map from cohomology to the dual of homology.

To prove surjectivity, start with a functional on homology, lift it to a functional on the subspace of cycles that vanishes on boundaries, and extend that functional to all chains in degree $k$. Such an extension exists by extending a vector-space basis. Its vanishing on boundaries makes the resulting cochain a cocycle. The extension is not canonical, but its class maps to the originally specified functional.

For injectivity, suppose a cocycle restricts to zero on cycles. Define a functional on the image of the boundary map from degree $k$ by assigning to the boundary of a chain the value of the cocycle on that chain. This is well-defined: two chains with the same boundary differ by a cycle, on which the value is zero. Extend this functional to all chains in degree $k-1$. The coboundary of the extension is exactly the given cocycle. Hence its class was zero. Together these two arguments prove the canonical isomorphism with the dual homology space.

The proof exposes the coefficient hypothesis. Extending a functional from a subspace is automatic for vector spaces. For abelian groups, a homomorphism from a subgroup need not extend to the whole group: sending the subgroup of even integers to the integers by halving cannot extend to an integer-valued homomorphism on all integers. That failure is the source of the extra integral obstruction measured by the Ext term. Even over a field the argument concerns the linear dual, not an identification with the original vector space by an unspecified basis. Nor does it compute the cup product: the restriction construction uses the differential, whereas multiplication also uses the diagonal. The two kinds of information must remain distinct.

## Summary

The front/back formula satisfies a signed Leibniz rule, associativity, and naturality, so it defines a cohomology ring. A universal-model chain homotopy proves graded commutativity, which need not hold literally on cochains. Cap products obey the stated signed boundary and pairing identities. Explicit diagonals distinguish the torus from a wedge and compute the projective mod-two powers, preparing the duality proof.
