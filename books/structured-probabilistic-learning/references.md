
# References and Further Reading

This bibliography identifies standard comparison texts, historical sources, and current research directions. The book is written under CC BY-SA 4.0; standard definitions and probabilistic conventions belong to shared practice. References are included for further reading, historical context, alternative treatments, and broader coverage.

A named result or study is a retrieval convention, not a complete history. Probabilistic ideas are often the product of long development across communities; a modern statement may differ sharply from an early source.

## Gaussian processes and kernels

- Rasmussen, C. E., & Williams, C. K. I. (2006). *Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning*. MIT Press. The standard reference for GP regression, classification, and approximate inference; freely available at gaussianprocess.org/gpml.
- Stein, M. L. (1999). *Interpolation of Spatial Data: Some Theory for Kriging*. Springer. A rigorous treatment of covariance functions, kriging, and asymptotic theory.
- Wendland, H. (2004). *Scattered Data Approximation*. Cambridge University Press. A mathematical reference for positive-definite kernels and radial basis functions.

These texts use different notational conventions for kernel hyperparameters. Comparison is most useful when those conventions are recorded rather than blended.

## Graphical models and inference

- Koller, D., & Friedman, N. (2009). *Probabilistic Graphical Models: Principles and Techniques*. MIT Press. An encyclopedic treatment of directed and undirected models, exact and approximate inference, and learning.
- Murphy, K. P. (2012). *Machine Learning: A Probabilistic Perspective*. MIT Press. A broad text covering graphical models, Gaussian processes, and many related topics with unified notation.
- Wainwright, M. J., & Jordan, M. I. (2008). "Graphical models, exponential families, and variational inference." *Foundations and Trends in Machine Learning*, 1(1--2), 1--305. A self-contained monograph on variational methods and their information-geometric foundations.

The graphical-model literature varies in its treatment of directed versus undirected representations. This book treats both and emphasizes the computational consequences of each choice.

## Conditional random fields and structured prediction

- Sutton, C., & McCallum, A. (2012). "An introduction to conditional random fields." *Foundations and Trends in Machine Learning*, 4(4), 267--373. A tutorial treatment of CRFs, inference, and parameter estimation.
- Lafferty, J., McCallum, A., & Pereira, F. (2001). "Conditional random fields: Probabilistic models for segmenting and labeling sequence data." *Proceedings of the Eighteenth International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML)*, 282--289. The original CRF paper.

## Hidden Markov models and sequential inference

- Rabiner, L. R. (1989). "A tutorial on hidden Markov models and selected applications in speech recognition." *Proceedings of the IEEE*, 77(2), 257--286. The classic tutorial on HMMs, forward-backward, and Viterbi algorithms.
- Bishop, C. M. (2006). *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning*. Springer. Chapters 8 and 13 cover graphical models and sequential models with a unified variational perspective.

## Numerical linear algebra for kernel methods

- Golub, G. H., & Van Loan, C. F. (2013). *Matrix Computations* (4th ed.). Johns Hopkins University Press. The standard reference for Cholesky factorization, conditioning, and stable linear solvers.
- Higham, N. J. (2002). *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms* (2nd ed.). SIAM. A detailed treatment of roundoff error, jitter, and numerical stability.

## Structure learning and causality

- Spirtes, P., Glymour, C., & Scheines, R. (2000). *Causation, Prediction, and Search* (2nd ed.). MIT Press. A foundational text on constraint-based structure learning and causal discovery.
- Pearl, J. (2009). *Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference* (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press. The standard reference for causal inference, do-calculus, and structural equation models.

## Historical attribution checklist

Every historical or priority claim should record:

1. a classification as contemporary exposition, secondary history, translated primary source, or primary source;
2. verified author, title, edition, publisher or archive, and stable locator;
3. a note explaining whether the citation supports provenance, formulation, evidence, example, or broader context;
4. a check that modern terminology is not projected unchanged backward;
5. independent review of culturally broad claims and disputed priority.

## Reading-route recommendations

For a deeper treatment of Gaussian processes, proceed to Rasmussen and Williams. For kernel theory and approximation, Wendland provides the mathematical foundations. For graphical models, Koller and Friedman is the natural next step. For variational inference, Wainwright and Jordan treat the subject in depth. For structure learning and causality, Spirtes, Glymour, and Scheines cover constraint-based methods, while Pearl treats the causal framework. For numerical linear algebra, Golub and Van Loan is the standard reference.

## Citation policy for contributors

Paraphrase sources and cite the job they perform. Do not copy exercise sequences, distinctive examples, diagrams, or prose merely because a theorem is standard. Direct quotations require exact locators and license or fair-use review. When sources disagree, record the disagreement. When provenance is unknown, label it unknown rather than inventing a clean lineage.
