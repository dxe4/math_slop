
# A probability model is more than a fitted curve

## Purpose and prerequisites

This chapter introduces the central distinction between point prediction and probabilistic modeling. A point predictor returns a single number $\hat{f}(x)$ for each input $x$. A probability model returns a distribution over possible outcomes, and often a distribution over possible functions. The difference is not merely notational: it changes what questions can be asked, what diagnostics are available, and what errors are possible.

The chapter assumes familiarity with multivariate probability, Bayesian conditioning, and basic linear algebra. No prior exposure to Gaussian processes or graphical models is required.

## From point prediction to distributions

Consider a regression task with inputs $x \in \mathcal{X}$ and real-valued outputs $y \in \mathbb{R}$. The simplest probabilistic model is

$$Y = f(X) + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{2}).$$

This says that, for any fixed input $x$, the output $y$ is normally distributed around $f(x)$ with variance $\sigma^{2}$. The function $f$ may be known, parametric, or completely unknown. If $f$ is unknown, a Bayesian treatment places a prior over functions and computes a posterior after observing data.

**Definition (Probabilistic regression model).** A probabilistic regression model is a joint distribution $p(y, f \mid x)$ over outputs $y$ and latent quantities $f$, together with a likelihood $p(y \mid f, x)$ and a prior $p(f \mid x)$. After observing data $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_{i}, y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}$, the posterior $p(f \mid \mathcal{D})$ and the posterior predictive $p(y_{*} \mid x_{*}, \mathcal{D})$ are obtained by Bayes' theorem.

The posterior predictive distribution answers questions that a point predictor cannot. For example:

- What is the probability that $Y_{*}$ exceeds a threshold $t$?
- What is the expected loss under an asymmetric cost function?
- How much should we expect to learn if we observe an additional point?

A point predictor discards this structure. Even a predictor that reports a mean and a variance is only useful if those numbers come from a coherent probability model whose assumptions can be inspected.

## Three kinds of uncertainty

Every probabilistic model separates at least three conceptually distinct uncertainties. Only the first two appear in standard posterior formulas; the third is external to the model and must be addressed by other means.

**Observation noise (aleatoric uncertainty).** Under the likelihood $p(y \mid f, x)$, even the true function $f$ does not determine $y$ exactly. The variance $\sigma^{2}$ captures irreducible variation: measurement error, omitted variables, or genuinely stochastic behavior. This uncertainty remains even with infinite data.

**Latent-function uncertainty (epistemic uncertainty).** If $f$ is unknown, the posterior $p(f \mid \mathcal{D})$ reflects remaining ignorance about the function. As the data set grows and covers the input space more densely, this uncertainty typically concentrates. In regions far from any training data, the posterior remains broad.

**Model uncertainty.** The likelihood, the prior over functions, the noise model, and the input representation may all be wrong. A narrow posterior under a misspecified model can be confidently wrong. No amount of Bayesian conditioning inside a single model detects this problem automatically.

**Example (Confidently wrong).** Suppose data are generated by $y = \sin(2\pi x) + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.01)$, but the model assumes $f$ is a constant function with a tight prior around zero. With enough data in $[0, 1]$, the posterior over the constant will concentrate near zero with tiny variance, giving high confidence and systematically wrong predictions. The posterior variance reports uncertainty about the constant, not uncertainty about whether a constant is appropriate.

## Posterior and posterior predictive: a first derivation

To make the preceding concrete, consider a parametric model with a Gaussian prior on weights. Let $f(x)=w^{T}\phi(x)$ where $\phi:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R^d$ is fixed. Place a proper prior $w\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma_w)$ with $\Sigma_w$ positive definite, and assume $y\mid x,w\sim\mathcal N(w^T\phi(x),\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2>0$.

Let $\Phi$ be the $n \times d$ design matrix with rows $\phi(x_{i})^{T}$, and let $y \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ be the vector of observed outputs. The posterior over weights is Gaussian:

$$w \mid \mathcal{D} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{w}, \Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal{D}}),$$

where

$$\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal{D}} = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Phi^{T}\Phi + \Sigma_{w}^{-1}\right)^{-1}, \quad \mu_{w} = \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal{D}}\Phi^{T}y.$$

For a test input $x_{*}$, the posterior predictive distribution is

$$p(y_{*} \mid x_{*}, \mathcal{D}) = \int p(y_{*} \mid x_{*}, w)\,p(w \mid \mathcal{D})\,dw = \mathcal{N}(\mu_{w}^{T}\phi(x_{*}), \phi(x_{*})^{T}\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal{D}}\phi(x_{*}) + \sigma^{2}).$$

The predictive variance has two terms: the posterior uncertainty about the weight vector, projected onto the test feature, plus the observation noise. This decomposition is a prototype for the more general Gaussian-process predictive variance derived in Chapter 4, "Conditioning a Gaussian Process."

## Why structure matters

A probability model becomes useful when its structure encodes domain knowledge. Three structural choices are especially important:

1. **Smoothness.** A kernel or covariance function encodes which inputs should produce similar outputs. This is the subject of Chapter 2, "Positive-Semidefinite Kernels."
2. **Factorization.** A graphical model encodes which variables interact directly and which are conditionally independent. This is the subject of Chapter 7, "Directed Graphical Models," and Chapter 8, "Undirected Models and Factors."
3. **Conditioning.** Given observations, the model specifies how beliefs update. Exact and approximate inference algorithms carry out this update. This is the subject of Chapter 9, "Exact and Approximate Inference."

Without explicit structure, a model may fit training data well while generalizing poorly or while hiding its assumptions from inspection.

## Common errors

- **Reporting intervals without stating assumptions.** A credible interval is conditional on the likelihood, the prior, the hyperparameters, and the data-generating process. Omitting these conditions makes the interval uninterpretable.
- **Treating the posterior as objective truth.** The posterior is a consequence of modeling assumptions. Different priors or likelihoods yield different posteriors from the same data.
- **Confusing aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty.** Observation noise does not shrink with more data; posterior uncertainty about a latent function typically does. Mixing the two leads to incorrect sample-size planning.
- **Ignoring model uncertainty.** A well-calibrated posterior inside a wrong model is still wrong. Model checking, cross-validation, and sensitivity analysis are necessary complements to posterior computation.

## Worked example: Bayesian linear regression on two points

Consider a one-dimensional input $x \in \mathbb{R}$ with feature map $\phi(x) = (1, x)^{T}$, so $f(x) = w_{0} + w_{1}x$. Place a prior $w \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{2})$ and assume observation noise variance $\sigma^{2} = 0.25$. Observe $(x_{1}, y_{1}) = (0, 1)$ and $(x_{2}, y_{2}) = (1, 2)$.

The design matrix and response vector are

$$\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad y = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Compute $\Phi^{T}\Phi$:

$$\Phi^{T}\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Then

$$\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal{D}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.25}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Inverting:

$$\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal{D}} = \frac{1}{9 \cdot 5 - 4 \cdot 4}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{29}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Next, $\Phi^{T}y = (3, 2)^{T}$, so

$$\mu_{w} = \frac{1}{0.25} \cdot \frac{1}{29}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{4}{29}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 28/29 \\ 24/29 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.9655 \\ 0.8276 \end{bmatrix}.$$

For a test input $x_{*} = 0.5$, we have $\phi(x_{*}) = (1, 0.5)^{T}$. The predictive mean is

$$\mu_{w}^{T}\phi(x_{*}) = \frac{28}{29} + \frac{24}{29} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{28 + 12}{29} = \frac{40}{29} \approx 1.3793.$$

The predictive variance is

$$\phi(x_{*})^{T}\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal{D}}\phi(x_{*}) + \sigma^{2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{29}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} + 0.25.$$

Compute the quadratic form:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 - 2 \\ -4 + 4.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = 3 + 0.25 = 3.25.$$

Dividing by $29$ gives $3.25/29 \approx 0.11207$. Adding $\sigma^{2} = 0.25$ yields predictive variance $\approx 0.36207$, so the predictive standard deviation is $\approx 0.6017$.

Thus $y_{*} \mid x_{*} = 0.5, \mathcal{D} \sim \mathcal{N}(1.3793, 0.3621)$. The mean lies between the two training outputs, and the variance is dominated by observation noise because two closely spaced observations leave little epistemic uncertainty about the linear trend.

## Practice exercises

**Exercise 1.1 (Posterior mean as a compromise).** In the Bayesian linear regression model above, assume $\Sigma_w$ is positive definite, $\sigma^2>0$, and $\Phi$ has full column rank. Show that the posterior mean $\mu_w$ can be written as a precision-weighted average of the prior mean $0$ and the maximum-likelihood estimate $\hat w_{\mathrm{ML}}=(\Phi^T\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^Ty$.

**Solution.** Let $A = \Sigma_{w}^{-1}$ and $B = \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Phi^{T}\Phi$. The posterior precision is $A + B$, and the posterior mean is $(A + B)^{-1}B\hat{w}_{\text{ML}}$. This is the precision-weighted average because the weight on the prior mean is $(A + B)^{-1}A$ and the weight on $\hat{w}_{\text{ML}}$ is $(A + B)^{-1}B$, and these sum to the identity. As the data precision $B$ grows, the posterior mean shifts toward the maximum-likelihood estimate; as the prior precision $A$ grows, it shifts toward the prior mean.

**Exercise 1.2 (Predictive variance at training points).** Show that for Bayesian linear regression with a nondegenerate prior and $\phi(x_i)\ne0$, the predictive variance at $x_i$ is strictly larger than $\sigma^2$. Explain what happens when $\phi(x_i)=0$.

**Solution.** The predictive variance at $x_i$ is $\phi(x_i)^T\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal D}\phi(x_i)+\sigma^2$. Since $\Sigma_{w\mid\mathcal D}$ is positive definite, the quadratic term is strictly positive exactly when $\phi(x_i)\ne0$. If $\phi(x_i)=0$, the latent prediction is identically zero and the predictive variance equals $\sigma^2$ despite the nondegenerate weight posterior.

**Exercise 1.3 (Model uncertainty versus posterior width).** Suppose two statisticians fit the same data with different models. Statistician A uses a richly parameterized model and obtains a broad posterior. Statistician B uses a restrictive model and obtains a narrow posterior. Which statistician is more likely to be "confidently wrong" on out-of-sample data? Why?

**Solution.** Posterior widths alone do not determine which statistician is more likely to be wrong: either model may be misspecified. B's restrictive model and narrow posterior warn that systematic error may be hidden, while A's broad posterior reports more within-model uncertainty. Out-of-sample checks and sensitivity analysis are needed to compare their actual risks.

**Exercise 1.4 (Calibration check).** A model reports $95\%$ predictive intervals. In a held-out test set of $200$ points, only $170$ fall inside their intervals. What does this suggest, and what should be checked?

**Solution.** The empirical coverage is $170/200 = 85\%$, well below the nominal $95\%$. This indicates under-coverage. Possible causes include: (i) the observation noise variance $\sigma^{2}$ is underestimated; (ii) the model is misspecified and the posterior is over-concentrated; (iii) the prior is too strong relative to the data; (iv) there is covariate shift between training and test. A calibration audit should examine residual plots, compare marginal likelihoods across candidate models, and test sensitivity to prior hyperparameters.

## Summary and forward pointers

This chapter established that a probability model is a structured statement of assumptions, not merely a fitted curve with error bars. The three uncertainties---observation noise, latent-function uncertainty, and model uncertainty---must be kept distinct. Bayesian conditioning gives coherent updating rules, but only within the chosen model.
