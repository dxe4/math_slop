
# Rings and Ideals

## From one operation to two

A group captures the symmetries of a single operation. Real arithmetic, however, involves both addition and multiplication, and the two interact through the distributive law. A **ring** abstracts this pair of operations. Understanding rings is essential before we can discuss polynomial arithmetic, finite fields, and modules---the structures that dominate the rest of this book.

## Definition and first examples

A **ring** $(R, +, \cdot)$ is a set $R$ with two binary operations satisfying:

1. $(R, +)$ is an abelian group with identity $0$.
2. Multiplication is associative: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c \in R$.
3. The distributive laws hold:
   $$a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c \quad \text{and} \quad (b + c) \cdot a = b \cdot a + c \cdot a.$$

A ring is **commutative** if $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b \in R$. It has a **unity** (or identity element) $1 \neq 0$ with $1 \cdot a = a = a \cdot 1$.

**Examples.**
- The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ form a commutative ring with unity.
- For $n\geq2$, the ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ of integers modulo $n$ is a commutative ring with unity $1\ne0$.
- The set $M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})$ of $2 \times 2$ integer matrices is a non-commutative ring with unity $I_{2}$.
- The trivial ring $\{0\}$ is a ring; some authors exclude it by requiring $0 \neq 1$.

## Zero divisors and integral domains

In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ we have $2 \cdot 3 = 0$ even though $2 \neq 0$ and $3 \neq 0$. In this book, a **zero divisor** is a nonzero element $a$ for which some nonzero $b$ satisfies $ab=0$ or $ba=0$.

A commutative ring with unity and no zero divisors is an **integral domain**. The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are an integral domain; $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is not. A key fact: if $R$ is an integral domain, then $a \cdot b = a \cdot c$ and $a \neq 0$ imply $b = c$. This cancellation law fails in the presence of zero divisors.

**Worked example.** In $\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$, find all zero divisors.

**Solution.** A nonzero residue $a$ is a zero divisor if and only if $1<\gcd(a,15)<15$. Thus the zero divisors are $\{3,5,6,9,10,12\}$. The zero residue is a nonunit but is excluded by our zero-divisor convention. For instance, $3\cdot5\equiv0\pmod{15}$.

## Ideals and quotient rings

An **ideal** $I$ of a ring $R$ is an additive subgroup such that $r \cdot i \in I$ and $i \cdot r \in I$ for all $r \in R$ and $i \in I$. In a commutative ring, the second condition is redundant.

Ideals are the substructures that allow us to form quotients. Define $a \sim b$ when $a - b \in I$. The equivalence classes form the **quotient ring** $R/I$ with operations

$$(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I, \qquad (a + I) \cdot (b + I) = a \cdot b + I.$$

The ideal condition guarantees that multiplication is well defined: if $a' = a + i$ and $b' = b + j$, then

$$a' \cdot b' = a \cdot b + a \cdot j + i \cdot b + i \cdot j \in a \cdot b + I.$$

**Example.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal $(n) = \{kn : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ yields the quotient $\mathbb{Z}/(n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

## Principal ideal domains

In a commutative ring with unity, the ideal generated by one element is $(a)=\{ra:r\in R\}$ and is called **principal**. In a general ring with unity, the two-sided ideal generated by $a$ consists of finite sums of terms $ras$, so the shorter formula need not apply. Without a unity even this description needs extra additive terms; we will not use a formula for that case. A **principal ideal domain** (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is principal.

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are a PID: every ideal is $d\mathbb{Z}$ where $d$ is the greatest common divisor of the ideal's elements. The polynomial ring $F[x]$ over a field $F$ is also a PID; this fact underlies the Euclidean algorithm for polynomials, which we develop in later chapters.

For a commutative ring with unity, a proper ideal $P$ is **prime** when $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$, equivalently when $R/P$ is an integral domain. A proper ideal $M$ is **maximal** when no proper ideal lies strictly between $M$ and $R$, equivalently when $R/M$ is a field.

**Important non-example.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ of integer polynomials is not a PID. The ideal $(2, x)$ cannot be generated by a single polynomial: any generator would have to divide both $2$ and $x$, hence be $\pm 1$, but $(\pm 1) = \mathbb{Z}[x] \neq (2, x)$.

## Homomorphisms and the isomorphism theorems

A **ring homomorphism** $\varphi : R \to S$ satisfies

$$\varphi(a + b) = \varphi(a) + \varphi(b) \quad \text{and} \quad \varphi(a \cdot b) = \varphi(a) \cdot \varphi(b).$$

The **kernel** $\ker(\varphi) = \{r \in R : \varphi(r) = 0\}$ is an ideal of $R$. The **image** $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)$ is a subring of $S$.

**Theorem 5.4 (First isomorphism for rings).** If $\varphi : R \to S$ is a surjective ring homomorphism, then $R / \ker(\varphi) \cong S$.

**Worked example.** Define $\varphi : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ by $\varphi(n) = n \bmod 5$. This is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $(5)$. By the first isomorphism theorem, $\mathbb{Z}/(5) \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$.

## Common errors

- **Treating subrings as ideals.** Every ideal is a subring, but not conversely. The even integers $2\mathbb{Z}$ are an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$, but $\mathbb{Z}$ itself is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ that is not an ideal.
- **Assuming $a \cdot b = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$.** This property is guaranteed in integral domains, but does not require a unity: it also holds in the nonunital ring $2\mathbb{Z}$. In $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, $3 \cdot 4 = 0$ with neither factor zero. 
- **Confusing ring homomorphisms with group homomorphisms.** A map that preserves addition may fail to preserve multiplication. The map $n \mapsto 2n$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$ is a group homomorphism but not a ring homomorphism because $2 \cdot (m \cdot n) \neq (2m) \cdot (2n)$ in general.

## Quotient computation needs an ideal certificate

The algebraic input to a quotient is a ring R and a two-sided ideal I, not just a subset with a convenient name. The additive cosets define the carrier, and multiplication must be independent of representatives. The expansion above proves that independence from ideal absorption. Without it, multiplying two coset labels can depend on the chosen representatives and fail to define any operation.

**Theorem 5.1 (First isomorphism theorem with the actual map).** For a ring homomorphism φ:R→S, the map
$$R/\ker\varphi\longrightarrow\operatorname{im}\varphi,\qquad a+\ker\varphi\longmapsto\varphi(a)$$
is a ring isomorphism.

*Proof.* If a−b lies in the kernel, additivity gives φ(a)=φ(b), so the map is well-defined. It preserves sums and products by the homomorphism equations. Every image element has a preimage coset, giving surjectivity. A coset maps to zero exactly when its representative belongs to the kernel, so only the zero coset is killed; the map is injective. ∎

This proves more than matching two finite cardinalities. It supplies the isomorphism and identifies which residue represents which element. In an implementation, a quotient conversion must retain that map rather than treating two equally sized tables as automatically interchangeable.

**Proposition 5.2 (Prime and maximal quotient criteria).** For a commutative ring with identity and a proper ideal I, the quotient R/I is a domain exactly when I is prime, and is a field exactly when I is maximal.

*Proof.* A product of two nonzero quotient classes is zero precisely when ab∈I while neither a nor b is in I. Excluding such pairs is exactly primality, and properness ensures the quotient identity is nonzero.

If I is maximal and a∉I, the ideal I+(a) must be R. Thus 1=i+ra for some i∈I, making r+I an inverse of a+I. Conversely if the quotient is a field and J strictly contains I, choose a∈J\I. Its nonzero class has an inverse r+I, so ra−1∈I⊆J and hence 1∈J. Therefore J=R, proving maximality. ∎

The inverse witness 1=i+ra is useful computational evidence. A program reporting that a quotient class is a unit can return r and its defining product relation; it need not appeal to an unobserved search through every other class.

## Finite ideals by coefficient enumeration

For a finite commutative unital ring R and supplied elements s₁,…,s_t, the generated ideal is exactly
$$I=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^t r_js_j:r_j\in R\right\}.$$
This is a finite set, although its naive enumeration may be large.

**Proposition 5.3 (Generated-ideal algorithm).** Enumerating all |R|^t coefficient tuples and collecting those sums returns the ideal generated by the s_j.

*Proof.* The displayed set contains every generator by setting its coefficient to one and the others zero. It contains zero and is closed under addition and additive inverse by changing coefficients. Multiplying a sum by any r∈R multiplies each coefficient, so the set is an ideal. Any ideal containing all s_j must contain every such scalar multiple and sum, so the displayed set is the smallest one. ∎

An inert reference program is:

```text
ideal_generated_by(R, generators):
    require R is a specified finite commutative unital ring
    I = empty set
    for coefficients in all tuples from R of length len(generators):
        value = R.zero
        for r, s in paired coefficients and generators:
            value = R.add(value, R.multiply(r, s))
        insert value into I
    return I
```

For an empty generator list there is one empty tuple and its sum is zero, giving the zero ideal. With t generators the enumeration takes O(t|R|^t) ring operations before set-storage costs. This is a transparent correctness-first algorithm, not a claim of efficiency in the bit length of |R|. Additive-group generators or specialised polynomial algorithms can compress the search, but their invariants must be stated separately.

**Worked example 5.4 (A quotient of a finite nonfield).** In R=Z/12Z, the ideal generated by eight is {0,4,8}: multiplying eight by residues cycles through those three values. The additive cosets are represented by 0,1,2,3, and reduction modulo four is a surjective ring map R→Z/4Z with exactly that kernel. Therefore R/(8)≅Z/4Z. Its class represented by two is a nonzero zero divisor, so the quotient is not a field despite having four elements. This contrasts with the irreducible-polynomial construction of F₄.

## Units and nonunits in a finite commutative ring

**Theorem 5.5.** In a finite commutative unital ring, every nonzero nonunit is a zero divisor, while a unit cannot be a zero divisor.

*Proof.* Multiplication by a is a map from the finite set R to itself. If a is a nonunit, one is not in its image, so the map is not surjective and hence not injective. There exist b≠c with ab=ac, giving a(b−c)=0 with b−c≠0. If a≠0, this is a zero-divisor witness. Conversely, if a has inverse and ab=0, multiplying by that inverse gives b=0, so no nonzero witness exists. ∎

The finiteness hypothesis is essential. In Z, the element two is a nonunit but not a zero divisor. The theorem explains why a finite-ring inverse search that exhausts all candidates can be converted into a collision witness for a nonzero nonunit. Zero itself remains excluded from the zero-divisor convention used in this book.

## Chinese remainders give a constructive decomposition

**Theorem 5.6 (Two-modulus computation).** If positive integers m,n are coprime, reduction gives an isomorphism Z/(mn)→Z/m×Z/n. If rm+sn=1, an inverse sends (a,b) to asn+brm modulo mn.

*Proof.* The displayed expression is a modulo m and b modulo n, proving surjectivity of reduction and correctness of the inverse. If an integer reduces to zero in both components, divisibility by coprime m,n implies divisibility by mn, so the reduction map is injective. It preserves the ring operations, completing the proof. ∎

**Worked example 5.7 (Twelve as three times four).** The identity −3+4=1 gives component selectors four and nine modulo twelve. Thus (a mod3,b mod4) maps to 4a+9b mod12. The pair (2,3) maps to 35≡11, whose reductions are indeed two and three. Units correspond to pairs of units: F₃ has units one and two, while Z/4Z has units one and three, giving the four original units {1,5,7,11}. This is a complete ring decomposition with an explicit conversion, not merely a count of four units.

## Polynomial normal forms and the multivariable warning

For a monic one-variable modulus f, division gives a unique remainder of degree below deg f whether or not f is irreducible. The quotient is a ring in all cases, and becomes a field only with the extra irreducibility hypothesis. Addition and multiplication therefore use the same reduction routines in both settings while their available inverse operations differ.

With several generators in a multivariable ideal, an arbitrary division remainder is not automatically canonical. For example over Q let I=(x+y,x−y). The polynomial x lies in I because x=((x+y)+(x−y))/2. But dividing x first by x+y leaves −y, which neither leading-x generator can reduce further. A nonzero remainder from that generator list therefore does not certify nonmembership. The Groebner chapter develops the extra leading-ideal property that makes a canonical nonmembership conclusion valid.

A combination certificate ∑a_i g_i=f always proves membership in the generated ideal, because ideals absorb coefficients and sums. Its soundness does not require a Groebner basis. Completeness of a particular search for such certificates is a separate question. This distinction between sound output and complete decision procedure recurs throughout computational algebra.

## A finite quotient with both product and nilpotent structure

**Worked example 5.8.** Consider R=F₂[x]/(x³+x). The modulus factors as x(x+1)². The two factors x and (x+1)² are coprime, so reduction induces
$$R\cong F_2\times F_2[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),$$
where in the second factor x maps to 1+ε. To prove this directly, the kernel of simultaneous reduction consists of polynomials divisible by both coprime factors, hence by their product. Surjectivity follows from the explicit component selectors below. Both sides have eight elements, consistently with the unique degree-below-three representatives on the left.

In R the relation x³=x gives x⁴=x². Set e₀=1+x² and e₁=x². Then e₀+e₁=1, each e_i squares to itself, and e₀e₁=x²+x⁴=0. Reducing modulo x sends e₀ to one and e₁ to zero. Reducing modulo (x+1)² sends e₀ to zero and e₁ to one, since x=1+ε has square one. Thus e₀ and e₁ select the two components. A pair consisting of a scalar a and a residue b+cε is represented by ae₀+(b+c(x+1))e₁. This proves surjectivity with a formula, rather than solely by counting.

There is also a nonzero nilpotent n=x²+x. It is nonzero because its representative has degree below three, and n²=x⁴+x²=0. Its coordinates are (0,ε). Consequently R is neither a field nor a product of fields: any product of fields has no nonzero nilpotents, as a square is zero componentwise only when every component is zero.

An element of a product ring is invertible exactly when both components are invertible, since the equations for an inverse separate componentwise. In F₂ the only unit is one. In the dual-number factor, b+cε is invertible exactly when b=1: if b=0 it squares to zero, while (1+cε)²=1. There are therefore precisely two units in R, namely 1 and 1+n. Each is its own inverse. This agrees with the general finite-ring theorem but also gives the exact unit set and inverse witnesses.

This example separates three pieces of information that a carrier size does not encode: the product decomposition, the nilpotent direction, and the distinguished residue x. An eight-element field would have seven units and no nonzero nilpotent. Even the product F₂×F₄ would have three units and no nonzero nilpotent. A coefficient array of length three can describe the eight residues here only when its reduction relation is retained; replacing that relation by an irreducible cubic changes every one of these structural conclusions.

The nilpotent also identifies a genuine quotient operation: factoring out its ideal leaves F₂×F₂. In the displayed coordinates, that ideal is precisely zero in the first component and the span of ε in the second. Removing the nilpotent direction therefore reduces eight elements to four while preserving the two component selectors. It does not merge the two components into a field.

A unital ring with a prime number of elements is necessarily that prime field. Its additive subgroup generated by one has order equal to the ring's characteristic. That order divides the additive group order by Lagrange, and it cannot be one in a nonzero ring. Hence it is the given prime, so integer multiples of one exhaust the carrier. Reduction from the integers therefore gives an isomorphism from the prime residue field onto the ring, preserving its actual addition and multiplication. Composite cardinality has no analogous conclusion: a prime-power cardinality can support either a field or a ring with nilpotents, depending on the operations already specified.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise 1

Prove that in any ring $R$, the element $0$ satisfies $0 \cdot a = 0 = a \cdot 0$ for all $a \in R$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Expand (0+0)a by distributivity and cancel in the additive group.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

We have $0 \cdot a = (0 + 0) \cdot a = 0 \cdot a + 0 \cdot a$ by distributivity. Subtracting $0 \cdot a$ from both sides (valid because $(R, +)$ is a group) gives $0 = 0 \cdot a$. The proof for $a \cdot 0$ is symmetric.

</details>

### Exercise 2

List all units in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. Explain why every nonzero nonunit is a zero divisor, with zero listed separately.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use gcd(a,12)=1 for units and a(12/gcd(a,12))=0 for nonzero nonunits.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

A residue $a$ is a unit exactly when $\gcd(a,12)=1$, so the units are $\{1,5,7,11\}$. The nonzero nonunits $\{2,3,4,6,8,9,10\}$ are zero divisors: if $d=\gcd(a,12)>1$, then $a(12/d)\equiv0\pmod{12}$ and $12/d$ is nonzero modulo $12$. Conversely a zero divisor cannot be a unit. The remaining nonunit is $0$, excluded from the zero-divisor definition used here.

</details>

### Exercise 3

Show that the ideal $(x)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is prime but not maximal.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Evaluate at x=0 and identify the quotient with Z.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The quotient $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x)$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ via the evaluation map $f(x) \mapsto f(0)$. Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain but not a field, the ideal $(x)$ is prime (quotient is a domain) but not maximal (quotient is not a field).

</details>

### Exercise 4

In $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}[x]$, perform polynomial division of $f(x) = x^{3} + 2x^{2} + 3x + 4$ by $g(x) = x + 1$. Verify that $f = q \cdot g + r$ with $\deg(r) < \deg(g)$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Cancel the leading term at each step and keep the remainder in F₇[x].

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Over the field $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$, division proceeds as follows. The leading term of $f$ is $x^{3}$ and of $g$ is $x$, so the first term of $q$ is $x^{2}$. Compute $x^{2} \cdot g = x^{3} + x^{2}$. Subtract from $f$: $(x^{3} + 2x^{2}) - (x^{3} + x^{2}) = x^{2}$. Bring down $3x + 4$ to get $x^{2} + 3x + 4$. Next term of $q$ is $x$; $x \cdot g = x^{2} + x$; subtract to get $2x + 4$. Next term of $q$ is $2$; $2 \cdot g = 2x + 2$; subtract to get $2$. Thus $q(x) = x^{2} + x + 2$ and $r(x) = 2$. Check: $(x^{2} + x + 2)(x + 1) + 2 = x^{3} + 2x^{2} + 3x + 4 = f(x)$.

</details>

### Exercise 5 — A quotient with four elements
<a id="finite-rings-ex-5"></a>

Compute (Z/12Z)/(8) and decide whether it is a field.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The ideal generated by eight is {0,4,8}; use reduction modulo four.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Reduction Z/12Z→Z/4Z is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel {0,4,8}, exactly the ideal (8). The quotient is therefore Z/4Z. Its class two is nonzero but squares to zero, so it is not a field. Four-element cardinality does not imply the field F₄.

### Exercise 7 — The finite hypothesis
<a id="finite-rings-ex-7"></a>

Why does the finite-ring nonunit/zero-divisor theorem not apply to two in Z?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiplication by two is injective but not surjective on the infinite set Z.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The element two has no integer inverse and kills no nonzero integer. Its multiplication map has image the even integers, so is not onto, but remains injective. Equivalence of injectivity and surjectivity for a self-map was used only because the ring was finite. The failure over Z shows that finiteness cannot be dropped.

</details>

### Exercise 8 — A CRT reconstruction
<a id="finite-rings-ex-8"></a>

Find the residue modulo twelve corresponding to (1 mod3,2 mod4).

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the selectors four and nine.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The inverse formula gives 4·1+9·2=22≡10 modulo twelve. Direct reduction gives 10≡1 mod3 and 10≡2 mod4. Since reduction is an isomorphism, that residue is unique modulo twelve.

</details>

### Exercise 9 — A nonprincipal ideal is proper
<a id="finite-rings-ex-9"></a>

Prove (2,x) is proper in Z[x], completing the non-PID example.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Send a polynomial to its constant coefficient modulo two.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The map Z[x]→F₂ evaluating at zero then reducing modulo two is surjective. Its kernel is exactly polynomials with even constant term: each has form 2a+xh(x). Thus its kernel is (2,x), and it is proper because one maps to one. Combined with the common-divisor argument in the text, this proves a proper ideal that cannot be principal.

</details>

### Exercise 10 — A misleading remainder
<a id="finite-rings-ex-10"></a>

Over Q[x,y], show x∈(x+y,x−y) although division by the displayed generators can leave −y.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write a combination using one-half.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The identity x=(x+y)/2+(x−y)/2 proves membership. Dividing x by x+y subtracts that generator and leaves −y. Both generators have leading x under a lexicographic order with x first, so neither reduces the remaining y term. The nonzero remainder is therefore not a valid nonmembership certificate without a suitable Groebner basis.

</details>

## Summary

Ideals make quotient multiplication independent of representatives, and explicit homomorphisms identify the resulting quotient. Finite ideal enumeration, inverse witnesses and Chinese remainders supply exact algorithms and conversions. Polynomial remainders remain ring computations until field hypotheses are proved, while multivariable nonmembership requires the stronger Groebner machinery. The next chapter turns integer and polynomial relation matrices into module normal forms.
