
# Coset Enumeration and Presentations

A presentation supplies generators and relations, not a ready-made finite multiplication table. Coset enumeration tries to build a finite action from that symbolic input. A completed action is useful, but its size equals the subgroup index only when the construction also certifies that no actual cosets are missing.

Prepare [permutation groups and stabilisers](computational-group-theory.md), cosets, generated subgroups and quotient groups. Let G=⟨X|R⟩ be a finite presentation, meaning the free group on the finite alphabet X modulo the normal closure of the relators R. Let H be generated by a finite list Y of words in X and their formal inverses. Neither G nor its index over H is assumed finite at the outset.

## 1. Right cosets and action conventions

This chapter uses right cosets Ht and a right action by multiplication: (Ht)^x=Htx. A word is read along table edges from left to right. This differs from the left permutation composition convention in the preceding chapter; if converting to left permutation functions, use the inverse word so the group law is represented consistently.

**Definition 8.1 (Coset table).** A table has rows labelled by prospective cosets and columns indexed by X∪X⁻¹. The entry C[i,x]=j records Ht_i x=Ht_j. Its inverse entry must satisfy C[j,x⁻¹]=i. A complete finite table has every entry defined, each generator column acts as a permutation, every relator traces a loop from every row, and every subgroup word in Y traces a loop from the distinguished base row.

During enumeration, row labels can be provisional. Two different labels can later be proved to denote the same coset and merged. The number of currently allocated rows is therefore not an established index. An undefined edge is missing information, not a proof that a word leaves the subgroup.

A valid complete table defines a right G-action because inverse edges give an action of the free group and the relators act trivially everywhere. Fixation of the base row by Y makes every member of H fix it. If the action is transitive, mapping an actual coset Hw to the row reached from the base by w is a well-defined surjection H\G→rows. Thus such a table proves a lower bound on the actual index. Equality needs an upper-bound certificate as well.

## 2. Two certificates give the exact index

**Definition 8.2 (Transition witnesses).** Choose a representative word t_i for every row, with t₀ the empty word. For each transition C[i,x]=j, provide a proof that t_i x t_j⁻¹ belongs to H in the presented group.

A finite symbolic proof can be specified as follows. Give a word h in the supplied subgroup generators Y± and express (t_i x t_j⁻¹)h⁻¹ in the free group as a finite product of conjugates of defining relators and their inverses. Free reduction then verifies the word identity. This is a checkable finite certificate of membership; it does not assume an algorithm solving every word problem.

**Theorem 8.3 (Exact finite-index certificate).** A transitive complete table satisfying the relator and subgroup loops, together with valid transition witnesses, proves that its n rows are in bijection with the actual right cosets of H. In particular [G:H]=n.

*Proof of the lower bound.* The action gives the surjection from actual cosets onto the n rows described above. Therefore there are at least n cosets.

*Proof of the upper bound.* Every word in X± follows a finite table path from the base. Inductively its actual coset is represented by the current t_i: initially this is H, and a transition witness proves Ht_i x=Ht_j at the next edge. Thus every actual coset equals one of the n listed representative cosets, giving at most n. The bounds force equality and make the two identifications inverse bijections. ∎

A finite quotient action alone is insufficient. For example the infinite cyclic group has a transitive action on a cycle of any finite length, even when H is trivial and its true index is infinite. The transition witnesses rule out mistaking those quotient actions for exact coset enumerations.

## 3. Deductions and coincidences

A Todd–Coxeter-style construction records partial edges and propagates three kinds of information. First, defining C[i,x]=j also defines its inverse edge. Second, every relator must return each row to itself; a trace with only one missing edge can force that edge. Third, incompatible targets for an already determined transition produce a coincidence, requiring row equivalence and propagation of their other edges.

The subgroup words supply initial loops at the base. New undefined edges can be assigned fresh provisional rows when no deduction fills them. A sound implementation records why a coincidence identifies the actual represented cosets. Merely having the same number of currently known neighbours does not prove two labels equal.

When rows merge, their representative words remain meaningful, but the table redirects to an equivalence-class representative. The membership proofs establishing each merge must be retained or recomposed into the final transition witnesses. A resource limit or an unfinished coincidence queue leaves a partial result; it must return “unknown under this limit”, rather than claiming an infinite index or an exact finite table.

A simple inert word tracer illustrates the partial-result contract:

```text
trace(table, base, word):
    row = base
    for letter in word:
        if table[row,letter] is undefined:
            return unknown
        row = representative(table[row,letter])
    return row
```

After an exact complete enumeration, reaching the base means the word is in H and reaching another row means it is not. Before completion, reaching a row or an undefined entry needs the relevant certificate before it is assigned that interpretation.

## 4. A rigorous finite-index search baseline

**Theorem 8.4 (Finite-index semidecision).** There is an exhaustive algorithm which terminates with an exact certificate whenever H has finite index in a finitely presented G. It never returns an incorrect finite index; on an infinite-index input it need not terminate.

*Proof.* Enumerate finite transitive permutation tables on n labelled rows for increasing finite size bounds, with one permutation per positive generator and inverse columns forced. Their relator and subgroup loops are finite checks. For each surviving table, choose words t_i reaching its rows by a finite breadth-first traversal.

Dovetail these candidate tables with finite word-certificate searches for all their transition witnesses. One can enumerate bounded-length products of conjugates of the finitely many defining relators, and bounded-length words in the subgroup generators, then freely reduce the resulting words. At every global bound there are finitely many candidates; increase all bounds so no candidate or finite proof is starved.

If the actual index is finite, its true coset table is among the candidates. Each actual transition membership has a finite word proof by the definition of the presented group and generated subgroup. Finitely many transitions therefore have a common finite proof-length bound, and the exhaustive search eventually encounters that complete certificate. Theorem 8.3 makes every returned result correct. If the index is infinite, no exact finite certificate can exist, so the procedure returns none, though it can keep searching forever. ∎

This gives a transparent termination theorem with stated hypotheses. Practical coset enumeration uses deductions and coincidences to avoid searching all finite tables. Their efficiency depends on the strategy and presentation; the existence proof above supplies no polynomial time or memory bound. A fair practical strategy and its precise termination theorem must not be replaced by an unqualified claim that every allocation order works.

## 5. Three complete examples

**Worked example 8.5 (Rotations in the triangle group).** Take G=⟨a,b|a³,b²,abab⟩ and H=⟨a⟩. Use representatives t₀=1,t₁=b. Right multiplication by a fixes both rows, since ba=a⁻¹b and powers of a lie in H. Right multiplication by b swaps the rows, since b²=1. This is a transitive two-row action, with every displayed relator looping and a fixing the base.

The nontrivial transition witness from row b under a is ba b⁻¹=a⁻¹∈H. The other a witness is a∈H, and both b transitions have identity witness using b²=1. Thus Theorem 8.3 proves index two. This is the action on two cosets, not the regular six-point action of the full triangle group.

**Worked example 8.6 (A reflection in the square group).** Let G=⟨a,b|a⁴,b²,abab⟩ and H=⟨b⟩. Take representatives 1,a,a²,a³. On row i modulo four, right a sends i to i+1 and right b sends i to −i. These are permutations; b² and a⁴ loop automatically, and abab sends i to i+1,−i−1,−i,i, so also loops. The subgroup generator b fixes row zero.

Every a transition has identity witness, including the wraparound using a⁴=1. For b, the required witness is a^i b a^i=b, using ba^i=a^(−i)b derived from the relator. Thus all transitions have subgroup witnesses and the exact index is four. The b column fixes rows zero and two and exchanges one and three. No assumption that H is normal was needed.

**Worked example 8.7 (Finite actions of an infinite group).** Let G=⟨a| ⟩≅Z. For H=⟨a²⟩ the two-row table where a swaps the rows is exact. Representatives 1,a give one transition witness identity and the wraparound witness a²∈H; inverse transitions similarly use a⁻² or identity. Hence the index is two.

For H={1}, a cycle of length m still gives a transitive action satisfying every relator, since there are none, and the subgroup condition is empty. But its wraparound witness would require a^m∈H. A nonempty power of a is not the identity in the free cyclic group, so that certificate fails. The true index is infinite. The action provides a lower bound m, not an exact index m.

## 6. Schreier generators from an exact table

**Theorem 8.8.** Given an exact finite coset table and representatives t_i, the elements
$$s_{i,x}=t_i x t_{C[i,x]}^{-1}$$
generate H.

*Proof.* Each is in H by its transition witness, so their generated subgroup is contained in H. Conversely write any h∈H as a word x₁⋯x_l. Its table path has row sequence i₀=0,…,i_l=0. Multiplying the successive elements s_(i_(j−1),x_j) cancels adjacent representatives and gives t₀h t₀⁻¹=h. Thus every h is a product of these generators. ∎

This is the right-action version of Schreier rewriting. It supplies finite subgroup generators from a finite-index table, even when H was initially described with a different generating list. It does not alone supply a complete defining presentation for H; deriving all subgroup relations needs the further Reidemeister–Schreier relation construction.

The kernel of the right coset action is the core ∩_(g∈G)g⁻¹Hg, the largest normal subgroup contained in H. Indeed an element fixes Hg exactly when it lies in g⁻¹Hg, so fixation of every coset is exactly that intersection. Consequently a faithful coset action can have a nontrivial base stabiliser. A quotient group G/H exists precisely when H itself is normal; an arbitrary coset action must not be labelled quotient multiplication.

## 7. A complete subgroup rewriting calculation

For the infinite cyclic group with $H=\langle a^2\rangle$, the two exact coset representatives are $t_0=1,t_1=a$. The signed alphabet has $a$ and $a^{-1}$, both swapping the rows. The four Schreier transition generators are
$$s_{0,a}=1,\quad s_{1,a}=a^2,\quad
s_{0,a^{-1}}=a^{-2},\quad s_{1,a^{-1}}=1.$$
Thus the table recovers the subgroup generators $a^2,a^{-2}$, rather than merely reporting its index.

Following the word $a^6$ traverses the two rows three times and returns to the base. Its successive transition factors multiply to $(a^2)^3=a^6$, an explicit membership expression in the supplied subgroup generator. The word $a^5$ finishes at the other row, which certifies nonmembership once the action has been identified with actual cosets. Every integer power is decided by the same finite table, although the group itself is infinite.

In the square-group example, the four-point action has a different useful property. Its reflection subgroup $H=\langle b\rangle$ intersects its conjugate $a^{-1}Ha=\langle a^2b\rangle$ trivially, since the two subgroups of order two are distinct. Hence the normal core, being contained in that intersection, is trivial. The four-point action is faithful and represents the whole eight-element square group as permutations. It is still not a quotient group of order four. In contrast the triangle rotation subgroup is normal, so its two-point action has kernel exactly that rotation subgroup and represents its two-element quotient. The actual kernel calculation distinguishes these outcomes; the number of cosets alone does not.

A finite index is not a proof that the presented group is finite. The order formula multiplies the index by the subgroup order, so the latter must also be known finite before it yields a finite group order. The two-coset action of the infinite cyclic group on the cosets of its even powers is an explicit counterexample to dropping that hypothesis. Its finite permutation image records a quotient action, while the infinite kernel remains part of the original group.

Several coset actions can retain information lost by one. Acting on the disjoint union of two coset sets has kernel equal to the intersection of the two action kernels, because a group element fixes every point exactly when it does so in each part. For the Klein four-group, choose two distinct subgroups of order two. They are normal, so each two-point coset action has that subgroup as its kernel and is individually unfaithful. Their intersection is trivial, making the combined four-point action faithful. The total number of points is the sum of the indices, not their product. This gives a certified way to combine small action representations while keeping each coset interpretation and its exact kernel.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 — A quotient action is not enough
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-1"></a>

Why does a finite transitive action with H fixing a point prove only a lower bound on [G:H]?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Map actual cosets to orbit points.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The map Hw↦base·w is well-defined because H fixes the base, and transitivity makes it onto. Thus there are at least as many actual cosets as orbit points. Different cosets can still map to one point when the true stabiliser is larger than H. Transition witnesses giving all actual cosets among the representatives are needed for the opposite bound and equality.

</details>

### Exercise 2 — A transition certificate
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-2"></a>

For right cosets, prove Ht_i x=Ht_j exactly when t_i x t_j⁻¹∈H.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use equality of cosets as multiplication by a subgroup element on the left.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Equality means t_i x=h t_j for some h∈H, which rearranges to t_i x t_j⁻¹=h∈H. Conversely that membership gives the same equation and hence equality of the right cosets. The expression order is specific to right cosets and should not be replaced by the left-coset version without changing conventions.

</details>

### Exercise 3 — Square-group table
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-3"></a>

For the reflection subgroup in the square group, list the a and b row permutations on rows 0,1,2,3.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use i↦i+1 and i↦−i modulo four.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The a column is [1,2,3,0], a four-cycle. The b column is [0,3,2,1], fixing zero and two and swapping one and three. They satisfy a⁴=b²=1 and abab=1. Together with the transition membership witnesses from the worked example they give the exact four-coset table, rather than only a permissible finite action.

</details>

### Exercise 4 — A two-coset infinite-group example
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-4"></a>

For G=⟨a| ⟩ and H=⟨a²⟩, certify the wraparound transition from representative a under a.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The target representative is the empty word.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The transition has source t₁=a and target t₀=1, so its witness is a·a·1⁻¹=a², one of the supplied generators of H. Thus Ha·a=H. The reverse transition uses the appropriate inverse witness. This finite index does not require the whole group G to be finite.

</details>

### Exercise 5 — An incomplete trace
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-5"></a>

What should a membership routine report when a word path reaches an undefined table entry?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

No actual target coset has yet been determined.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

It reports unknown under the current partial information. An undefined edge is neither a subgroup membership proof nor a nonmembership proof. A completed exact table allows the final-row decision; a sound action certificate can sometimes prove nonmembership by reaching a different point, but missing entries by themselves establish no conclusion.

</details>

### Exercise 6 — Why a coincidence needs proof
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-6"></a>

Why cannot two provisional rows be merged only because their currently defined neighbours look alike?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Partial tables can agree locally while representing distinct cosets.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Undefined or partial edges omit information that may later distinguish the rows. Equality of actual cosets requires their representative quotient to belong to H, justified by deductions from subgroup words and defining relators. A purely visual or local-pattern merge could identify different actual cosets and falsify the upper-bound certificate. Deductions must preserve actual coset equality, not merely table similarity.

</details>

### Exercise 7 — Recover subgroup generators
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-7"></a>

Explain why the product of transition generators along a closed base-row word equals that word in H.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Adjacent representative inverses and representatives cancel.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

For a path with rows i₀=0,…,i_l=0, multiply t_(i₀)x₁t_(i₁)⁻¹, t_(i₁)x₂t_(i₂)⁻¹ and so on. The adjacent representative factors cancel, leaving t₀(x₁⋯x_l)t₀⁻¹, which is the original word because t₀ is empty. Each factor is in H, so this proves constructive generation of every subgroup word represented by a closed path.

</details>

### Exercise 8 — Coset action kernel
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-8"></a>

Prove the kernel of the action on right cosets is ∩g⁻¹Hg.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Determine the stabiliser of one coset Hg, then intersect.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

An element x fixes Hg exactly when Hgx=Hg, equivalent to gxg⁻¹∈H, hence x∈g⁻¹Hg. Fixation of every coset is therefore membership in the intersection of these conjugates. That intersection is normal and contained in H, and any normal subgroup contained in H lies in every conjugate, making it the largest such subgroup.

</details>

### Exercise 9 — A finite-index search condition
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-9"></a>

Why does the exhaustive certificate search terminate when the actual index is finite?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

There are finitely many actual transitions and each has a finite word witness.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The actual finite coset action appears among the enumerated candidate tables. Each of its finitely many transition memberships has a finite proof in the generators, subgroup words and normal closure of the defining relators. A common finite bound covers all those witnesses. Dovetailing all table sizes and proof lengths eventually reaches that bound and finds the exact certificate. The argument gives no such stopping point for infinite index.

</details>

### Exercise 10 — Quotient group or action?
<a id="finite-cosets-ex-10"></a>

Does a four-row coset table for a reflection subgroup of the square group define a quotient group of order four?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Check normality of the reflection subgroup, rather than only the row count.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

No. A reflection subgroup is not normal: conjugating its generating reflection by a quarter-turn gives a different reflection. Its right cosets still form a valid four-point G-set, but multiplication of coset representatives is not well-defined as quotient multiplication. The table records a permutation action, whose base stabiliser is H and whose kernel is its core, rather than a quotient group G/H.

</details>

## Summary

A presentation requires symbolic relation proofs as well as finite actions. Relator and subgroup loops give a lower index bound, while transition membership witnesses give the upper bound; together they certify an exact table. Partial enumeration can remain unknown, and finite-index termination needs a stated strategy. Schreier rewriting extracts subgroup generators, while the action kernel is the normal core. These contracts will help the final algebra kernel distinguish certified answers from incomplete symbolic searches.
