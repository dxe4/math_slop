
# Computational Algebraic Geometry

## Polynomial systems and their geometry

Algebraic geometry studies the zero sets of systems of polynomial equations. When the polynomials have coefficients in a field---especially $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, or a finite field---we can ask computational questions: Does the system have solutions? How many? Can we parametrise them? This chapter introduces the algebraic tools, with emphasis on Gröbner bases and elimination theory, that make these questions algorithmic.

## Affine varieties and ideals

Let $k$ be a field and $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ the polynomial ring. An **affine algebraic variety** is the set

$$V(I) = \{(a_{1}, \dots, a_{n}) \in k^{n} : f(a_{1}, \dots, a_{n}) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}$$

for some ideal $I \subseteq k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$. Conversely, the **vanishing ideal** of a set $X \subseteq k^{n}$ is

$$I(X) = \{f \in k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}] : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in X\}.$$

**Hilbert's Nullstellensatz.** If $k$ is algebraically closed, then $I(V(I)) = \sqrt{I}$, the radical of $I$.

Over finite fields, the Nullstellensatz fails in this form because $k^{n}$ is finite and many polynomials vanish identically. One must work in the algebraic closure or use the ideal-variety correspondence over the finite field directly.

## Monomial orders and Gröbner bases

A **monomial order** $<$ on $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ is a total order on monomials that is a well-ordering and respects multiplication: if $x^{\alpha} < x^{\beta}$, then $x^{\gamma} x^{\alpha} < x^{\gamma} x^{\beta}$.

Common monomial orders:
- **Lexicographic (lex):** $x^{\alpha} < x^{\beta}$ if the leftmost non-zero entry of $\beta - \alpha$ is positive.
- **Graded reverse lexicographic (grevlex):** first compare total degree, then break ties by preferring monomials with smaller rightmost non-zero exponent.

For nonzero $f$, write $\operatorname{LM}(f)$ for its largest monomial, $\operatorname{LC}(f)$ for that monomial's coefficient, and $\operatorname{LT}(f)=\operatorname{LC}(f)\operatorname{LM}(f)$ for the leading term.

A **Gröbner basis** for an ideal $I$ with respect to $<$ is a finite set $G \subseteq I$ such that the ideal generated by leading terms of $G$ equals the ideal generated by leading terms of $I$:

$$(\operatorname{LT}(g) : g \in G) = (\operatorname{LT}(f) : f \in I).$$

**Buchberger's algorithm.** Given a generating set for $I$, compute the $S$-polynomial of each pair:

$$S(f,g)=\frac{L}{\operatorname{LC}(f)\operatorname{LM}(f)}f-\frac{L}{\operatorname{LC}(g)\operatorname{LM}(g)}g,$$

where $L=\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{LM}(f),\operatorname{LM}(g))$. For monic polynomials this is the familiar cancellation formula using the two leading monomials.

Reduce $S(f, g)$ modulo $G$. If the remainder is non-zero, append it to $G$ and repeat. The algorithm terminates because monomial ideals satisfy the ascending chain condition.

## Elimination and the extension theorem

In lexicographic order with $x_{1} > x_{2} > \dots > x_{n}$, a Gröbner basis $G$ for $I$ satisfies the **elimination property**:

$$G \cap k[x_{i}, \dots, x_{n}] \text{ is a Gröbner basis for } I \cap k[x_{i}, \dots, x_{n}].$$

Elimination finds necessary polynomial constraints on the retained coordinates. Back-substitution is a separate **extension step**: a candidate satisfying those constraints must still admit values for the eliminated variables.

Over $\mathbb C$, let $J=I\cap\mathbb C[x_2,\ldots,x_n]$ and let $\pi$ discard the first coordinate. Then

$$\pi(V(I))\subseteq V(J).$$

The **closure theorem** identifies $V(J)$ with the Zariski closure of $\pi(V(I))$, the smallest algebraic set containing the projected solutions. The inclusion need not be equality. Thus computing an elimination ideal does not automatically determine which candidates extend.

**Theorem 18.1 (Extension, stated).** Let $I=(f_1,\ldots,f_s)\subseteq\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, omitting zero generators. Write

$$f_j=c_j(x_2,\ldots,x_n)x_1^{N_j}+\text{terms of lower degree in }x_1,$$

where $N_j\ge0$ and $c_j$ is a nonzero polynomial. If $p\in V(J)$ and at least one $c_j(p)\ne0$, then some $a_1\in\mathbb C$ makes $(a_1,p)\in V(I)$. This condition is sufficient. Where all $c_j$ vanish, the theorem gives no conclusion; extension may succeed or fail. Generators with $N_j=0$ already belong to $J$ and vanish at every candidate. In particular, a generator of positive degree in $x_1$ with nonzero constant leading coefficient guarantees extension of every candidate.

**A missing witness.** Consider $f=au^2+u+b$ and $g=au+1$, eliminating $u$. Since $f-ug=b$, a common root requires $b=0$. When $a\ne0$ and $b=0$, the unique common root is $u=-1/a$. When $a=0$, however, $g=1$, so no witness exists. The exact projection is therefore $\{(a,0):a\ne0\}$. Its Zariski closure is the entire line $b=0$: a polynomial vanishing at every $(a,0)$ with $a\ne0$ also vanishes at the origin, by the univariate root bound. The elimination variety consequently contains a candidate that cannot extend.

The imported results are stated in [Setiabrata's computational algebra notes, printed pages 48, 50–51 and 53](https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~ls823/4370fa19.pdf).

**Worked example.** Find the solutions in $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ of

$$\begin{cases}
x^{2} + y^{2} = 1, \\\ 
x^{2} - y = 0.
\end{cases}$$

**Solution.** Use lex order $x>y$. Set $f_2=x^2-y$ and subtract it from $f_1=x^2+y^2-1$ to obtain $f_3=y^2+y-1$. The pair $G=\{f_2,f_3\}$ generates the original ideal. Its only $S$-polynomial is

$S(f_2,f_3)=y^2(x^2-y)-x^2(y^2+y-1)=-y^3-x^2y+x^2$.

Reducing $x^2$ to $y$ and $y^2$ to $1-y$ gives zero, so Buchberger's criterion makes $G$ a Gröbner basis. First solve

$y^2+y-1=0,\qquad y=(-1\pm\sqrt5)/2$,

then solve $x^2=y$. Each nonzero value of $y$ has two square roots in $\mathbb C$, giving four complex solutions; two are real.

Here $x^2-y$ is monic in $x$, so its constant leading coefficient guarantees complex extension of every eliminated $y$-candidate; a negative real $y$, however, has no real square root.

## Applications to finite fields

Over $\mathbb{F}_{q}$, Gröbner bases can count solutions, test consistency, and compute elimination ideals. The quotient $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I$ is finite-dimensional over $k$ exactly when the initial ideal contains a positive power of every variable. Equivalently, for each $x_i$ some leading monomial in a Gröbner basis divides a power of $x_i$. A fixed-order basis need not literally contain a univariate polynomial in every variable. For zero-dimensional ideals, the FGLM algorithm can convert a basis between monomial orders.

**Example.** Count the solutions in $\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}$ of $x^{2} + y^{2} = 1$ and $x + y = 1$.

**Solution.** Over $\mathbb{F}_{2}$, $x^{2} = x$ and $y^{2} = y$, so the first equation becomes $x + y = 1$, identical to the second. The solutions are $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$. There are two solutions.

## Common errors

- **Assuming a Gröbner basis with respect to one order is a basis with respect to another.** Lex bases are often much larger than grevlex bases. For a zero-dimensional ideal, FGLM converts between orders; outside that hypothesis a different method is required.
- **Forgetting that $V(I)$ may be empty even when $I \neq (1)$.** Over non-algebraically-closed fields, the weak Nullstellensatz does not apply. Over $\mathbb{R}$, $x^{2} + 1 = 0$ has no solutions.
- **Using floating-point arithmetic for exact algebraic computations.** Gröbner basis computations require exact rational or finite-field arithmetic.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise 1

Compute a Gröbner basis for $I = (x^{2} + y, xy + y)$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$ with lex order $x > y$.

**Solution.** $f_{1} = x^{2} + y$, $f_{2} = xy + y$. $S(f_{1}, f_{2}) = y(x^{2} + y) - x(xy + y) = y^{2} - xy = y(y - x)$. Reduce modulo $f_{2}$: $y(y - x) + (xy + y) = y^{2} + y$. So $f_{3} = y^{2} + y$. Now $S(f_{2}, f_{3}) = y(xy + y) - x(y^{2} + y) = y^{2} - xy = y(y - x)$, which reduces to $0$ using $f_{2}$ and $f_{3}$. The basis $\{x^{2} + y, xy + y, y^{2} + y\}$ is a Gröbner basis.

### Exercise 2

Solve the system $x^{2} + y^{2} = 2$, $xy = 1$ over $\mathbb{C}$ using elimination.

**Solution.** From $xy = 1$, $y = 1/x$. Substitute: $x^{2} + 1/x^{2} = 2$, so $x^{4} - 2x^{2} + 1 = 0$, giving $(x^{2} - 1)^{2} = 0$. Thus $x = \pm 1$, and $y = \pm 1$ with the same sign. The solutions are $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$, each with multiplicity $2$ in the algebraic sense.

### Exercise 3

Show that the ideal $(x^{2} + y^{2} + 1) \subseteq \mathbb{R}[x, y]$ has no real zeros but is not the unit ideal.

**Solution.** For real $x, y$, $x^{2} + y^{2} + 1 \geq 1 > 0$, so there are no real zeros. However, $1 \notin (x^{2} + y^{2} + 1)$ because the polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ and does not divide $1$.

### Exercise 4

Count the solutions in $\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}$ of $x^{2} + y^{2} = 1$.

**Solution.** In $\mathbb{F}_{3}$, the squares are $0^{2} = 0$, $1^{2} = 1$, $2^{2} = 1$. So $x^{2}, y^{2} \in \{0, 1\}$. The pairs $(x^{2}, y^{2})$ summing to $1$ are $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$. For $(0, 1)$: $x = 0$, $y \in \{1, 2\}$, giving $2$ solutions. For $(1, 0)$: $x \in \{1, 2\}$, $y = 0$, giving $2$ solutions. Total: $4$ solutions.
