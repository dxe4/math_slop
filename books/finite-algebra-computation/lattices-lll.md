
# Lattices and LLL

An integer basis can describe a lattice using very long, nearly parallel vectors even when that lattice contains short simple vectors. Lattice reduction changes the basis by reversible integer operations. The LLL algorithm proves that a finite sequence of such operations produces a short first vector, with a quantified approximation guarantee.

Prepare [Smith normal form](modules-over-pids.md), inner products and Gram–Schmidt from [linear algebra](#/books/linear-algebra-structure-proofs). We work with linearly independent column vectors b₁,…,b_n in Zᵐ, with n≤m. All Gram–Schmidt quantities in the algorithm are computed exactly as rational numbers. Finite-precision variants require separate error analysis and are not established by the proof here.

## 1. A lattice and its basis changes

**Definition 11.1.** The lattice generated by B=(b₁,…,b_n) is
$$L(B)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^n z_i b_i:z_i\in\mathbb Z\right\}.$$
Its rank is n. The covolume in its real span is det(BᵀB)^(1/2), not a square determinant when m>n. Linear independence makes the Gram determinant positive.

**Proposition 11.2 (Unimodular changes).** If U∈GL_n(Z), then L(BU)=L(B). Conversely two integer bases of the same lattice are related by such a U. Their covolumes agree.

*Proof.* U maps Zⁿ bijectively to itself, so B applied to its image gives the same lattice. Conversely, express each new basis column as an integer combination of old columns, giving B′=BU with U integral. Express the old basis in the new one, giving an integral inverse because the real-coordinate map B is injective. Thus det U=±1. Finally det((BU)ᵀBU)=(det U)²det(BᵀB)=det(BᵀB). ∎

Swapping basis columns or subtracting an integer multiple of one column from another is unimodular. Multiplying a column by two is not: it can replace the lattice by a proper sublattice. A short real-linear combination is therefore irrelevant unless its coefficients are integers.

The shortest nonzero length λ₁(L) exists in this setting. Every nonzero lattice vector is an integer vector with positive integer squared norm, so the nonempty set of those squared norms has a least value. This is an existence statement for an exact shortest vector, not a claim that LLL always finds it.

## 2. Gram–Schmidt records the geometry

Define orthogonal vectors and coefficients recursively by
$$b_i^*=b_i-\sum_{j<i}\mu_{ij}b_j^*,\qquad
\mu_{ij}=\frac{\langle b_i,b_j^*\rangle}{\|b_j^*\|^2}.$$
Independence ensures every denominator is positive. With integer input all entries are rational. The b_i* need not be lattice vectors, because their construction uses rational coefficients; they measure perpendicular components of the integer basis.

**Definition 11.3 (LLL conditions).** Fix δ=3/4. A basis is LLL-reduced when
$$|\mu_{ij}|\le\tfrac12\quad(j<i)$$
and, for i≥2,
$$\|b_i^*\|^2\ge(\delta-\mu_{i,i-1}^2)\|b_{i-1}^*\|^2.$$
The first is size reduction. The second is the Lovász condition. Neither says that the basis is orthogonal or that its vectors are individually shortest.

To size-reduce b_i by b_j, replace it by b_i−r b_j for a nearest integer r to μ_ij. Process j from i−1 down to one. The coefficient μ_ij changes to μ_ij−r and enters [−1/2,1/2]. Earlier-index changes do not disturb already reduced later coefficients, because b_j lies in the span of the first j orthogonal vectors. A fixed tie convention makes the choice at half-integers deterministic; either nearest choice gives the proved inequality.

## 3. The exact algorithm

Begin at k=2, reduce b_k against all earlier vectors, and inspect the adjacent Lovász condition. If it holds, move to k+1. If it fails, swap b_(k−1) and b_k, recompute the affected orthogonal data, and return to max(2,k−1). Stop after passing the last vector.

```text
LLL(B):
    require columns of B are integer and linearly independent
    U = identity_matrix(number_of_columns)
    k = 2
    while k <= number_of_columns:
        compute exact Gram_Schmidt_data(B)
        for j from k-1 down to 1:
            r = nearest_integer(mu[k,j])
            column_k(B) -= r * column_j(B)
            column_k(U) -= r * column_j(U)
            recompute exact Gram_Schmidt_data(B)
        if norm2_star[k] >= (3/4 - mu[k,k-1]^2)*norm2_star[k-1]:
            k += 1
        else:
            swap columns k and k-1 in both B and U
            k = max(2, k-1)
    return B, U
```

This is inert reference pseudocode with one-based mathematical indexing. Recomputing all orthogonal data is deliberately simple; an efficient implementation can update it locally with its own proved formulas. Every column operation applied to B is also applied to U, preserving the equation B_current=B_initial U. The accumulator is an exact lattice-equivalence certificate.

An invariant is that the prefix before the current k has already passed its size and Lovász conditions. Size reduction of the new column leaves that prefix unchanged. Passing advances the prefix. A swap can disturb the adjacent earlier level, which is why the algorithm moves back instead of blindly continuing forward. The earlier prefix ending before that disturbed pair remains reduced.

## 4. A positive integer potential proves termination

Let B_j consist of the first j basis columns and put
$$d_j=\det(B_j^TB_j)=\prod_{i=1}^j\|b_i^*\|^2,\qquad
D=\prod_{j=1}^n d_j.$$
Every d_j is a positive integer because its Gram matrix is integral and positive definite. Therefore D is a positive integer, even though the individual orthogonal lengths can be rational.

**Theorem 11.4 (Termination).** Size reduction leaves D unchanged, and a swap caused by a failed Lovász condition multiplies D by a factor less than δ. Consequently exact LLL terminates.

*Proof.* Replacing b_k by b_k−r b_j for j<k leaves every prefix before k unchanged. A later prefix contains both columns, so its Gram determinant is unchanged by the unimodular operation. Thus all d_i, and D, are unchanged.

For a swap at positions k−1,k, all prefixes except k−1 either contain neither changed position or contain both, so only d_(k−1) changes. Orthogonally projecting onto the complement of the first k−2 vectors, the new vector at position k−1 is b_k*+μ_(k,k−1)b_(k−1)*. Its squared norm is the sum of their squared perpendicular lengths. Hence
$$\frac{d'_{k-1}}{d_{k-1}}=
\frac{\|b_k^*\|^2}{\|b_{k-1}^*\|^2}+\mu_{k,k-1}^2<\delta,$$
where the strict inequality is exactly the failed Lovász condition. This is also D′/D.

After s swaps, D<δˢD_initial, while D≥1. Thus only finitely many swaps are possible. Between swaps, there are finitely many reductions for each k and k advances at most n times before stopping. An infinite execution would therefore require infinitely many swaps, already ruled out. The final invariant gives every LLL condition. ∎

This proof is stronger than saying a basis “looks shorter after a swap”. Some individual columns can become longer. The integer Gram potential is the quantity that decreases. It also avoids applying an unproved rounding argument to small computed Gram–Schmidt lengths.

If every initial vector has length at most M≥1, Hadamard gives d_j≤M^(2j), so D_initial≤M^(n(n+1)). The potential therefore bounds the number of swaps in terms of n and log M. A complete bit-complexity bound also needs bounds on intermediate coefficients and arithmetic costs; this swap count alone is not that full theorem.

## 5. The short-vector guarantee

**Theorem 11.5 (Approximation bound).** For a δ=3/4 LLL-reduced basis,
$$\|b_1\|\le 2^{(n-1)/2}\lambda_1(L).$$

*Proof.* Size reduction gives μ_(i,i−1)²≤1/4. The Lovász condition therefore implies ||b_i*||²≥(1/2)||b_(i−1)*||². Iterating yields ||b_i*||²≥2^(−(i−1))||b₁||².

For any nonzero v=∑z_i b_i in the lattice, take the largest k with z_k≠0. Its component along b_k* is z_k b_k*, because earlier basis vectors have no component there and later coefficients vanish. Since z_k is a nonzero integer,
$$\|v\|^2\ge\|b_k^*\|^2
\ge2^{-(k-1)}\|b_1\|^2
\ge2^{-(n-1)}\|b_1\|^2.$$
Apply this to a shortest nonzero v and take positive square roots. ∎

The estimate can be far from equality. It is a proved dimension-dependent approximation factor, not an exact solution of every shortest-vector problem. The proof also depends on integer coefficients: a real coefficient z_k could be arbitrarily small.

## 6. Three complete reductions

**Worked example 11.6 (A large removable shear).** Take b₁=(1,0), b₂=(N,1) with integer N≥1. The first orthogonal vector is b₁ itself, μ₂₁=N, and b₂*=(0,1). Size reduction subtracts N b₁ and returns the standard basis (1,0),(0,1). Both orthogonal squared lengths are one and the Lovász inequality reads 1≥3/4. The lattice is Z² throughout, with shortest length one. The large original second coordinate coefficient was a presentation choice, not evidence of a sparse sublattice.

**Worked example 11.7 (A swap is necessary).** Reverse that basis: b₁=(N,1), b₂=(1,0), N≥2. Then ||b₁*||²=N²+1, μ₂₁=N/(N²+1), and ||b₂*||²=1/(N²+1). The coefficient already has magnitude below one-half, so size reduction makes no change. But the Lovász right side is (3/4)(N²+1)−N²/(N²+1), greater than the left side for N≥2. Thus a swap is required.

After swapping, size reduction is the previous example and returns the standard basis. The initial full determinant has absolute value one, while d₁=N²+1, so D_initial=N²+1. The final D equals one. The potential decreases even though the lattice covolume stays one, showing why covolume alone cannot measure progress.

**Worked example 11.8 (Already reduced does not mean orthogonal).** Let b₁=(2,0), b₂=(1,2). Then μ₂₁=1/2, ||b₁*||²=4 and ||b₂*||²=4. Choose the half-integer tie toward zero; no size step is needed. The Lovász inequality is 4≥(3/4−1/4)·4=2, so the basis is reduced but not orthogonal.

Its vectors are (2a+b,2b) with integers a,b. If b=0, a nonzero vector has length at least two. If b≠0, its second coordinate already has magnitude at least two, so its length is at least two, with equality impossible when b is odd and the first coordinate cannot vanish. The vector (2,0) attains length two, so λ₁=2. The first vector is exactly shortest here, although the general guarantee was only a factor √2 in dimension two.

## 7. What a reduced basis certifies

A result should include the integer matrix U, the equation B_out=B_in U and det U=±1, or a transcript of unimodular operations proving that condition. Exact Gram–Schmidt data can then certify the size and Lovász inequalities. Together those facts prove both lattice preservation and the approximation theorem's hypotheses.

A short vector alone does not certify that it belongs to the intended lattice: its integer coefficients in the original basis are needed. A claimed near-relation among real numbers similarly needs a declared approximation error budget before it becomes an exact algebraic relation. LLL can locate promising integer combinations; it does not turn uncertain decimal input into a theorem about an unknown real constant.

Rational input can be handled by clearing a common positive denominator, reducing the resulting integer basis, then undoing that uniform scale. Scaling every vector together preserves integer combination structure up to a known overall factor. Scaling separate columns by unrelated nonunits generally changes the lattice and cannot be treated as the same operation.

## 8. A rounding application and its precise limit

An ordered lattice basis also gives a simple nearest-plane approximation to a point $v$ in its real span. Process $i=n,n-1,\ldots,1$. At each stage round the coefficient of the current residual along $b_i^*$ to a nearest integer $c_i$, add $c_i b_i$ to the chosen lattice vector and subtract it from the residual. Descending order ensures later adjustments do not change a previously settled higher orthogonal component.

**Proposition 11.9 (Nearest-plane residual bound).** This procedure returns a lattice vector $w$ with
$$\|v-w\|^2\le\tfrac14\sum_{i=1}^n\|b_i^*\|^2.$$

*Proof.* After the step for $i$, the remaining coefficient of $b_i^*$ has magnitude at most one-half. Earlier basis vectors lie in the span of the first $i-1$ orthogonal vectors, so subsequent steps leave that coefficient unchanged. The final residual therefore has orthogonal expansion $\sum_i\theta_i b_i^*$ with $|\theta_i|\le1/2$. Pythagoras gives the claimed inequality. The selected coefficients $c_i$ are integers, so $w$ genuinely belongs to the lattice. ∎

**Worked example 11.10 (Rounding need not find the closest point).** Use the reduced basis $(2,0),(1,2)$ and target $v=(1,1)$. Its coefficient along $b_2^*=(0,2)$ is one-half. With the earlier tie-toward-zero convention, choose zero. The remaining coefficient along $b_1^*=(2,0)$ is also one-half, again rounded to zero. The returned point is therefore the origin, at distance $\sqrt2$.

But the lattice point $(1,2)$ is at distance one from the target. Thus this exact rounding calculation is not a closest-vector answer. The bound remains correct: its right side is $(4+4)/4=2$, matching the returned squared residual. A reduced basis helps control approximation while leaving a real distinction between an available nearby point and a proved closest point.

If the target lies outside the lattice's real span, its perpendicular component cannot be changed by subtracting lattice vectors. One can first project to the span and then add that fixed perpendicular squared distance to the displayed bound. Omitting it would claim an impossible improvement in a direction the lattice cannot reach.

The parameter interval also has a mathematical purpose. For any fixed $1/4<\delta<1$, the same potential proof uses $\delta<1$ to force decreasing swaps. The length proof uses $\delta-1/4>0$ and gives first-vector factor $(\delta-1/4)^{-(n-1)/2}$. The chosen value $3/4$ makes that factor $2^{(n-1)/2}$. Increasing the parameter changes both the permitted adjacent geometry and the swap decrease bound; it is not a cosmetic tuning constant detached from the proofs.

## When dimension two gives an exact shortest vector

A stronger two-dimensional reduction condition yields an exact answer. Suppose a basis satisfies |μ₂₁|≤1/2 and ||b₁||≤||b₂||. Then b₁ is a shortest nonzero lattice vector. To prove this, write any vector as mb₁+nb₂. If n=0 its length is at least ||b₁|| unless it is zero. Since ||b₂||²=μ₂₁²||b₁||²+||b₂*||², the length assumption gives ||b₂*||²≥3||b₁||²/4. For |n|≥2, the perpendicular component alone therefore has squared length at least three times ||b₁||². For n=±1, minimising the parallel component over integral m chooses zero as a nearest integer to ∓μ₂₁; its minimum total squared length is ||b₂||², which is at least ||b₁||². Ties at a half-integer do not change that minimum.

This proves exact optimality under the stated stronger conditions. The usual Lovász condition with parameter three-quarters does not imply the second condition, so the general LLL approximation factor cannot be silently replaced by equality. The earlier basis (2,0),(1,2) satisfies both conditions and illustrates the stronger theorem.

Orthogonal changes of ambient coordinates preserve the reduction inequalities exactly. They preserve every inner product, so the Gram–Schmidt coefficients and squared orthogonal lengths are unchanged. Hence size reduction, the Lovász tests and the approximation bound all have the same truth values after such a change. Integer combination coefficients are unchanged as well, so the same unimodular basis certificate transports to the rotated lattice. An arbitrary invertible change of coordinates does not preserve this Euclidean geometry: a shear or unequal coordinate scaling can change lengths and angles while preserving linear independence. The unimodular operations inside the algorithm change the generating basis of one fixed lattice; an ambient coordinate transformation acts on the whole geometric problem. Distinguishing these two operations prevents an arithmetic certificate from being mistaken for a metric invariance statement.

The shear has coordinate matrix with columns (1,0),(−7,1), determinant one and integral inverse. It maps Z² bijectively to itself. <details>

The coefficient is ⟨(13,1),(1,0)⟩/1=13. Subtracting thirteen copies of b₁ gives (0,1). The operation is unimodular and the output basis is orthogonal with both lengths one. There is no floating-point small-pivot decision in this calculation.

### Exercise 3 — A positive integer potential
<a id="finite-lll-ex-3"></a>

Why are the prefix Gram determinants integers even though Gram–Schmidt vectors can have rational coordinates?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Define the determinants directly from the original integer prefix matrices.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Each prefix B_j has integer entries, so B_jᵀB_j is an integer matrix and its determinant is an integer. Independence makes it positive. Gram–Schmidt proves that same determinant is the product of the rational squared orthogonal lengths; the product is integral even when individual factors are not. This integrality supports the lower bound D≥1 used in termination.

</details>

### Exercise 4 — Why size reduction is not enough
<a id="finite-lll-ex-4"></a>

For b₁=(3,1), b₂=(1,0), show size reduction leaves the coefficient unchanged but the Lovász condition fails.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use ||b₁||²=10 and μ₂₁=3/10.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The nearest integer to 3/10 is zero, so no size step occurs. The perpendicular squared length is 1−9/10=1/10. The Lovász right side is (3/4−9/100)·10=33/5, much greater than 1/10. Thus a swap is required despite the small coefficient. Size reduction controls coefficients, while the adjacent length condition controls a different part of the geometry.

</details>

### Exercise 6 — The highest nonzero coefficient
<a id="finite-lll-ex-6"></a>

Why does a lattice vector whose last nonzero basis coefficient is z_k have norm at least ||b_k*||?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look at its orthogonal component in that direction and use integrality.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Earlier basis vectors lie in the span of the first k−1 orthogonal vectors, and later coefficients are zero. Hence the b_k* component is z_k b_k*. The nonzero integer z_k has absolute value at least one, so this component alone has norm at least ||b_k*||. The total norm is no smaller than any orthogonal component's norm.

</details>

### Exercise 7 — Covolume versus potential
<a id="finite-lll-ex-7"></a>

Why cannot lattice covolume alone prove termination of an algorithm using only unimodular basis changes?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Covolume is invariant under every allowed step.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Every allowed step preserves the lattice and therefore its covolume, so that quantity cannot strictly decrease at swaps. The prefix Gram determinants depend on the ordered basis, even though the full determinant does not, and their product supplies a decreasing integer potential. The example with reversed sheared basis has constant covolume one but potential dropping from N²+1 to one.

</details>

### Exercise 8 — Clearing denominators
<a id="finite-lll-ex-8"></a>

Explain why multiplying an entire rational basis by one common positive denominator is legitimate, and how to recover lengths afterward.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Every integer combination is multiplied by the same scalar.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

If cB is integral, then L(cB)=cL(B). Reducing cB uses a unimodular U, so dividing the result cBU by c gives BU with the same original lattice. All vector lengths and λ₁ scale by c, leaving the approximation ratio unchanged. Unrelated scaling of separate columns would not have this uniform relationship and can alter the integer lattice.

</details>

### Exercise 9 — A membership certificate
<a id="finite-lll-ex-9"></a>

What information certifies that a claimed short vector belongs to the original lattice?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Supply integer coordinates in the original basis.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

A vector z∈Zⁿ with v=B_in z certifies membership. If v is an output basis vector, the corresponding column of the recorded unimodular U provides those coordinates. Merely giving small real coordinates of v or its length does not prove membership in the intended integer span. Exact reconstruction is the required equation.

</details>

### Exercise 10 — What the swap bound omits
<a id="finite-lll-ex-10"></a>

Why does a logarithmic bound on the number of swaps not by itself prove a complete polynomial bit-time bound?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Arithmetic operands can change size during the computation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

A bit-time analysis must bound intermediate integer and rational coefficient sizes and the cost of exact arithmetic on them, as well as counts of reductions and orthogonal updates. The potential bounds one class of steps but does not automatically bound all operand lengths. The termination and approximation theorems here are complete statements, while a stronger complexity theorem needs those additional arguments.

</details>

## Summary

Unimodular changes preserve a lattice while improving its ordered basis. Exact Gram–Schmidt coefficients define size reduction and the Lovász condition; a positive integer prefix-Gram potential proves finite termination. The reduced first vector has a quantified shortest-vector approximation, not an unconditional exact solution. The final project combines these certificates with the book's finite-field, polynomial, module and group computations.
