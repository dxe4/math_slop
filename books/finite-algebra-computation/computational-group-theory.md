
# Permutation Groups and Schreier–Sims

## From theory to algorithm

The preceding chapters developed group theory abstractly and representation-theoretically. This chapter turns those concepts into algorithms that a computer can execute. We study permutation group algorithms, coset enumeration, and the computation of conjugacy classes and character tables. The emphasis is on correctness, termination, and complexity bounds.

## Permutation representations and data structures

A finite group $G$ of degree $n$ acting on a set $\{1, \dots, n\}$ is represented by a generating set $S$ of permutations. The group elements are not stored explicitly; instead, algorithms manipulate words in the generators.

**Schreier-Sims algorithm.** This fundamental algorithm computes a **base** and **strong generating set** (BSGS) for a permutation group. A base is a sequence $(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_k)$ whose pointwise stabilizer is trivial. Put $G^{(1)}=G$ and let $G^{(i+1)}$ be the stabilizer of $\beta_i$ in $G^{(i)}$. A generating set $S$ is strong relative to the base when $S\cap G^{(i)}$ generates $G^{(i)}$ for every $i$; Schreier generators are used to construct this property.

The BSGS structure supports:
- membership testing (is a given permutation in $G$?);
- order computation ($|G|=\prod_i [G^{(i)}:G^{(i+1)}]=\prod_i |\beta_i^{G^{(i)}}|$);
- random element generation.

**Complexity.** Standard deterministic and randomized Schreier-Sims variants run in polynomial time under their stated permutation-operation and membership-test models. Exact exponents and probability guarantees depend on the variant, so an implementation should cite the version whose bound it reports.

## The permutation contract

This chapter uses left actions and composes permutations right to left: (gh)(x)=g(h(x)). An array encoding must be a bijection of its declared finite carrier. Repeated images, out-of-range entries or mismatched carrier sizes do not define the advertised permutation. Inverse arrays satisfy p⁻¹[p[i]]=i at every index.

The primary computation below assumes S is closed under inverses, adjoining inverse generators if necessary. An orbit traversal then explores generator edges without needing to enumerate the whole group. Store for every orbit point γ a transversal element t_γ∈G with t_γ(β)=γ, choosing t_β=1.

**Proposition 7.1 (Orbit traversal).** Starting at β and repeatedly adding s(γ) for s∈S and known γ finds exactly the G-orbit of β. On a carrier of n points it processes at most n points and n|S| generator edges.

*Proof.* Every reached point is a word in S applied to β, so belongs to the orbit. Conversely every group element is a finite word because S includes inverses; induction on word length shows the traversal eventually reaches its image of β. Each point is enqueued only once, giving the finite bound. If a new point is s(γ), the word s t_γ supplies its transversal. ∎

## Schreier generators with a proof

**Theorem 7.2 (Schreier’s lemma).** With the orbit and transversals just described, the point stabiliser G_β is generated by
$$h_{s,\gamma}=t_{s(\gamma)}^{-1}s\,t_\gamma,\qquad s\in S,\ \gamma\in G\beta.$$

*Proof.* Each displayed element fixes β by following β→γ→s(γ)→β, so their generated subgroup lies in G_β. Conversely take g∈G_β and write g=s_l⋯s₁. Put γ_i=s_i⋯s₁(β), with γ₀=β. The corresponding h_i satisfies s_i=t_(γ_i)h_i t_(γ_(i−1))⁻¹. Substituting into the word for g cancels adjacent transversal factors, leaving
$$g=t_{\gamma_l}h_l\cdots h_1t_{\gamma_0}^{-1}=h_l\cdots h_1,$$
because γ_l=γ₀=β and t_β=1. Thus every stabiliser element belongs to the generated subgroup, proving equality. ∎

The inverse on the first transversal is part of the left-action convention. Reversing it without also reversing the convention can produce elements that do not fix β. The fixing equation itself is a simple certificate for each proposed Schreier generator.

A direct recursive implementation computes these finite generator lists exactly. Choose a point moved by the current group, compute its orbit and Schreier generators, then recurse on that stabiliser. If no point is moved by any supplied generator, every generator is identity and the current group is trivial. At most n chosen points are needed, since a permutation fixing every point is identity.

The resulting base β₁,…,β_k gives a chain G=G₁≥G₂≥⋯≥G_(k+1)=1, with each next group fixing the next base point. Taking the union of the generator lists gives a strong generating set: its intersection with every G_i contains the complete generating set constructed for that level and consists only of elements of that level. This proves exactness without assuming an approximate or random list is already strong.

The naive recursion may produce many redundant generators. Efficient Schreier–Sims variants reduce those lists and can have substantially better complexity. The finite proof here establishes correctness and termination of the declared construction; it does not derive a polynomial bit-time bound for every possible implementation called Schreier–Sims.

## Sifting decides membership completely

**Theorem 7.3 (Sifting).** Given an exact stabiliser chain, orbit transversals and a permutation g on the same carrier, perform the following at each level i. If γ=g(β_i) is outside the recorded orbit under G_i, reject. Otherwise replace g by t_γ⁻¹g. After the last level, accept exactly when the remaining permutation is identity. This accepts precisely the elements of G and yields a generator word for every accepted input.

*Proof.* Each replacement fixes β_i and preserves fixation of earlier base points, because its transversal lies in G_i. If the original input belongs to G, its successive residues lie in the correct stabilisers, so their point images lie in the recorded orbits. The final residue is in G_(k+1)=1, so it is accepted.

Conversely each recorded transversal is in G. An accepted identity residue expresses the original input as a product of those transversals, hence as an element of G and, using their stored words, as a word in the original generators. A rejected orbit image could not occur for a member of that level's stabiliser. A nonidentity final residue also rules out membership, since the final stabiliser inside G is trivial. ∎

The final identity condition cannot be omitted. A base is chosen for G, not necessarily for the full ambient symmetric group. An outside permutation can fix every base point. Sifting must establish that the residual map is actually identity on the entire carrier.

## Exact order and a program outline

Orbit–stabiliser gives [G_i:G_(i+1)]=|G_iβ_i|. Multiplying those indexes yields
$$|G|=\prod_i|G_i\beta_i|.$$
This is a proved count from exact stabilisers, rather than an estimate obtained from how many random words happened to be generated.

```text
build_chain(S, carrier):
    validate every generator as a permutation of carrier
    add inverse generators to S
    if every generator is identity: return empty_chain
    choose beta moved by some generator
    orbit, transversal = breadth_first_orbit(beta, S)
    T = empty generator list
    for gamma in orbit:
        for s in S:
            append inverse(transversal[s(gamma)]) * s * transversal[gamma]
                   to T unless this is identity
    return (beta, orbit, transversal) followed by build_chain(T, carrier)
```

This is inert pseudocode. Its group multiplication is composition in the stated order. The returned recursive generators fix β, so subsequent chosen points can be taken outside the already fixed prefix. Every orbit and generator loop is finite, and the stabiliser depth is bounded by the carrier size. Duplicates can be removed by exact array equality; deleting a generator merely because it looks similar or has the same cycle lengths is not justified.

**Worked example 7.4 (A complete S₃ chain).** Let r=(123), s=(12), acting on {1,2,3}. The orbit of one has three points, with t₁=1,t₂=r,t₃=r². A Schreier generator is r⁻¹s=(23), fixing one. The exact stabiliser of one has order two: a permutation fixing one can only swap or fix the other two points, and (23) supplies the swap. Choose the next base point two, whose orbit there is {2,3}. The next stabiliser is trivial, giving order 3·2=6.

To sift g=(13), its first point image is three, so replace it by (r²)⁻¹g=r(13)=(23). This residual sends the second base point two to three, so multiplying by the inverse transversal (23) leaves identity. The recovered decomposition is g=r²(23). Each step is a complete exact permutation calculation.

**Worked example 7.5 (A cyclic subgroup and an outsider).** Let G=⟨(1234)⟩. The orbit of one has four points and its stabiliser in G is trivial, so base (1) suffices and |G|=4. The outsider (12) sends one to two, passing the orbit condition with transversal r=(1234). But r⁻¹(12) is nonidentity despite fixing one. The final residual condition rejects it. A routine accepting merely because it fixed the chosen base after one stage would incorrectly enlarge the group.

**Worked example 7.6 (The square symmetry group).** Let r=(1234), s=(24). Their actions preserve a square, with srs=r⁻¹. The orbit of one has four points, and its stabiliser contains 1,s. No further symmetry of the square fixes that vertex while changing the two adjacent vertices independently, so that stabiliser has order two; equivalently the normal forms r^i s^j give at most eight elements and the displayed orbit/stabiliser already gives eight. A base (1,2) has orbit lengths four and two, proving order eight. The group has degree four, not order four.

## A complete alternating-group chain

**Worked example 7.7.** Let a=(123) and b=(124) in S₄, with permutations composed from right to left, and let G be the subgroup they generate. Both generators are even, so G is contained in A₄ and has at most twelve elements. The orbit of four contains four itself, b(4)=1, b inverse(4)=2 and a inverse b(4)=3. Hence that orbit has four elements. Its stabiliser contains the three distinct powers of a, since a fixes four. Orbit–stabiliser therefore gives at least four times three elements in G. The upper bound is attained: G=A₄, and the stabiliser is exactly the cyclic group generated by a.

Choose first-stage transversals t₄=1, t₁=b, t₂=b inverse and t₃=a inverse b. At the next stage choose point one. Its orbit under the stabiliser is {1,2,3}, with transversals 1,a,a². Fixing both four and one forces the remaining stabiliser to be trivial, because neither nonidentity power of a fixes one. Thus (4,1) is a base and the orbit lengths are four and three.

Every element g has a unique expression t_(g(4)) a^j for j in {0,1,2}. To prove existence, the residual t_(g(4)) inverse g fixes four and hence is a power of a. For uniqueness, applying two proposed expressions to four makes their transversals equal, and cancellation makes their powers of a equal. These twelve words give an exact enumeration and a membership certificate in this small example. The proof also separates the two necessary bounds: exhibited orbit points and stabiliser elements give a lower bound, while containment in the even permutations gives the upper bound that proves no stabiliser generators are missing.

## Coset enumeration

Given a finitely presented group $G = \langle X \mid R \rangle$ and a subgroup $H = \langle Y \rangle$, **coset enumeration** attempts to construct the permutation representation of $G$ on the cosets of $H$. The Todd-Coxeter algorithm is the classical method.

The algorithm maintains a coset table with entries $C[i][x]$ recording the coset reached from coset $i$ by generator $x$. It applies the relations $R$ to deduce coincidences and fills in undefined entries. With a fair strategy that eventually processes every pending deduction and defines every needed entry, a finite-index instance terminates with the complete coset table. An arbitrary sequence of choices can postpone required deductions indefinitely, while an infinite-index instance need not terminate.

**Example.** Enumerate cosets of $H = \langle a \rangle$ in $G = \langle a, b \mid a^{3}, b^{2}, abab \rangle \cong S_{3}$.

**Solution.** Start with coset $1=H$. Since $a\in H$, set $1^a=1$, and define $1^b=2$. The relation $b^2=1$ gives $2^b=1$. Tracing $abab=1$ forces $2^a=2$. Thus $a$ fixes both cosets and $b$ swaps them. This is the two-point coset action, not the regular action, which would have six points. Since $[S_3:\langle a\rangle]=2$, the completed table has exactly two cosets.

## Computing conjugacy classes

For a permutation group, conjugacy classes can be computed by orbit-stabiliser methods. The centraliser of an element $g$ is the set of permutations commuting with $g$; its size can be computed from the BSGS. The conjugacy class size is then $|G| / |C_{G}(g)|$.

For the symmetric group $S_{n}$, conjugacy classes are determined by cycle type, so enumeration is trivial: generate all partitions of $n$.

**Algorithm for general permutation groups.**
1. Keep the input group $G$ fixed and initialize an uncovered set $U=G$. While $U$ is nonempty, choose $g \in U$.
2. Compute $C_G(g)$ and the conjugacy class of $g$ under the original $G$, using a correct BSGS; the class has size $|G|/|C_G(g)|$.
3. Record the class and remove it from $U$ only; never remove elements from the ambient group $G$.
4. The chosen $g$ belongs to its class, so each pass strictly decreases $|U|$. Stop when $U$ is empty; conjugacy classes are equal or disjoint, so the recorded classes partition the original $G$.

## Computing character tables

The **Burnside algorithm** computes the character table from the class algebra. Let $C_{1}, \dots, C_{r}$ be the conjugacy classes with class sums $K_{i} = \sum_{g \in C_{i}} g$ in the group algebra $\mathbb{C}[G]$. The class sums commute and satisfy

$$K_{i} K_{j} = \sum_{k} c_{ijk} K_{k}$$

with structure constants $c_{ijk} \in \mathbb{Z}$. The commuting class-multiplication matrices have simultaneous eigenspaces indexed by irreducible characters. On the character $\chi$, multiplication by $K_i$ has eigenvalue $|C_i|\chi(g_i)/\chi(1)$. Thus the eigenvalues determine character values only after this class-size and degree scaling; repeated eigenspaces may require several class matrices to separate them.

Finite-field character algorithms choose a prime $p\nmid |G|$ and a field containing the needed roots of unity; for example one can require $p\equiv1\pmod e$ when $e$ is the group exponent. They then use a justified modular-to-characteristic-zero lifting or reconstruction step. The relevant roots have orders dividing $e$; referring to “$p$-th roots of unity” in characteristic $p$ would be wrong.

## Common errors

- **Assuming coset enumeration always terminates.** Fair Todd-Coxeter strategies terminate for finite-index inputs; infinite-index inputs may run forever, and fairness is part of the termination statement.
- **Treating modular output as a complex character table.** Reconstruction needs a theorem and enough modulus or lifting precision to distinguish the permitted algebraic integers; a single universal size inequality is not a substitute for the chosen algorithm's hypotheses.
- **Confusing group order with permutation degree.** A permutation group of degree $n$ is a subgroup of $S_n$, so its order is at most $n!$, though it can still be exponentially large relative to the input degree. A BSGS stores it without listing all elements.

A conjugacy class can be explored without first listing every element of the group. Start with one element x and repeatedly conjugate every discovered element by each supplied generator and its inverse. The discovered set remains in the conjugacy class, and closure under these conjugations ensures closure under conjugation by every word. Thus when the finite queue is exhausted it is the whole class. A stored predecessor word certifies each discovered conjugate exactly as in an ordinary orbit search. The stabiliser of x in this conjugation action is its centraliser, so orbit–stabiliser recovers the class size as the group order divided by the centraliser order. This is a second action in which the same certified orbit machinery applies; it does not require treating a conjugate as an unlabelled table row.

### Exercise 1

Apply the Todd-Coxeter algorithm to enumerate cosets of $H = \langle b \rangle$ in $G = \langle a, b \mid a^{4}, b^{2}, abab \rangle \cong D_{4}$. How many cosets are there?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Track the right cosets H,Ha,Ha²,Ha³; b reverses the rotation exponent.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Start with coset $1 = H$, so $1^{b} = 1$. Define $1^{a} = 2$. Then $2^{a} = 3$, $3^{a} = 4$, and $4^{a} = 1$ from $a^{4}$. From $abab$, trace $1^{a} = 2$, $2^{b} = ?$, and require $2^{b} = 4$ (since $1^{abab} = 1$). Then $4^{a} = 1$, $1^{b} = 1$, consistent. Now $2^{b} = 4$ implies $4^{b} = 2$ from $b^{2}$. Check $3^{b}$: from $3^{a} = 4$, $4^{b} = 2$, $2^{a} = 3$, so $3^{abab} = 3$ requires $3^{b} = 3$. The coset table has four cosets, matching $[D_{4} : \langle b \rangle] = 4$.

</details>

### Exercise 2

Explain why the Schreier-Sims algorithm always terminates.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the exact recursive Schreier construction and a base of at most the carrier size.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The algorithm builds a chain of stabilisers $G = G^{(1)} \geq G^{(2)} \geq \dots \geq G^{(k+1)} = \{e\}$. At each level $i$, it computes the orbit of $\beta_{i}$ under $G^{(i)}$ and Schreier generators for the stabiliser $G^{(i+1)}$. The orbit size is at most $n$, and the depth $k$ is at most $n$. Each new Schreier generator is tested for membership in $G^{(i+1)}$; if it is new, it enlarges $G^{(i+1)}$. Since $|G|$ is finite, this process stabilises after finitely many steps.

</details>

### Exercise 3

Compute the structure constants $c_{ijk}$ for $S_{3}$ with classes $C_{1} = \{e\}$, $C_{2} = \{(12), (13), (23)\}$, $C_{3} = \{(123), (132)\}$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Expand each product of class sums by counting products of elements; for example, $K_2^2$ counts the nine ordered pairs of transpositions by their product.

</details>

<details>

$K_1$ is the identity, so $c_{1jk}=c_{j1k}=\delta_{jk}$. Also $K_2^2=3K_1+3K_3$, $K_2K_3=K_3K_2=2K_2$, and $K_3^2=2K_1+K_3$. Thus, beyond the identity constants, the nonzero constants are $c_{221}=3$, $c_{223}=3$, $c_{232}=c_{322}=2$, $c_{331}=2$, and $c_{333}=1$.

### Exercise 4

Describe how to test whether a given permutation $\pi$ lies in a permutation group $G$ given by a BSGS.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Remove the matching orbit transversal at every level and inspect the final full permutation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Given a base $(\beta_{1}, \dots, \beta_{k})$ and strong generating set, test whether there exists $g_{1} \in G$ with $g_{1}(\beta_{1}) = \pi(\beta_{1})$. This is possible if and only if $\pi(\beta_{1})$ lies in the orbit of $\beta_{1}$ under $G$. If so, find a transversal element $u_{1}$ with $u_{1}(\beta_{1}) = \pi(\beta_{1})$ and replace $\pi$ by $u_{1}^{-1} \pi$. Now $\pi$ fixes $\beta_{1}$; recurse on $G^{(2)}$ with base point $\beta_{2}$. If at any step the image is not in the orbit, $\pi \notin G$. After all levels, membership holds only if the final residue is the identity. An outside permutation can fix every chosen base point when the base is a base for $G$ rather than for the ambient symmetric group; for example, with $G=\langle(12)\rangle\le S_4$ and base $(1)$, the outsider $(34)$ fixes the base point. The final identity test rejects it.

</details>

### Exercise 5 — A Schreier generator fixes the base
<a id="finite-permutation-ex-5"></a>

Prove t_(sγ)⁻¹ s t_γ fixes β.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Follow β through the three maps in right-to-left composition.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The transversal sends β to γ; s sends that point to sγ; the inverse target transversal sends sγ back to β. Thus the composite fixes β and lies in the stabiliser. All three factors belong to G, so the composite is a genuine element of that stabiliser, not merely a permutation fixing the point outside G.

</details>

### Exercise 6 — Orbit count versus group count
<a id="finite-permutation-ex-6"></a>

A complete chain has orbit lengths 5,4,3,2. Find the group order and state why the word complete matters.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply orbit–stabiliser indexes down to the identity subgroup.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The order is 5·4·3·2=120 when the final stabiliser is identity and every level is exact. If a nontrivial final stabiliser remained, its order would be another factor; approximate orbit lists or incomplete strong generators would not justify the count. Group order is derived from the full chain, not simply the first orbit.

For G=⟨(12)⟩≤S₄, show (1) is a base but the outsider (34) fixes it.

### Exercise 8 — An orbit transversal word
<a id="finite-permutation-ex-8"></a>

If t_γ sends β to γ and a new point is sγ, which product gives its transversal under the stated convention?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Apply the old transversal first.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The product s t_γ sends β first to γ and then to sγ, so it is the required transversal. The reversed product t_γ s generally sends β to a different point. An implementation must use the same composition convention in orbit traversal, Schreier generators and sifting.

### Exercise 10 — Same cycle type is not same element
<a id="finite-permutation-ex-10"></a>

Why cannot duplicate removal replace distinct permutations having the same cycle type by one?

<details>

The two transpositions have the same cycle type but are different permutations. Together they generate S₃, while either alone generates an order-two subgroup. ## Summary

Permutation arrays represent a group through generators and a fixed action convention. Orbit transversals and Schreier’s lemma construct exact stabilisers; a complete chain gives both a group order and a membership decision with generator words. The final identity residual prevents outsiders fixing a base from being accepted. The next chapter develops coset tables and the extra certificates needed when a group is given by a presentation.
