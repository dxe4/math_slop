
# Groebner Bases and Elimination

## Polynomial systems and their geometry

Algebraic geometry studies the zero sets of systems of polynomial equations. When the polynomials have coefficients in a field---especially $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, or a finite field---we can ask computational questions: Does the system have solutions? How many? Can we parametrise them? This chapter introduces the algebraic tools, with emphasis on Gröbner bases and elimination theory, that make these questions algorithmic.

## Affine varieties and ideals

Let $k$ be a field and $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ the polynomial ring. An **affine algebraic variety** is the set

$$V(I) = \{(a_{1}, \dots, a_{n}) \in k^{n} : f(a_{1}, \dots, a_{n}) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}$$

for some ideal $I \subseteq k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$. Conversely, the **vanishing ideal** of a set $X \subseteq k^{n}$ is

$$I(X) = \{f \in k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}] : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in X\}.$$

**Hilbert's Nullstellensatz.** If $k$ is algebraically closed, then $I(V(I)) = \sqrt{I}$, the radical of $I$.

Over finite fields, the Nullstellensatz fails in this form because $k^{n}$ is finite and many polynomials vanish identically. One must work in the algebraic closure or use the ideal-variety correspondence over the finite field directly.

## Monomial orders and Gröbner bases

A **monomial order** $<$ on $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ is a total order on monomials that is a well-ordering and respects multiplication: if $x^{\alpha} < x^{\beta}$, then $x^{\gamma} x^{\alpha} < x^{\gamma} x^{\beta}$.

Common monomial orders:
- **Lexicographic (lex):** $x^{\alpha} < x^{\beta}$ if the leftmost non-zero entry of $\beta - \alpha$ is positive.
- **Graded reverse lexicographic (grevlex):** first compare total degree, then break ties by preferring monomials with smaller rightmost non-zero exponent.

For nonzero $f$, write $\operatorname{LM}(f)$ for its largest monomial, $\operatorname{LC}(f)$ for that monomial's coefficient, and $\operatorname{LT}(f)=\operatorname{LC}(f)\operatorname{LM}(f)$ for the leading term.

A **Gröbner basis** for an ideal $I$ with respect to $<$ is a finite set $G \subseteq I$ such that the ideal generated by leading terms of $G$ equals the ideal generated by leading terms of $I$:

$$(\operatorname{LT}(g) : g \in G) = (\operatorname{LT}(f) : f \in I).$$

**Buchberger's algorithm.** Given a generating set for $I$, compute the $S$-polynomial of each pair:

$$S(f,g)=\frac{L}{\operatorname{LC}(f)\operatorname{LM}(f)}f-\frac{L}{\operatorname{LC}(g)\operatorname{LM}(g)}g,$$

where $L=\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{LM}(f),\operatorname{LM}(g))$. For monic polynomials this is the familiar cancellation formula using the two leading monomials.

Reduce $S(f, g)$ modulo $G$. If the remainder is non-zero, append it to $G$ and repeat. The algorithm terminates because monomial ideals satisfy the ascending chain condition.

## Elimination and the extension theorem

In lexicographic order with $x_{1} > x_{2} > \dots > x_{n}$, a Gröbner basis $G$ for $I$ satisfies the **elimination property**:

$$G \cap k[x_{i}, \dots, x_{n}] \text{ is a Gröbner basis for } I \cap k[x_{i}, \dots, x_{n}].$$

Elimination finds necessary polynomial constraints on the retained coordinates. Back-substitution is a separate **extension step**: a candidate satisfying those constraints must still admit values for the eliminated variables.

Over $\mathbb C$, let $J=I\cap\mathbb C[x_2,\ldots,x_n]$ and let $\pi$ discard the first coordinate. Then

$$\pi(V(I))\subseteq V(J).$$

The **closure theorem** identifies $V(J)$ with the Zariski closure of $\pi(V(I))$, the smallest algebraic set containing the projected solutions. The inclusion need not be equality. Thus computing an elimination ideal does not automatically determine which candidates extend.

**Theorem (Extension, stated).** Let $I=(f_1,\ldots,f_s)\subseteq\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, omitting zero generators. Write

$$f_j=c_j(x_2,\ldots,x_n)x_1^{N_j}+\text{terms of lower degree in }x_1,$$

where $N_j\ge0$ and $c_j$ is a nonzero polynomial. If $p\in V(J)$ and at least one $c_j(p)\ne0$, then some $a_1\in\mathbb C$ makes $(a_1,p)\in V(I)$. This condition is sufficient. Where all $c_j$ vanish, the theorem gives no conclusion; extension may succeed or fail. Generators with $N_j=0$ already belong to $J$ and vanish at every candidate. In particular, a generator of positive degree in $x_1$ with nonzero constant leading coefficient guarantees extension of every candidate.

**A missing witness.** Consider $f=au^2+u+b$ and $g=au+1$, eliminating $u$. Since $f-ug=b$, a common root requires $b=0$. When $a\ne0$ and $b=0$, the unique common root is $u=-1/a$. When $a=0$, however, $g=1$, so no witness exists. The exact projection is therefore $\{(a,0):a\ne0\}$. Its Zariski closure is the entire line $b=0$: a polynomial vanishing at every $(a,0)$ with $a\ne0$ also vanishes at the origin, by the univariate root bound. The elimination variety consequently contains a candidate that cannot extend.

The imported results are stated in [Setiabrata's computational algebra notes, printed pages 48, 50–51 and 53](https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~ls823/4370fa19.pdf).

**Worked example.** Find the solutions in $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ of

$$\begin{cases}
x^{2} + y^{2} = 1, \\\ 
x^{2} - y = 0.
\end{cases}$$

**Solution.** Use lex order $x>y$. Set $f_2=x^2-y$ and subtract it from $f_1=x^2+y^2-1$ to obtain $f_3=y^2+y-1$. The pair $G=\{f_2,f_3\}$ generates the original ideal. Its only $S$-polynomial is

$S(f_2,f_3)=y^2(x^2-y)-x^2(y^2+y-1)=-y^3-x^2y+x^2$.

Reducing $x^2$ to $y$ and $y^2$ to $1-y$ gives zero, so Buchberger's criterion makes $G$ a Gröbner basis. First solve

$y^2+y-1=0,\qquad y=(-1\pm\sqrt5)/2$,

then solve $x^2=y$. Each nonzero value of $y$ has two square roots in $\mathbb C$, giving four complex solutions; two are real.

Here $x^2-y$ is monic in $x$, so its constant leading coefficient guarantees complex extension of every eliminated $y$-candidate; a negative real $y$, however, has no real square root.

## Applications to finite fields

Over $\mathbb{F}_{q}$, Gröbner bases can count solutions, test consistency, and compute elimination ideals. The quotient $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I$ is finite-dimensional over $k$ exactly when the initial ideal contains a positive power of every variable. Equivalently, for each $x_i$ some leading monomial in a Gröbner basis divides a power of $x_i$. A fixed-order basis need not literally contain a univariate polynomial in every variable. For zero-dimensional ideals, the FGLM algorithm can convert a basis between monomial orders.

**Example.** Count the solutions in $\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}$ of $x^{2} + y^{2} = 1$ and $x + y = 1$.

**Solution.** Over $\mathbb{F}_{2}$, $x^{2} = x$ and $y^{2} = y$, so the first equation becomes $x + y = 1$, identical to the second. The solutions are $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$. There are two solutions.

## Common errors

- **Assuming a Gröbner basis with respect to one order is a basis with respect to another.** Lex bases are often much larger than grevlex bases. For a zero-dimensional ideal, FGLM converts between orders; outside that hypothesis a different method is required.
- **Forgetting that $V(I)$ may be empty even when $I \neq (1)$.** Over non-algebraically-closed fields, the weak Nullstellensatz does not apply. Over $\mathbb{R}$, $x^{2} + 1 = 0$ has no solutions.
- **Using floating-point arithmetic for exact algebraic computations.** Gröbner basis computations require exact rational or finite-field arithmetic.

## Division terminates, but its conclusion needs a basis

Fix a monomial order and a finite ordered list G of nonzero polynomials over a field. At each division step, cancel the current leading term by a multiple of some g whose leading monomial divides it. If none does, move that leading term into the remainder. In either case the remaining polynomial's leading monomial strictly decreases. The well-order property therefore prohibits an infinite sequence of steps.

The result is an identity f=∑q_g g+r, where no monomial of r is divisible by any leading monomial of G. A zero remainder proves ideal membership because the identity is an explicit combination certificate. A nonzero remainder does not prove nonmembership for an arbitrary generating list, as the example (x+y,x−y) in the ring chapter showed. The Groebner property is exactly what makes the converse sound.

**Proposition 9.1 (Canonical normal form).** If G is a Groebner basis of I, every fully reduced remainder of f is the same. Moreover f∈I exactly when that remainder is zero.

*Proof.* Any two remainders r,s have r−s∈I because both differ from f by combinations of G. Every monomial of r−s, if nonzero, is still outside the leading-monomial ideal of G. But any nonzero element of I has leading monomial in that ideal by the Groebner definition, contradiction. Thus r=s. Applying the same argument to a remainder of an element of I gives zero; the reverse implication follows from the division identity. ∎

The unique remainder depends on the chosen monomial order and ideal. It is not an order-independent simplification of a polynomial expression. Exact rational or finite-field coefficients are part of the contract, because a rounded cancellation can change the leading term and the entire subsequent path.

## Buchberger’s criterion proved

Normalise generators to be monic, dividing by their nonzero leading coefficients. This is legal over a field and does not change their generated ideal.

**Theorem 9.2 (Buchberger’s criterion).** A finite generating list G is a Groebner basis exactly when every pair's S-polynomial reduces to zero by G.

*Proof of necessity.* Each S-polynomial is in I. The normal-form proposition for a Groebner basis makes its remainder zero.

*Proof of sufficiency.* For nonzero f∈I, express f as a finite sum of monomial multiples c_i m_i g_i, with c_i scalars, and choose a representation minimising the largest occurring product monomial M=m_i LM(g_i). The well-order gives a least possible M. If LM(f)=M, some LM(g_i) divides LM(f), as required.

Suppose instead that LM(f)<M. The coefficients of the top monomial cancel. Choose one top summand's generator g₀ as a reference. Since all generators are monic, the sum of top coefficients is zero, and the total top group can be written as sums of
$$c_i\left(\frac{M}{\operatorname{LM}(g_i)}g_i-
\frac{M}{\operatorname{LM}(g_0)}g_0\right).$$
Each parenthesis is M/L times an S-polynomial, with L the lcm of the two leading monomials. Its S-polynomial has leading monomial strictly below L because its leading terms cancel. A zero-remainder division expresses it as combinations of G whose product leading monomials are at most that smaller initial leading monomial. Multiplication by M/L makes all these product monomials strictly below M.

Replacing the entire cancelling top group by those combinations therefore gives a representation of f with a smaller maximum monomial, contradiction. Thus LM(f)=M after all, proving every nonzero ideal element has a leading monomial divisible by one from G. That is the Groebner property. ∎

The zero-remainder calculation carries the bounded combination needed in this proof. Merely asserting that each S-polynomial belongs to I would say nothing new, since that membership is automatic for every generating list.

## Why the algorithm terminates

**Lemma 9.3 (Dickson’s lemma).** In any infinite sequence of vectors in Nⁿ, some earlier vector is componentwise at most a later one.

*Proof.* Every infinite sequence of natural numbers has an infinite nondecreasing subsequence: either one value occurs infinitely often, or values are unbounded after every finite prefix and one chooses a strictly increasing subsequence. Apply this to the first coordinates, then use induction on the remaining n−1 coordinates of that subsequence. The resulting pair is nondecreasing in every coordinate. The case n=1 was just proved. ∎

Consequently monomial ideals satisfy the ascending chain condition. Otherwise choose a monomial newly entering each strictly larger ideal. Dickson gives an earlier chosen monomial dividing a later one, forcing that later monomial into the earlier ideal and contradicting its choice.

**Theorem 9.4 (Buchberger termination and correctness).** Process every pending pair, adding each nonzero reduced S-remainder as a monic new generator and adding its new pairs. This process terminates and returns a Groebner basis generating the original ideal.

*Proof.* A new remainder belongs to the existing ideal by its division identity and S-polynomial expression, so adding it preserves the ideal. Its leading monomial is not divisible by any existing leading monomial, hence strictly enlarges the generated monomial ideal. The ascending chain condition permits only finitely many additions. After the last addition there are only finitely many pending pairs, so processing them finishes. Every pair then reduces to zero in the final list, and Buchberger’s criterion proves correctness. ∎

An inert reference outline is:

```text
G = monic nonzero input generators
pending = all unordered distinct pairs from G
while pending is nonempty:
    remove a pair (f,g)
    r = remainder(S_polynomial(f,g), G)
    if r != 0:
        r = r / leading_coefficient(r)
        add pairs (h,r) for every h currently in G to pending
        append r to G
return G
```

Termination is a theorem, not an assertion of a small run time. Degrees and coefficient sizes can grow substantially. The outline can store combination vectors along every operation to certify that every new polynomial belongs to the original ideal. Independent verification of a particular claimed basis then has two parts: equality of generated ideals and zero remainders for every final pair.

## Elimination from leading monomials

**Theorem 9.5 (Elimination property).** In lex order with x₁>⋯>x_n, the elements of G involving only x_(i),…,x_n form a Groebner basis for I intersected with that polynomial subring.

*Proof.* Take nonzero f in the intersection. Some LM(g) divides LM(f), so that leading monomial uses only the retained variables. Under lex order a polynomial whose leading monomial uses no eliminated variable cannot have a lower term containing one: any such term would outrank every retained-only monomial at the first eliminated coordinate where it appears. Hence g itself lies in the retained subring. This proves the leading-ideal condition there. Division of f by those retained generators stays in the subring and has zero remainder, also proving they generate the intersection. ∎

This is an algebraic statement over any field. The earlier closure and extension theorems over C address a different question: which retained-coordinate solutions actually extend to full points. Elimination can lose a nonvanishing condition, and the proved leading-monomial argument does not supply a missing geometric witness.

**Worked example 9.6 (A new S-polynomial supplies the missing relation).** Over Q with lex x>y take I=(x²−y,xy−1). Their S-polynomial is y(x²−y)−x(xy−1)=x−y². Adjoin it. Reducing xy−1 with x=y² gives y³−1. The pair x−y²,y³−1 generates the original ideal: it gives xy−1 by multiplying the first generator by y and adding y³−1, while x²−y reduces to y⁴−y=y(y³−1).

Its leading monomials are x and y³. The single S-polynomial reduces to zero by replacing x with y², so it is a Groebner basis. The quotient has basis 1,y,y². Over C there are three choices for y with y³=1 and each gives x=y². In characteristic three the same displayed equations have y³−1=(y−1)³, hence only one geometric point, though the quotient still has dimension three. Algebraic multiplicity and distinct point count are not interchangeable.

## Finite-field points need the field equations

**Proposition 9.7.** The quotient by x_j^q−x_j for every variable is the ring of all functions F_qⁿ→F_q. In particular every ideal containing these field equations is radical, and its quotient dimension counts the retained finite-field points.

*Proof.* Reduction in each variable gives representatives of degree below q in that variable, so the quotient has vector-space dimension qⁿ. Evaluation maps it to the product of qⁿ copies of F_q, one per point. It is injective: a polynomial of degree below q in each variable vanishing on the full grid is zero, by induction in the variables and the univariate root bound. Equal dimensions make evaluation an isomorphism. Every ideal in a finite product of fields sets some components to zero and leaves the others arbitrary, so its quotient is a product of fields and is reduced. Its dimension is the number of remaining components, exactly its zero-set point count. ∎

**Worked example 9.8 (Two points versus an infinite-dimensional coordinate ring).** Over F₂ the equations x²+y²=1 and x+y=1 reduce as functions to one equation, with points (0,1),(1,0). But the ideal generated by those polynomials is only (x+y+1), whose quotient is F₂[y] and has infinite vector-space dimension. Adding the field equation y²+y gives lex basis {x+y+1,y²+y}; the x-field equation follows from these. The quotient basis is 1,y and its dimension two now correctly counts the two grid points. The additional field equations justify replacing pointwise identities by algebraic quotient relations.

### Exercise 1

Compute a Gröbner basis for $I = (x^{2} + y, xy + y)$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$ with lex order $x > y$.

<details>

The first S-remainder is y²+y; verify all final pairs, including the first and third.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$f_{1} = x^{2} + y$, $f_{2} = xy + y$. $S(f_{1}, f_{2}) = y(x^{2} + y) - x(xy + y) = y^{2} - xy = y(y - x)$. Reduce modulo $f_{2}$: $y(y - x) + (xy + y) = y^{2} + y$. So $f_{3} = y^{2} + y$. Now $S(f_{2}, f_{3}) = y(xy + y) - x(y^{2} + y) = y^{2} - xy = y(y - x)$, which reduces to $0$ using $f_{2}$ and $f_{3}$. The basis $\{x^{2} + y, xy + y, y^{2} + y\}$ is a Gröbner basis.



For completeness the remaining pair gives S(f₁,f₃)=y²f₁−x²f₃=y³−x²y. Reducing x² to −y yields y³+y²=yf₃, which reduces to zero. Thus all three final pairs have been addressed, rather than only the two displayed first.

### Exercise 2

Solve the system $x^{2} + y^{2} = 2$, $xy = 1$ over $\mathbb{C}$ using elimination.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use xy=1 to eliminate y, keeping the nonzero-x condition.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

From $xy = 1$, $y = 1/x$. Substitute: $x^{2} + 1/x^{2} = 2$, so $x^{4} - 2x^{2} + 1 = 0$, giving $(x^{2} - 1)^{2} = 0$. Thus $x = \pm 1$, and $y = \pm 1$ with the same sign. The solutions are $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$, each with multiplicity $2$ in the algebraic sense.

</details>

### Exercise 5 — A nonmembership certificate
<a id="finite-groebner-ex-5"></a>

Why is a nonzero fully reduced remainder a nonmembership certificate only after the Groebner property is established?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The leading ideal of the generators must contain the leading monomial of every ideal element.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

For a Groebner basis, a nonzero remainder cannot belong to the ideal because its leading monomial is outside the generated leading ideal. Without that property, an ideal element can have such a nonzero remainder, as x divided by x+y,x−y shows. The division identity alone proves only that f and its remainder are congruent modulo the ideal.

</details>

### Exercise 6 — A strict leading-ideal increase
<a id="finite-groebner-ex-6"></a>

Why does adding a nonzero reduced S-remainder strictly enlarge the leading-monomial ideal?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

No remainder monomial is divisible by any current leading monomial.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

In particular its leading monomial is not in the current monomial ideal, whose members are multiples of existing leading monomials. Adding it therefore includes a genuinely new monomial and gives strict containment. Dickson’s lemma prohibits infinitely many such strict increases, proving finite additions in Buchberger’s algorithm.

</details>

### Exercise 8 — A finite-field correction
<a id="finite-groebner-ex-8"></a>

Why does adding y²+y to the ideal (x+y+1) over F₂ change its quotient dimension while preserving its F₂-point set?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The added equation holds at every F₂ value of y but is not the zero polynomial.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Every finite-field point already has y²+y=0, so the F₂-point set stays {(0,1),(1,0)}. The original quotient is F₂[y], with all powers independent, whereas the added relation reduces every power to a combination of one and y. The new quotient therefore has dimension two. Pointwise vanishing and polynomial ideal membership are different statements until field equations are included.

</details>

## Summary

Groebner bases make multivariate normal forms canonical for a fixed order. Buchberger’s criterion connects S-pair cancellation to the leading ideal, and Dickson’s lemma proves algorithmic termination. Elimination supplies polynomial constraints, while extension and point counting need their separate field and reducedness hypotheses. Finite-field equations bridge grid functions and coordinate rings. The following short coding bridge uses exact linear algebra before the lattice and kernel-project chapters.
