## Preparation

**0.1.** The probability is a parameter of a model for visits. The observed proportion is a statistic calculated from one realised sample. Thirty out of one hundred gives 0.30, but another hundred visits can have a different proportion even with the same generating probability. The observed proportion alone does not establish a fixed known probability. **Marking:** Distinguish fixed model probability, observed statistic and repeated-sample variation; accept any correct repeated-sample example.

**0.2.** $P(\text{old},\text{fault})=0.4(0.1)=0.04$. Total fault probability is $0.04+0.6(0.02)=0.052$. Therefore $P(\text{old}\mid\text{fault})=0.04/0.052=10/13\approx0.7692$. Faulty items are disproportionately from old machines because their conditional fault probability is higher. **Marking:** Require joint masses, total-fault denominator and reverse conditional. A frequency table is equivalent; using 0.4 as the denominator answers the wrong conditional.

**0.3.** Use `rng.binomial(20, 0.3, size=1000)` and calculate `.mean()` and `.var(ddof=1)`. Theory gives mean 6 completions and variance 4.2 completions squared. The sample summaries need not be exactly equal to theory. The sample mean's approximate standard error is $\sqrt{4.2/1000}\approx0.0648$ completions. Repeat with several seeds rather than interpreting a tiny difference as a bug. Running averages in Lab 0 fluctuate; convergence is not monotonic. **Marking:** Require executable binomial draws, correct moments/units and sampling error reasoning. Accept seeded equivalent simulation; do not demand exact agreement or call simulation spread posterior uncertainty.

## 1 · Bayesian updating

**1.1.** $P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{defect})$ and $P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{sound})$ refer to different conditioning populations. They need not sum to one. Within either population, alarm plus no alarm must sum to one. After an alarm, posterior probabilities over the exhaustive hypotheses defect and sound also sum to one. **Marking:** Require the correct conditioning populations and a pair that genuinely sums to one. Merely saying probabilities are different is insufficient.

**1.2.** After an alarm, weights are $0.2(0.9)=0.18$ and $0.8(0.05)=0.04$, giving $0.18/0.22\approx0.8182$. After no alarm, weights are $0.2(0.1)=0.02$ and $0.8(0.95)=0.76$, giving $0.02/0.78\approx0.02564$. The complement of sensitivity is used only in the defective group; the sound group's negative probability is specificity, 0.95. **Marking:** Credit both weighted tables, complements and normalisation. Carry a local arithmetic slip through for method credit; swapping specificity and sensitivity is conceptual.

**1.3.** At the original 2% base rate and conditional independence, two positive alarms give $0.02(0.9)^2/[0.02(0.9)^2+0.98(0.05)^2]\approx0.86863$. A duplicate report adds no new sensor observation; conditional on the first report, it is certain under either hypothesis. Its likelihood ratio is one, leaving the posterior at approximately 0.26866. Two physically different sensors still need a justified conditional-independence assumption. **Marking:** Require the original 2% base rate, an explicit conditional-independence assumption and duplicate likelihood ratio one. Do not substitute the 20% base rate from 1.2.

## 2 · Beta-binomial inference

**2.1.** The credible interval describes posterior uncertainty about a probability $\theta$. A future individual outcome is either zero or one, so it is not distributed across that parameter interval. A future batch has a discrete predictive distribution that averages binomial probabilities over parameter uncertainty. **Marking:** Require parameter versus outcome support and the two sources of predictive variation. A credible interval cannot be relabelled as an individual predictive interval.

**2.2.** The prior kernel is $\theta^2(1-\theta)^6$ and likelihood kernel is $\theta^{12}(1-\theta)^8$. Their product is $\theta^{14}(1-\theta)^{14}$, hence Beta(15,15). Its mean is $15/30=0.5$. Equivalently, $(10/30)(0.3)+(20/30)(0.6)=0.5$. Neither the prior mean nor the observed proportion alone is the updated mean. **Marking:** Credit exponents, both posterior shapes, mean and the weighted-average interpretation. An equivalent normalised beta derivation earns full credit.

**2.3.** For grid midpoints `g=(np.arange(100)+0.5)/100`, normalise `beta.pdf(g,16,8)` and sum weights where `g>0.7`. Compare with `beta.sf(0.7,16,8)`; the exact value is 0.3818716495 (about 0.382), while the 100-point midpoint estimate is 0.3818393174. The absolute discrepancy is about 0.00003233. Increase grid size to 1,000 and 10,000. A finer grid reduces discretisation error, although event-boundary alignment can make convergence nonmonotone for general thresholds. The lab uses log weights to remain stable when densities are very small. Sequential 6/10 and 8/10 updates return the same Beta(16,8) as the combined data. Its central 90% interval is approximately [0.5036, 0.8137]. Equal spacing lets the common cell width cancel during normalisation; on an unequal grid multiply density by cell width before normalising. **Marking:** Require the stated 100-point midpoint grid, exact survival-function reference and a resolution comparison. Distinguish deterministic grid error from posterior spread; boundary-aware equivalent quadrature is acceptable.

## 3 · Prediction and decisions

**3.1.** One shared posterior $\theta$ creates dependence between future visits after integrating over uncertainty: for two conditionally independent Bernoulli visits, $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j\mid y)=\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid y)$. Independent per-visit $\theta_i$ draws remove that common uncertain quantity, giving independent Bernoulli outcomes with the posterior mean probability under the proposed construction. That can be appropriate for a model of independent heterogeneous probabilities drawn from a known distribution, but it is a different generative assumption and does not represent uncertainty about one shared rate. **Marking:** Require the shared-parameter dependence argument and a coherent alternative heterogeneity model. A fresh independent draw per visit does not propagate uncertainty about one common rate.

**3.2.** Mean $=5(3/5)=3$. Variance $=5(3)(2)(5+5)/(5^2\cdot6)=2$. Plug-in variance $=5(0.6)(0.4)=1.2$. The extra variation concerns uncertainty about the probability shared by the five future trials. In Lab 3, posterior predictive variance is about 7.82 versus 4.44 for the plug-in model. **Marking:** Credit predictive mean, predictive variance, plug-in comparison and interpretation of the extra 0.8 variance. A total-variance derivation is equivalent.

**3.3.** Expected loss of no intervention is $15(0.18)=2.7$ and intervention loss is 2, so intervene. Indifference occurs at cost 2.7. Under the lab's original loss 10 and Beta(16,8), $P(\theta<0.6)\approx0.23727$ and leaving has loss about 2.373, again favouring a cost-2 intervention. Altering costs changes the decision boundary without altering evidence. **Marking:** Require both losses, action and indifference cost. Accept any units-consistent expected-utility equivalent; a probability threshold without costs is insufficient.

## 4 · Continuous models

**4.1.** A row is a recording unit; exposure is the time or opportunity during which events could occur. Treating a ten-hour centre like a one-hour centre compares expected counts under different opportunities as if they were rates. Include exposure in the Poisson mean or log offset and record its unit. **Marking:** Require actual time/opportunity units and the Poisson mean relationship. More rows do not imply more exposure.

**4.2.** Shape becomes $3+5=8$ and rate becomes $2+4=6$. Mean rate is $8/6=4/3$, variance is $8/36=2/9$, and the expected count in exposure 3 is $3(4/3)=4$. Rate variance has squared rate units; a future count has additional Poisson variation. In the lab's hours-to-minutes change, $\lambda_{\rm min}=\lambda_{\rm hour}/60$, so a Gamma($a,b$) hourly rate induces Gamma($a,60b$) per minute; exposures multiply by 60. **Marking:** Credit the shape-rate update and all moments with units. Predictive mean is requested here; do not confuse rate variance with count variance.

**4.3.** Posterior precision is $1+2/4=1.5$, so variance is $2/3$. The posterior mean is $(2/3)(0+(2+4)/4)=1$. Completing the quadratic square gives $p(\mu\mid y)\propto\exp[-(3/4)(\mu-1)^2]$, so the posterior is Normal($1,2/3$), with variance as the second argument. Predictive variance is $4+2/3=14/3$, obtained as $E[\operatorname{Var}(Y_*\mid\mu,y)\mid y]+\operatorname{Var}[E(Y_*\mid\mu,y)\mid y]$. Mean is in measurement units and both variances are in squared measurement units. The larger predictive variance includes observation noise. **Marking:** Require precision weighting, posterior variance and added observation noise. Equivalent square completion earns full credit; variance/SD substitution is a model error.

## 5 · Monte Carlo

**5.1.** Draws approximate a posterior already fixed by the data and model. Increasing them improves numerical precision, reducing MCSE by roughly the square root of the increase in effective draws. It does not supply new service observations or materially change the target posterior spread. **Marking:** Require the fixed posterior target and numerical/scientific uncertainty distinction. Qualify square-root improvement by effective, not merely stored, draws.

**5.2.** MCSE is approximately $3/\sqrt{900}=0.1$. To reach 0.05 requires $(3/0.05)^2=3600$ effective draws. This approximation concerns the mean and assumes finite variance and a reliable ESS estimate. Use a suitable MCSE for a quantile or tail probability instead of reusing this number blindly. **Marking:** Credit both MCSE calculations and the relevant-mean ESS assumption. A different estimand requires a corresponding error assessment.

**5.3.** Metropolis pseudocode is `propose x_new ~ q(.|x); log_ratio = log_p(x_new)-log_p(x)` for a symmetric proposal; `if log(U) < min(0,log_ratio): x=x_new`; then `append(x)` regardless of acceptance. Out-of-support candidates have log density minus infinity and are rejected; an asymmetric proposal also needs the reverse/forward proposal ratio. Accepted-only output samples the jumps and weights states in proportion to their probability of being left; its stationary density is proportional to $p(x)a(x)$, where $a(x)$ is departure probability, rather than generally p(x). A reproducible numerical comparison for the lab's Beta(16,8) target is provided by `scripts/verify_core_assessments.py`: four starts [0.2,0.4,0.7,0.9], seeds 505–508, Gaussian proposal SD 1.0, 60,000 transitions per chain and 10,000 discarded. It records full-chain and jump-only mean, variance and $P(\theta>0.7)$ against exact references $2/3$, $2/225\approx0.00888889$ and 0.38187165 in `.cache/core_sampling_checks.json`. In that executed comparison, the full sample had mean 0.666868, variance 0.008763 and tail probability 0.385180; jump-only storage gave 0.663494, 0.011269 and 0.395116, respectively. Full-mean MCSE was 0.000761. The variance difference demonstrates a changed distribution even when means are relatively close. The source retains states on rejection and separately records accepted moves, so the comparison changes storage only, not the transition rule. Require the full mean within five estimated MCSEs of the exact mean and inspect density/variance and tail differences; a matching mean alone cannot validate the jump distribution. Smaller proposals can have high acceptance yet poor movement. Residence-time weighting can recover a full-chain summary from compressed states only if the correct dwell counts are retained. **Marking:** Require rejection-preserving pseudocode and actual reproducible full-versus-jump evidence for the beta target, including an exact reference. Explanation without the requested numerical demonstration earns only partial credit.

## 6 · Modern sampling

**6.1.** Good diagnostics can support faithful computation for a Gaussian model that allows impossible values. Investigate prior and posterior predictive mass below zero, units, and whether a positive-support likelihood is appropriate. The model can be scientifically poor without a sampling bug. **Marking:** Credit separation of computation and model support, plus a targeted predictive or observation-protocol check. Threshold recitation alone is insufficient.

**6.2.** First verify data, units, constraints and prior predictions on a simpler simulated case. Second diagnose geometry and identification, standardising predictors or using an appropriate non-centred hierarchy and regularising priors. Third rerun multiple chains with suitable warmup and tuning, inspect divergence locations, traces, R-hat, ESS and relevant MCSE. Increasing target acceptance can help numerical integration but should follow investigation. Do not interpret the reported tail probability as reliable while these failures persist. **Marking:** Require three ordered, justified actions addressing model coding, geometry and fresh verification. Longer chains alone do not resolve these reported failures.

**6.3.** Beta(10,10) with 14/20 gives Beta(24,16), mean 0.6 and central 90% interval [0.4711427327, 0.7230985087]. The original Beta(2,2) analysis gives Beta(16,8), mean 2/3 and interval [0.5035649163, 0.8136561968]. The stronger prior shifts the posterior towards 1/2 and reduces its width in this example. Run the changed model with four chains, 1,500 warmup and 2,000 retained draws each, sequential cores, target acceptance 0.9 and seed 6063; `scripts/verify_core_assessments.py` supplies runnable model code and writes this refit's own mean, interval, mean/quantile MCSE, R-hat, bulk/tail ESS and divergences to `.cache/core_sampling_checks.json`. The executed refit gave mean 0.599835, mean MCSE 0.001521, sampled 90% interval [0.468784, 0.724726], R-hat 1.002392, bulk ESS 2572.6, tail ESS 3501.9 and 0 divergences. The mean error is 0.11 MCSEs. Compare the sampled mean with 0.6 in MCSE units, using five MCSEs as a generous debugging screen, and each quantile against its own MCSE. Diagnose any discrepancy instead of widening tolerance until it passes. Good agreement checks computation for the revised model; the original fit's diagnostics cannot validate it. **Marking:** Require the correct revised posterior, both intervals and fresh refit diagnostics with mean MCSE. Analytic-only work earns calculation credit, not the requested fit credit; accept other successful seeds/settings with their own evidence.

## 7 · Regression

**7.1.** The mean interval reflects uncertainty about the regression parameters. A future visit also has residual variation around its conditional mean. Integrating both layers ordinarily produces a wider predictive interval. Even perfectly known coefficients do not eliminate visit-to-visit noise. **Marking:** Require the distinct random quantities and observation-noise layer. More parameter draws alone cannot convert a mean interval into an outcome interval.

**7.2.** The mean is $10+1.5(2)=13$. Conditional on that single draw, the central 90% normal interval is $13\pm1.64485(2)$, approximately [9.71,16.29]. It omits parameter uncertainty because coefficients and scale were fixed. A full posterior predictive interval simulates over joint parameter draws and is not necessarily obtained by this same fixed-width formula. **Marking:** Credit mean, quantile multiplier, conditional interval and its missing parameter uncertainty. Reasonable rounding is acceptable.

**7.3.** Add centre intercepts, ideally with a justified hierarchical distribution, and check the distribution of workload-adjusted centre means in replicated data. Consider group covariates if centres are not plausibly exchangeable. More draws only refine the original common-intercept posterior; they cannot introduce group variation absent from its generative assumptions. Also check the prior's implied waits and extrapolation limits. **Marking:** Require a plausible group model, observable discrepancy and limits of more computation. Alternative group covariates earn full credit when justified.

## 8 · Generalised linear models

**8.1.** Odds convert nonlinearly to probability. An odds multiplier of two moves 0.2 to 1/3 (about 13.3 percentage points) and 0.5 to 2/3 (about 16.7 points). Absolute impacts depend on baseline probabilities and the target population. A percentage-point difference is an absolute change; a percentage increase is relative to a baseline. **Marking:** Require a nonlinear conversion argument, such as $p_1=2p_0/(1+p_0)$, and correct percentage-point language. The 0.2 and 0.5 baselines above are illustrative, not required givens. Accept other defensible baselines or the correct formula with an explanation of baseline dependence; a local arithmetic slip retains method credit.

**8.2.** Rate $=\exp(\log3+2\log1.5)=3(1.5)^2=6.75$ events per exposure unit. Expected count $=4(6.75)=27$. The log expected count includes the offset $\log4$. Estimating a free exposure coefficient would describe a different model. **Marking:** Credit rate, exposure-scaled mean and fixed offset coefficient one. Distinguish events per exposure from number of events.

**8.3.** Let D be difficulty, S support, and C completion. A plausible graph has D→S, D→C, and S→C. The S–C association can mix support effects and differences in difficulty. A causal interpretation needs an assignment/identification argument, sufficiently measured pre-treatment confounders where relevant, overlap, and clear interventions. Posterior precision does not establish these conditions. A randomised offer of support would answer a better-defined intervention question, subject to adherence and outcome measurement issues. **Marking:** Require a confounding path and an identification/design argument. Valid alternative causal graphs are acceptable if assumptions and target intervention are explicit.

## 9 · Hierarchies

**9.1.** More visits give precise information about three particular intercepts. The distribution of intercepts across possible centres is still learned from only three group realisations and can be sensitive to its hyperprior. Within-group sample size and number of groups provide different kinds of information. **Marking:** Require distinction between group-level replication and within-group precision, plus hyperprior sensitivity. Row count alone is not evidence for population spread.

**9.2.** For $n=1$, $w=1/(4+1)=0.2$, so posterior mean is $0.2(14)+0.8(10)=10.8$. For $n=16$, $w=16/(4+16)=0.8$, giving 13.2. Both shrink towards 10, but the larger group retains more of its observed departure. The full lab integrates uncertainty in population parameters rather than fixing them. **Marking:** Credit both weights and means and explain movement towards the population mean. A precision-weighted equivalent earns full credit.

**9.3.** For each joint draw, take $\mu_\alpha,\tau,\beta,\sigma$; draw $\alpha_*\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_\alpha,\tau^2)$; compute $\mu_*=\alpha_*+\beta$; draw the visit from Normal($\mu_*,\sigma^2$). Replacing $\alpha_*$ by a fixed population mean loses between-centre variation and uncertainty in population parameters. Adding only observation noise does not repair the missing group layer. **Marking:** Require joint hyperparameter draws, a new group effect and individual noise; reuse the group effect for multiple visits to the same future centre. Do not silently use an existing fitted effect.

## 10 · Model checking

**10.1.** Yes. A common-intercept regression can be sampled accurately while failing to reproduce observed centre differences. R-hat concerns agreement of chains for the model's posterior; a predictive discrepancy concerns the model's relationship to observations. **Marking:** Require a specific generative failure alongside a computationally adequate fit. Do not claim passing R-hat proves exploration of every posterior feature.

**10.2.** Sum is 4. Mean difference is 1; squared deviations are 0, 1, 4, 1, so sample variance is $6/3=2$. The approximate paired SE is $\sqrt{4(2)}=\sqrt8\approx2.83$. The estimated improvement is not large relative to this uncertainty, and four observations are too few to trust a normal approximation strongly. This is not a posterior probability that one model is true. **Marking:** Require the paired sum and sample variance with n−1 denominator, SE and cautious interpretation. Independent SE addition is not the paired calculation.

**10.3.** Hold out an entire hospital at a time, keeping all visits from its patients out of training. For a new-hospital target, draw new group effects rather than using fitted hospital effects. Repeated-patient records crossing training and test sets would leak information. Row-level LOO mostly measures a new visit among represented hospitals, often with information about the same patient still available. Preprocessing and model selection must respect the same grouping. If the target is also future time, incorporate that ordering. **Marking:** Require a target-matched group split, repeated-patient leakage protection, training-only preprocessing and new-group prediction. Accept temporal restrictions when justified by deployment.

## 11 · Robustness

**11.1.** SBC simulates from the assumed model and checks whether inference recovers its conditional distributions coherently. Actual observations can come from a different process. Correct inference for a normal likelihood does not give that likelihood heavy tails. Conversely, a single reasonable predictive plot cannot validate every posterior direction. **Marking:** Require the distinct simulated versus real-data checking targets. Approximate SBC uniformity is neither proof of correctness nor real-world adequacy.

**11.2.** Variance is $2^2(4/(4-2))=8$, SD $\sqrt8\approx2.828$. Under the symmetric t model with these degrees of freedom the location equals the model's mean. In contaminated observed data, the fitted robust location need not equal the empirical sample mean and may represent a different practical summary of the main process. State the intended estimand rather than treating robustness as automatic superiority. **Marking:** Credit variance/SD, the existence of moments at nu=4 and the location/empirical-mean distinction. Scale 2 is not SD 2.

**11.3.** If long unobserved waits cause nonresponse after conditioning on recorded variables, the observed sample does not identify the population wait distribution without additional assumptions or information. Introduce a sensitivity parameter describing how missing waits differ from comparable observed waits, vary it over substantively plausible values, and propagate the resulting uncertainty into the decision. Follow-up samples or external records could inform that parameter. Do not claim that a convenient imputation model has empirically established MAR. **Marking:** Require explicit nonidentification, a scientifically interpretable sensitivity parameter and propagation to conclusions. A defensible alternative selection model or follow-up design is valid.

## 12 · Decisions

**12.1.** Improvement can be too small to pay for implementation, or its downside can be severe under the relevant utility. Specify the future population size, effect scale, gain per improvement, rollout and opportunity costs, decision horizon, and risk preferences. A probability of positive effect omits the magnitude of benefits and losses. **Marking:** Require magnitudes and utilities as well as sign probability, plus population/horizon. Alternative risk preferences are valid if stated.

**12.2.** Utility is 2,000 when $\Delta=0.06$ and −2,000 when $\Delta=-0.02$. Expected net utility is $0.7(2000)+0.3(-2000)=800$, so choose B. Expected opportunity loss from B is $0.3(2000)=600$; from A it is $0.7(2000)=1400$. Perfect information achieves expected utility 1,400, so EVPI is $1400-800=600$. This is an upper bound on information value for this decision, not a guaranteed gain from collecting a small sample. **Marking:** Credit state utilities, current action, both opportunity losses and EVPI with units. EVPI equals current optimal expected opportunity loss here; do not treat it as a trial-specific return.

**12.3.** One acceptable memo: “For 10,000 future visits equally distributed across the eight simulated centres, choose design B under the stated utility. The estimated absolute improvement is 8.8 percentage points, with a 90% posterior interval of about 4.1–13.5 points. At 5 utility units per extra completion and a 1,000-unit rollout cost, expected net benefit is about 3,392 units in the saved simulation (analytic expectation about 3,395). This concerns conditional expected incremental value rather than realised outcomes, and alternative priors retain the same action with somewhat smaller estimated gains. This conclusion depends on stable centre-specific probabilities and applies to the synthetic setting; it does not establish an effect for real services or unseen centres.” **Marking:** Require five sentences covering population, horizon, action, effect scale, uncertainty and a limitation; assess consistency with the learner’s attached analysis. Utility sensitivity is a useful optional addition, not an unrequested marking requirement. Use the saved simulation or clearly labelled analytic expectation; do not label expected-value uncertainty as realised risk.

## Feedback on the open lab investigations

Priors should be compared through predictions and decision-relevant summaries, not through whether they are labelled weak. Changing a random seed tests numerical stability; changing the likelihood tests a modelling assumption. For hierarchy investigations, preserve group labels and inspect both group and population parameters. For the capstone, switching from equal-centre to training-size weights changes the target population. Raising rollout cost to 5,000 changes break-even improvement to 0.10 and reverses the reference expected-utility action because the estimated improvement is below 0.10. These are legitimate changes of question; they should not be hidden as small implementation adjustments.
