# Expose the parallel hypothesis in triangle angle sum

*Difficulty 3 of 5 · [Geometry from proof to curvature audit studio](index.md) · exercise 1 of 12*

Prove that a Euclidean triangle has angle sum 180 degrees by drawing a parallel through one vertex. Then give a spherical triangle with three right angles and identify exactly why it does not contradict the proof.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use alternate interior angles at the chosen vertex

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Take the North Pole and two equatorial points separated by 90 degrees

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Great circles do not satisfy the Euclidean parallel postulate

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>

For Euclidean triangle ABC, draw through C the unique line parallel to AB. The angles at A and B reappear at C as alternate interior angles. Together with angle C they form a straight angle, so $A+B+C=180$ degrees. On the unit sphere, take the North Pole N and equatorial points P,Q separated by 90 degrees of longitude. Meridians NP,NQ meet the equator at right angles and meet each other at a right angle at N, so the sum is 270 degrees. There is no contradiction: the Euclidean proof used a parallel through C and Euclidean alternate-angle behavior, while distinct spherical geodesic great circles always meet.

**Answer.** The Euclidean sum follows from a parallel through a vertex; the pole–equator spherical triangle has sum 270 degrees because great-circle geometry lacks that parallel structure.

</details>
