## Current course route

Use the [course page](#/courses/atlas-mathematics-education-research-methods) for its present preparation and available study steps. This Outline keeps its original explanations and exercises while a complete primary-book syllabus is being assigned.

Education research connects human activity, mathematical content, institutions, and evidence. Its hardest questions are not repaired by choosing a fashionable method. They are improved by making the inferential chain explicit:

```text
problem -> question -> construct -> design -> data -> analysis -> claim
```

Every arrow can fail. This course builds a research plan by auditing those arrows. It assumes basic statistical inference and familiarity with learning progressions, but it treats qualitative, quantitative, mixed, historical, and design-oriented work as genuine forms of inquiry with different warrant conditions.

## Entry diagnostic

For each proposed study, name the claim it can support and one claim it cannot:

1. Ten purposefully selected lessons are transcribed to study how diagrams
   enter proportional-reasoning arguments.
2. Sixteen classrooms are randomly assigned to two curricula.
3. A voluntary national teacher survey finds an association between reported
   confidence and years of experience.
4. A design team revises one task over four classroom cycles.
5. A review finds two reports from the same underlying trial.

**Diagnostic map.** The first can support contextualized process claims but not
population prevalence. The second can estimate an assigned-condition contrast
for participating clusters, subject to attrition and implementation, but does
not automatically generalize nationally. The third can estimate a
selection-qualified association, not a causal effect of experience. The fourth
can document local improvement and a design conjecture, not universal efficacy.
The fifth requires dependent estimates to be linked rather than counted as
independent studies.

If you cannot state the unit, target, and forbidden stronger claim for at least
four cases, work Sections 1–5 before drafting a study. If you can, begin your
own protocol in parallel and revise one section after each course section.

## 1. Start with a bounded research question

Compare three questions:

1. “Does discussion help mathematics learning?”
2. “How do grade-7 groups use diagrams while revising proportional-reasoning explanations in one six-week unit?”
3. “In the participating grade-7 classes, what is the average difference on a prespecified proportional-reasoning assessment between a structured-discussion unit and the usual unit, under the implemented assignment procedure?”

The first names an interest but leaves population, intervention, comparison, outcome, time, and mechanism open. The second is a process question suited to close analysis of activity and discourse. The third is an estimand-oriented comparison. Neither bounded question is inherently superior; each permits a different claim.

Write the intended claim before selecting data. If the hoped-for conclusion is causal, identify the assignment or identification strategy that could separate the intervention from alternative explanations. If the hoped-for conclusion concerns meaning or process, identify which episodes and contrasts could make the interpretation answerable.

## 2. Define constructs through mathematics and observation

Terms such as “understanding,” “engagement,” “reasoning,” “identity,” and “teacher knowledge” are not self-measuring. A construct definition should include:

- its mathematical content and boundaries;
- observable performances that count as evidence;
- nearby constructs that should not be conflated;
- tasks or contexts in which it is expected to appear;
- plausible ways the measurement can fail.

**Example.** “Understands equivalence” is too broad. A narrower construct might be: *coordinates symbolic and area representations to justify why multiplying numerator and denominator by the same nonzero whole number preserves a fraction's value*. Evidence could include building a partition correspondence, explaining the multiplicative relation, and transferring to an unfamiliar denominator. Fast simplification alone would not establish the representational coordination.

Operationalization does not make a construct identical to its measure. A rubric score is evidence under a measurement argument, not the learner itself.

## 3. Choose a design that matches the inference

### Descriptive and qualitative designs

Observation, interview, artifact analysis, discourse analysis, ethnography, and case study can characterize strategies, norms, interactions, and meanings in context. Sampling is often purposeful because cases are selected for their information, contrast, or theoretical relevance.

A strong qualitative design records how sites and episodes were selected, the researcher's relationship to the setting, how data were produced, how codes or interpretations changed, discrepant cases, and how claims are grounded in records. “We found themes” is not an analysis plan.

The conclusion may be transferable through a well-described mechanism or context; it is not automatically statistically representative of a population.

### Survey and correlational designs

Surveys can estimate distributions or associations when the target population, sampling frame, response process, weighting, and measurement properties are defensible. A large convenience sample can estimate its own responses precisely while representing a wider population poorly.

Correlation can support prediction or expose a relationship worth explaining. It does not by itself identify the effect of changing one variable because confounding, selection, measurement error, and reverse direction remain possible.

### Experiments

Random assignment creates a basis for estimating a causal contrast between assigned conditions, subject to implementation, attrition, interference, noncompliance, and measurement assumptions. The unit of assignment matters. If schools or classrooms are randomized, learners within a cluster are not independent assignment units; analysis and power planning must respect clustering.

A result for one intervention version, comparison condition, population, outcome, and implementation period does not establish a universal method effect.

### Quasi-experiments

Regression discontinuity, interrupted time series, matched comparisons, instrumental variables, and difference-in-differences can support causal inference when their design-specific assumptions are plausible and probed. Naming a method is not satisfying its assumptions. For example, difference-in-differences requires a defensible counterfactual trend argument; matching addresses observed variables, not every unobserved difference.

### Design-based and improvement-oriented inquiry

Iterative designs can develop a learning environment and a theory of how it operates. Each cycle should record the conjecture, intended mechanism, implementation, evidence, failure, and revision. Local improvement is valuable, but successive redesign makes a simple treatment-effect story difficult. The warranted product may be a refined design principle with conditions, not an average causal effect.

### Mixed methods

Mixed methods is not “some interviews plus some statistics.” State what each evidence stream can establish and where they integrate. Interviews might explain how an intervention was used; outcome comparisons might estimate a contrast; artifact analysis might test a proposed mechanism. Agreement can strengthen an account, while disagreement should be analyzed rather than averaged away.

### Systematic review, meta-analysis, and research synthesis

Research synthesis is itself a designed study. Begin with a question whose
population, setting, intervention or phenomenon, comparison, outcome, and
eligible designs are explicit. Register or timestamp the protocol where
appropriate, then record databases and other sources, complete search strings,
date limits, language and publication restrictions, deduplication, screening
decisions, exclusion reasons, and a flow from retrieved records to included
reports. One study may generate several papers, and one paper may report several
dependent estimates; neither papers nor estimates are automatically independent
study units.

Build a coding and risk-of-bias argument before combining results. At minimum,
record participants and contexts, intervention versions, comparison conditions,
assignment or identification designs, implementation, outcome constructs and
timing, missingness, estimate definitions, uncertainty, and selective-reporting
risks. Two scores both called “achievement” may measure incompatible
mathematics. Converting them to standardized units does not make their constructs
or comparisons identical.

A **meta-analysis** combines sufficiently comparable estimates under an
explicit statistical model. A common-effect model targets one shared underlying
effect under its assumptions. A random-effects model represents a distribution
of study effects, but the extra variance parameter does not explain why effects
differ, repair biased studies, or make unrelated estimands commensurable.
Multiple outcomes, time points, treatment arms, and estimates from the same
sample create dependence that the analysis must model, cluster, or reduce by a
prespecified rule.

Report heterogeneity as a substantive question, not only a statistic. Inspect
differences in mathematical content, populations, implementation, comparisons,
measurement, and design. Subgroup or meta-regression patterns are observational
across studies unless a stronger argument exists; small numbers of studies make
such patterns especially unstable.

If estimates cannot be combined responsibly, use a structured narrative or
other transparent synthesis rather than manufacturing one average. Preserve
study direction, magnitude, precision, design credibility, context, and
contradictory results. “Vote counting” significant versus nonsignificant results
throws away effect size and precision and can reverse the evidence story.

Publication bias is only one form of missing evidence. Outcome switching,
unreported time points, inaccessible dissertations, language restrictions, and
duplicate reports can all distort the observed record. Funnel plots and
statistical adjustments are sensitivity tools, not automatic corrections.

**Worked synthesis boundary.** Suppose three studies report effects of
“dynamic geometry.” One randomizes high-school classrooms and measures proof
explanations after a six-week unit; one compares volunteer university sections
on construction accuracy; one interviews middle-school learners about dragging.
All concern a tool family, but they do not estimate one common effect. A useful
synthesis can map affordances, settings, designs, outcomes, and findings, while a
single pooled achievement number would erase the central differences. A narrower
meta-analysis might become defensible only after selecting comparable
interventions, contrasts, constructs, timings, and estimates under a stated
protocol.

## 4. Sampling is part of the claim

Create a sampling ledger:

| Layer | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| Target | About whom, where, and when is the claim intended? |
| Frame | Who could actually be recruited or observed? |
| Selection | Random, complete, volunteer, purposeful, threshold-based? |
| Assignment | How did units enter conditions, if there are conditions? |
| Participation | Who consented, responded, attended, or produced usable data? |
| Analysis | Who was included, excluded, weighted, or imputed? |

Attrition is not only a percentage. Report reasons and whether loss differs by condition or relevant baseline features. In small qualitative samples, explain why these cases are informative. In quantitative work, distinguish precision for the analyzed sample from generalization to the target population.

## 5. Build a measurement argument

Validity is not a permanent sticker attached to a test. Assemble evidence that the proposed interpretation and use of scores are defensible in this population and setting.

Audit:

- **content:** do tasks represent the mathematics claimed?
- **response process:** are learners using the intended reasoning, language, and tools?
- **structure:** do item and score relationships support the proposed scale?
- **relations:** do scores relate to other measures as the construct account predicts?
- **consequences:** what decisions and exclusions follow from using the measure?
- **invariance and fairness:** do relevant groups encounter the same construct or different barriers?

Reliability concerns consistency under specified conditions. A highly consistent measure can reliably measure the wrong construct. Conversely, rich open responses may require carefully trained coding, double-coding, and uncertainty reporting rather than a single simplistic coefficient.

## 6. Plan analysis before results

For a quantitative comparison, state:

- primary outcome and scoring rule;
- estimand, such as intention-to-treat difference in means;
- assignment and clustering units;
- covariates chosen before outcome inspection;
- missing-data strategy;
- model and uncertainty interval;
- multiplicity treatment for several outcomes or subgroups;
- sensitivity analyses and assumption checks.

Report an estimate with its uncertainty and scale. A small p-value does not measure educational importance, replication probability, bias, or construct validity. A wide interval can include effects with different practical meanings; “not significant” is not proof of no effect.

For qualitative analysis, state:

- unit of analysis and corpus boundaries;
- code or interpretive development;
- examples, counterexamples, and negative cases;
- analyst roles and reflexive record;
- triangulation or contrast logic;
- how an interpretation earns or loses support.

Agreement between coders may be relevant, but high agreement does not ensure that the code captures important mathematics.

## 7. Work through a claim-boundary example

Suppose two volunteer grade-8 teachers use a new fraction-discussion routine for four weeks. Their 48 participating learners take the same locally written assessment before and after. The mean rises from 12.1 to 15.0 out of 20.

**What the arithmetic establishes.** For learners with both scores, on this scoring rule, the observed mean change is 2.9 points. A distribution, paired differences, uncertainty, missingness, and item-level behavior are still needed.

**What it does not establish.** Without a comparison or defensible counterfactual, the change cannot isolate the routine from ordinary instruction, test familiarity, maturation, attendance, scoring drift, or other events. Volunteer teachers and a local measure constrain generalization.

**Useful follow-up.** Inspect paired changes and missing cases; review items for alignment and alternate explanations; analyze recorded episodes for the proposed discussion mechanism; add a comparison or stronger rollout design; prespecify the next study's primary contrast. The first cycle can inform design without being advertised as universal causal proof.

## 8. Ethics is part of method

Education research often involves children, employees, unequal authority, and identifiable classrooms. Before data collection:

- obtain appropriate institutional ethics review;
- design meaningful consent and, where appropriate, assent;
- separate participation from grades, employment evaluation, or access to instruction;
- minimize data and specify access, retention, and deletion;
- anticipate deductive identification in small sites and quoted episodes;
- plan how adverse events, disclosures, withdrawal, and conflicts are handled;
- ensure that a comparison does not deny required support;
- compensate and credit community expertise without coercion or tokenism.

“De-identified” is a claim requiring a threat model. A school, rare role, date, quoted phrase, and mathematical episode can jointly identify someone even after names are removed.

## 9. Reproducibility and transparent reporting

Preserve a chain from raw record to claim:

```text
protocol -> consent/version record -> instrument -> data dictionary
-> cleaning decisions -> analysis code or analytic memo -> output -> claim table
```

Where ethical and lawful, preregister confirmatory questions, version instruments and code, retain provenance, and share de-identified data or realistic synthetic examples. When data cannot be shared, explain why and share metadata, transformations, code, and a controlled-access route where feasible.

Create a **claim table** before writing conclusions:

| Claim | Population/setting | Evidence | Assumptions | Uncertainty | Excluded stronger claim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Observed strategy pattern | selected lesson episodes | transcript turns and artifacts | records capture relevant interaction | episode selection, interpretation | prevalence in all classes |
| Assigned-condition contrast | participating clusters | prespecified outcome estimate | randomization, attrition handling | interval and sensitivity | effect of all discussion methods |

Transparent limitations do not weaken good research. They prevent a useful result from being asked to carry a larger conclusion than its design permits.

## 10. Practice and solutions

### Exercise 1: question-design alignment

A proposal asks, “Why do students dislike proof?” and plans only a five-item anonymous rating scale. Diagnose the mismatch and propose two coherent alternatives.

**Solution.** Ratings can describe responses to specified items in the sampled population; alone they cannot establish “why,” nor do they define proof or dislike. One alternative is a bounded prevalence/association question with a validated response-process argument and a defensible sampling plan. Another is a qualitative process question using purposefully selected interviews, classroom episodes, and artifact elicitation to examine how particular proof experiences are interpreted. Each alternative changes the permitted claim.

### Exercise 2: unit of assignment

Sixteen classrooms are randomly assigned, eight per condition, and 420 learner scores are collected. Why is an analysis that treats 420 learners as independently randomized problematic?

**Solution.** The intervention was assigned 16 times, not 420 times. Learners share teachers, peers, and implementation within classrooms, so their outcomes may be correlated. Treating them as independent can understate uncertainty. The analysis and power account should respect classroom clustering and the small number of assignment units, while reporting attrition and implementation by cluster.

### Exercise 3: null result

An estimated difference is 0.18 standard deviations with a 95% interval from -0.12 to 0.48. Evaluate “the intervention has no effect.”

**Solution.** The interval is compatible with a modest negative contrast, almost no contrast, and a moderate positive contrast under the model. The study has not established exact absence. Report the estimate, interval, outcome, population, design, and which practically meaningful values remain compatible. An equivalence claim would require prespecified equivalence bounds and an analysis designed for that question.

### Exercise 4: qualitative generalization

A detailed case study finds that learners use gestures to coordinate a graph and a physical motion. What can travel beyond the case?

**Solution.** The study can support a contextualized account of how gesture functioned in recorded episodes and may propose a mechanism or design conjecture for other settings. It cannot estimate how common the pattern is without an appropriate sampling design. Transfer depends on describing mathematical tasks, participants, language, tools, episode selection, and counterexamples well enough for readers to compare contexts.

### Exercise 5: synthesis without a false average

A review retrieves five reports described as “technology-supported fractions
instruction.” Two reports analyze different outcomes from the same trial; one is
a single-case design; one compares historical cohorts; and one randomized
schools. The reader computes the unweighted mean of all five reported effect
sizes. Diagnose the result.

**Solution.** Reports and estimates are not five independent, exchangeable study
units. The duplicated trial creates dependence; the designs support different
inferences; interventions, comparisons, outcome constructs, and scales may not
share an estimand; and unweighted averaging discards uncertainty. The reviewer
should reconstruct study/report links, apply protocol-based eligibility, code
design and construct differences, choose comparable estimates and a model only
where defensible, handle within-study dependence, and otherwise give a structured
narrative synthesis. Search and selective-reporting limitations must remain in
the conclusion.

## Final project: a claim-bound research protocol

Write a protocol for a feasible mathematics education study. It must contain:

1. one bounded primary question and one deliberately excluded stronger claim;
2. a mathematical-content analysis of the construct;
3. the unit of observation, sampling, assignment if any, and intended target;
4. a measurement argument with one response-process check;
5. an analysis plan, including missing data and discrepant cases;
6. an ethics and data-governance plan;
7. a worked miniature example using synthetic or ethically shareable data;
8. a claim table linking every proposed conclusion to evidence and assumptions;
9. one result pattern that would force the interpretation to change;
10. a reproducibility packet or a precise account of what cannot be shared.

### ED-METHODS — Weighted protocol rubric

**Rubric.** Question–design alignment counts for 25%; mathematical construct and
measurement reasoning for 20%; sampling and analysis for 20%; ethics and
governance for 15%; claim boundaries for 15%; reproducibility for 5%. Passing
work must identify the independent unit and must not use statistical,
qualitative, or design language to imply a stronger inference than the evidence
supports.

## Sources and evidence boundaries

The American Educational Research Association's official reporting standards
call for transparent reporting across empirical social-science methods, and
its ethics code addresses review, conflicts, participants, and accurate
reporting. They are professional standards, not evidence that following a
checklist eliminates bias. [AERA Standards for Research Conduct](https://www.aera.net/Publications/Standards-for-Research-Conduct)
and [AERA Code of Ethics](https://www.aera.net/Portals/38/docs/About_AERA/CodeOfEthics%281%29.pdf)
(accessed 2026-08-31).

The What Works Clearinghouse publishes design and review standards for
evaluating intervention evidence. This course uses the WWC site as an
authoritative example of explicit evidence criteria; it does not imply that
WWC review is the only legitimate research tradition or that an evidence tier
automatically transfers across populations and implementations.
[WWC standards and procedures](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Handbooks)
(accessed 2026-08-31).

PRISMA 2020 is a reporting guideline for systematic reviews, developed
primarily for reviews of intervention effects. It supports transparent reporting
of identification, selection, appraisal, and synthesis; checklist compliance is
not a substitute for a defensible education-specific question or method.
[PRISMA 2020 statement](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71) (accessed
2026-08-31). The current Cochrane Handbook chapter on meta-analysis supplies an
authoritative account of effect measures, dependence, heterogeneity,
random-effects interpretation, missing data, and sensitivity analysis. Its
health-intervention setting is explicit; this course transfers methodological
questions rather than clinical conclusions.
[Cochrane Handbook, Chapter 10](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-10)
(accessed 2026-08-31).

Lewis, Perry, and Murata's 2006 article uses Japanese and US lesson-study
examples to propose a descriptive knowledge base, mechanism study, and
iterative improvement research. It is used here to illustrate how improvement
and mechanism questions differ from a universal efficacy claim.
[Primary article and DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X035003003)
(accessed 2026-08-31).
