## 1. Numbers answer different questions

Imagine a shelf label: **12 oranges, £3.60, 1.8 kg**. The numbers have different jobs.

- $12$ is a count.
- $£3.60$ is an amount of money.
- $1.8 kg$ is a measurement.
- $£3.60 \div 12 = £0.30$ is a rate: cost per orange.

A digit string without its meaning can mislead. Before calculating, ask: “What does this number count or measure?” Keep units attached. $3.60 \div 12 = 0.30$ becomes useful only when read as **£0.30 per orange**.

Place value explains why position matters. In $4,072.5$, the 4 means four thousands, the 7 means seven tens, and the 5 means five tenths. A useful home experiment is to write $4072.5$ on scraps, then cover one digit at a time and say how much value disappeared.

## 2. Arithmetic as actions

Operations describe situations, not just button presses.

- Addition combines: 8 red beads and 5 blue beads make $8 + 5 = 13$ beads.
- Subtraction compares or removes: 13 beads minus 5 leaves 8; 13 is also 5 more than 8.
- Multiplication makes equal groups: 4 bags of 6 apples give $4 \times 6 = 24$ apples.
- Division shares or measures groups: 24 apples shared among 4 bags gives 6 per bag; 24 contains four groups of 6.

These inverse pairs help you check work. If $24 \div 4 = 6$, then $6 \times 4$ must return 24.

**Misconception:** “Multiplication always makes bigger.” Multiplying 12 by $1/2$ gives 6. For a positive quantity, factors above 1 enlarge it, factors between 0 and 1 shrink it, and multiplying by 1 preserves it. Multiplying zero by any of these factors still gives zero.

## 3. Fractions are numbers and comparisons

Fold a sheet of paper into four equal regions and shade three. You have shaded $3/4$: three pieces of size one-fourth. Equal pieces matter; three unequal scraps are not three-fourths.

Fractions can name the same point. If each fourth is split in two, $3/4 = 6/8$. Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number changes the description, not the amount.

To compare $2/3$ and $3/5$, imagine 15 equal pieces:

$2/3 = 10/15$ and $3/5 = 9/15$, so $2/3$ is larger.

For addition, the pieces must have a common size. $1/2 + 1/3$ is not $2/5$; halves and thirds are different units. Using sixths gives $3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6$.

Before moving to ratios, try [What does this quotient measure?](../apps/atlas/index.html#/labs?lab=visual-lab-quantity-division). Its [live quantity workshop](../apps/atlas/index.html#/labs?lab=visual-lab-quantity-division) asks you to name the unknown and its unit before calculating. Compare taking a fraction of liquid, finding a jug's capacity, counting complete portions, and accounting for liquid remaining when the process includes loss. The reading explains fraction multiplication and the whole-number rule locally.

## 4. Ratios and percentages

A ratio compares quantities. Orange squash mixed as 1 part concentrate to 4 parts water has 5 parts total. Concentrate is $1/5$ of the drink, or 20%, not 25%. The phrase “1 to 4” compares concentrate with water; the fraction of the whole compares concentrate with all five parts.

“Percent” means “per hundred.” Three equivalent forms are

$25\% = 25/100 = 1/4 = 0.25$.

To find 15% of £80, use friendly pieces: 10% is £8 and 5% is half of that, £4, so 15% is £12.

A 20% discount followed by a 20% rise does **not** return to the start. £100 falls to £80, then rises by 20% of £80, which is £16, ending at £96. Percent change always has a reference amount.

## 5. Estimate before you calculate

Suppose 7 notebooks cost £3.98 each. Round the price to £4: $7 \times £4 = £28$ estimates the purchase total. The exact total, £27.86, should be near £28. If a calculator shows £278.60, the estimate exposes a misplaced decimal.

For $198 \div 6.1$, use $200 \div 5 = 40$ as a broad estimate or $198 \div 6 = 33$ as a closer one. These point estimates check the scale of an answer. A justified range needs bounds on the inputs and a reason that those bounds control the result.

Try a “quantity walk” at home. Estimate the length of a table, mass of a bag of rice, and time to fill a glass. Then measure. Record the estimate, result, and whether you were high or low. After five attempts, adjust your internal benchmarks.

### Point estimates and guaranteed ranges

Suppose you know only that each notebook costs between £3.95 and £4.00, including the endpoints. You buy seven at the same price, with no other charges. The least possible bill is $7\times395=2765$ pence; the greatest is $7\times400=2800$ pence. Every permitted bill therefore lies between £27.65 and £28.00. Adding seven copies of a price between those bounds cannot leave this interval.

The £28 estimate is an upper bound: it overestimates the bill by at most 35p. This guarantee comes from the stated price interval. At the actual £3.98 price, the overestimate is 14p, which is inside the bound. A maximum possible error and an error found after an exact calculation answer different questions.

**Try a changed purchase.** You buy 9 identical items, each priced between £2.45 and £2.50, with no other charges. Use £22.50 as an estimate. Give a guaranteed range for the bill and the largest possible overestimate. Attempt both before reading the feedback.

**Hint.** Keep the count fixed and multiply each price endpoint by nine. Work in pence to keep whole numbers.

**Worked feedback.** The lower total is $9\times245=2205$ pence (£22.05), and the upper total is $9\times250=2250$ pence (£22.50). The overestimate is at most 45 pence, because $2250-2205=45$. A bill of £22.05 reaches that largest error. A bill of £22.50 makes the estimate exact. The range assumes the stated prices and charges; it is a guarantee for this model.

## Worked decision: which pack is better value?

Pack A has 6 rolls for £4.20. Pack B has 9 for £5.85.

- A costs $£4.20 \div 6 = £0.70$ per roll.
- B costs $£5.85 \div 9 = £0.65$ per roll.

B is 5p cheaper per roll, but “better value” also depends on whether you need nine, can store them, and can afford the larger total today. Mathematics informs a decision; it does not erase its context.

## Optional clinic on combining rates

This untimed extension continues the pack comparison. You may go straight to **Practice** and return later. Use a calculator if you wish, and write an attempt before reading the feedback.

### Predict the combined price

You buy **both** packs: six rolls for £4.20 and nine rolls for £5.85. What is the cost per roll across the complete purchase? Before calculating, predict whether it will be closer to 65p or 70p. Write the total cost and total roll count, with their units.

**Pause here to try it.**

### Check the price against the bill

Work in pence to keep whole numbers. The two costs add to $420+585=1005$ pence. The two counts add to $6+9=15$ rolls. The combined cost per roll is therefore $1005\div15=67$ pence.

Multiply back to the bill: $15\times67=1005$ pence. This exactly recovers what you paid. The answer is closer to 65p because nine rolls came at that lower rate and only six at the higher rate.

The tempting average $(70+65)\div2=67.5$ pence gives each **pack** equal influence. But the packs contain different numbers of rolls. At 67.5p per roll, fifteen rolls would cost $15\times67.5=1012.5$ pence, which does not reproduce the 1005p bill. Being between 65p and 70p is a useful check, but both answers pass that check; multiplying back exposes the error.

You can picture six entries of 70p and nine entries of 65p. Sharing their total equally across fifteen rolls gives 67p. The total is divided by the number of rolls, not the number of packs.

### Check how distance determines time

Speed in kilometres per hour means distance travelled divided by elapsed time. Before the next task, try this small check: **at a constant 3 km per hour, how long does 6 km take?** Write a multiplication equation that checks your answer.

**Check your preparation.** It takes 2 hours, because $3\times2=6$: 3 kilometres per hour for 2 hours covers 6 kilometres. If finding the unknown time is unfamiliar, revisit the multiplication equations in [What does this quotient measure?](../apps/atlas/index.html#/labs?lab=visual-lab-quantity-division), especially **Why dividing fractions uses the reciprocal**. Then return to the journeys below; you do not need calculus.

### Try two journeys

In each journey, speed is constant on each leg, and there are no stops or other parts of the journey. Average speed means **total distance travelled divided by total elapsed time**. Count distance even if the walker changes direction.

1. A walker covers 6 km at 3 km per hour, then a second 6 km at 6 km per hour. Find the time for each leg, the total distance, the total time, and the average speed. Explain whether $(3+6)\div2$ answers this question.
2. Change the instruction: the walker travels **one hour** at 3 km per hour, then **one hour** at 6 km per hour. Find the total distance, total time, and average speed. Explain why the answer differs from Journey 1.

**Write both attempts before continuing.** Keep distance and time visible; a speed number alone leaves the explanation unfinished.

### Hints for the journeys

For Journey 1, solve **speed × time = distance** for each leg. Equal distances need not take equal times. For Journey 2, the times are supplied; use the same multiplication equation to find each distance.

### Full answers for the journeys

**Journey 1.** The first leg takes $6\div3=2$ hours; the second takes $6\div6=1$ hour. The walker covers $6+6=12$ km in $2+1=3$ hours. The average speed is $12\div3=4$ km per hour. The walker spends twice as long at the slower speed. The direct average 4.5 km per hour would predict $4.5\times3=13.5$ km in those three hours, contradicting the stated 12 km.

**Journey 2.** The leg distances are $3\times1=3$ km and $6\times1=6$ km. The total is 9 km in 2 hours, so the average speed is $9\div2=4.5$ km per hour. Averaging the two speed numbers works here because the two durations are equal. Equal distances and equal times are different conditions.

In both the purchase and the journeys, add the underlying quantities before dividing. Use compatible units, count each contribution once, and keep the total after **per** positive. The receipt totals are cost and rolls; the journey totals are distance and time. The rates alone do not tell you how much each part contributes.

### Optional further reasoning

You may continue to **Practice** without this algebra. Let two rates be $r_1=A_1/B_1$ and $r_2=A_2/B_2$, with $B_1,B_2>0$. Use the same units for the two $A$ quantities and the same units for the two $B$ quantities. Assume the two groups do not overlap and that the actual combined totals are $A_1+A_2$ and $B_1+B_2$. Their combined rate is

$$
R=\frac{A_1+A_2}{B_1+B_2}.
$$

Substituting $A_1=B_1r_1$ and $A_2=B_2r_2$ gives

$$
R=\frac{B_1r_1+B_2r_2}{B_1+B_2}.
$$

Thus each rate's influence comes from its denominator contribution. Putting the simple average over a common denominator gives

$$
R-\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}
=\frac{(B_1-B_2)(r_1-r_2)}{2(B_1+B_2)}.
$$

The simple average therefore equals the combined rate exactly when the denominator contributions are equal or the rates themselves are equal. For speed, those contributions are durations. That is why the same two speed numbers produced different answers in the two journeys.

## Practice

1. In $53.407$, what value does the digit 4 represent?
2. Put in increasing order: $0.6$, $5/8$, $58\%$.
3. A recipe for 4 uses 300 g of flour. How much serves 10 at the same ratio?
4. A £60 coat is reduced by 25%. Find the new price.
5. Estimate, then calculate: 19 items at £2.49 each.
6. Explain why $1/3 + 1/4$ cannot be $2/7$.

## Answers

1. Four tenths, or $0.4$.
2. $58\% = 0.58$, then $0.6$, then $5/8 = 0.625$.
3. $300 \div 4 = 75$ g per serving, so $75 \times 10 = 750$ g.
4. 25% is one quarter; $£60 \div 4 = £15$, so the price is £45.
5. Estimate $20 \times £2.50 = £50$; exact total $19 \times £2.49 = £47.31$.
6. The denominators name different piece sizes. In twelfths, $1/3 + 1/4 = 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12$.

**Use the feedback.** If you missed 1, name the place of each digit before reading its value. If you missed 2 or 6, draw equal-sized pieces before converting fractions. If you missed 3 or 4, write what the whole is and what one part represents. If you missed 5, repeat the estimate with £2.50 and 20 items before checking the exact multiplication. Then redo the missed problem with a new number of your own.

### Exit check

Pick one bill, recipe, or shop label. Describe every number’s meaning and unit, make one comparison, and calculate one rate. Then explain how an estimate supports your answer.

For example, two packs may have different prices per item while the larger pack costs more today. A complete explanation names both facts and says which one matters for the decision. Keep your first attempt, revise it after checking the units and arithmetic, and state what changed. If you can do this without the worked example, you are ready for a longer course in fractions, algebra, or data.
