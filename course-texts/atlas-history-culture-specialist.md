## Workshop S1 — French reading, clause scope, and translation

A responsible translation requires more than matching cognate nouns. Identify the finite verb, its subject, qualifications, pronoun antecedents, scope words and mathematical dependencies. Preserve an uncertainty instead of silently resolving it into a later definition. French word order can place an important qualification inside commas; English can move it, but should not delete it.

For A, the head subject is the increment of a partial sum. The finite verb is a future form of “be”; the predicate says that this increment is infinitesimal. The qualification concerns all possible values of the integer index. It does **not**, by grammar alone, state that a single modern delta works simultaneously for all indices. The nearby remainder discussion introduces a sufficiently large index and the subsequent conclusion. Our modern analysis asks which dependence is justified; a translation must not answer that question by inserting a uniformity hypothesis not written in the clause.

For B, the plural participle agrees with the feminine plural noun for values, not with x or with the bounds. The preposition places these values within the given bounds. C uses an indefinite concessive construction: the subjunctive says that the choice of the later index is arbitrary. The surrounding paragraph supplies n′>n; translation cannot erase that restriction. Its main construction imagines assigning a sufficiently large n so as to control the magnitude of a finite tail over all the indicated x-values, with the later index arbitrary. This is the place to compare a modern uniform-Cauchy condition. Calling a single adverb “uniform convergence” would conceal the work done by the rest of the passage.

Here is a complete **course-authored French practice paragraph**, not a historical quotation:

*Pour chaque point fixé, choisissons un indice qui peut dépendre de ce point. Si un même indice convient à tous les points de l'intervalle, la condition est plus forte. Une borne observée sur trois points ne suffit pas à établir cette condition. Il faut conserver la dépendance de chaque choix.*

The imperative is first-person plural: it invites “let us choose,” rather than reporting a past event. The relative pronoun in the first sentence refers to an index. In the second, the same index is shared across all points. The third denies a sufficiency claim, and the fourth states an obligation. A cognate-by-cognate English list would miss the contrast in admissible dependence.

**Task history-specialist-s1-v1.** (i) Translate A, then B and C, with their immediate mathematical restrictions. Give a finite-verb/subject/scope map for all three. (ii) Translate every sentence of the authored paragraph. (iii) Repair “A says the partial sums are equicontinuous, and the French subjunctive proves uniform convergence.” (iv) Explain why retaining an older spelling in a transcription and choosing current English wording are different decisions.

**S1 complete key.** A: “The increment of s_n will be an infinitesimal quantity, for all possible values of n.” The subject is the increment; the future predicate is infinitesimality; the comma-delimited index qualification is retained. The feminine predicate adjective agrees with the noun for quantity, rather than the masculine noun for increment. B: “for all the values of x contained between the given bounds.” This is a prepositional fragment with no finite verb or independent clause subject; its participle qualifies the values. C: “whatever n′ may be,” subject to the surrounding n′>n. Here n′ is the subject and the subjunctive form of “be” is the finite verb. It is a concessive qualification, not a standalone assertion that the series converges. A translation can use “infinitely small” in place of “infinitesimal” if it records that choice and does not silently supply a modern definition.

The authored paragraph translates: “For each fixed point, let us choose an index that may depend on that point. If the same index works for all the points of the interval, the condition is stronger. A bound observed at three points does not suffice to establish that condition. We must preserve the dependence of each choice.” These are our present-day teaching sentences. The repair is that neither the isolated clause nor a grammatical mood supplies the two-variable estimates required for equicontinuity or uniform convergence. The full surrounding argument must be examined and the modern hypothesis stated separately. Diplomatic spelling records a witness; an English rendering explains a meaning under a stated policy. Neither operation authorises changing the mathematical quantifiers.

## Workshop S3 — Reconstruct the all-points finite-tail proof

This is **M, a modern real-valued reconstruction**, not a literal translation or a claim to settle every interpretation of historical infinitesimals. Let D be a nonempty subset of the real line and S_n:D→R. The uniform-Cauchy condition is

\[
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall x\in D\ \forall m>n\ge N:
 |S_m(x)-S_n(x)|<\varepsilon.
\]

It is equivalent to uniform convergence to a real-valued limit, using completeness of R. For sufficiency, fix x. The condition makes S_n(x) a real Cauchy sequence, hence it has a limit S(x). Given epsilon>0, choose N for tail tolerance epsilon/2. Fix n≥N and x; let m→∞. Absolute-value continuity gives |S(x)-S_n(x)|≤epsilon/2<epsilon. The same N worked for every x, so convergence is uniform. The order matters: construct S pointwise using completeness, then prove the uniform bound using the shared cutoff. The passage from a strict finite bound to a non-strict limit was accounted for.

For necessity, suppose S_n→S uniformly. For epsilon>0 choose N so |S_k(x)-S(x)|<epsilon/2 for all k≥N and all x. The triangle inequality gives |S_m(x)-S_n(x)|<epsilon. No boundedness of D and no continuity of S_n was needed for this equivalence. Real completeness was needed: in a noncomplete codomain a pointwise Cauchy sequence can lack its required limit.

If every S_n is continuous on D and convergence is uniform, S is continuous on D. Fix z∈D and epsilon>0; choose N with uniform error below epsilon/3. Continuity of this fixed S_N at z supplies delta>0 so |S_N(x)-S_N(z)|<epsilon/3 for x∈D with |x-z|<delta. Adding the two approximation errors proves |S(x)-S(z)|<epsilon. Continuity is relative to D; endpoint neighbourhoods are intersected with D.

The global hypothesis can be weakened for this conclusion. If each z has a relative neighbourhood U_z on which convergence is uniform, the same argument proves continuity at z. On an open real domain, convergence uniform on each compact subset suffices: choose a small closed interval around z lying in the domain. This is **local uniform convergence**. It need not supply one cutoff for the whole domain.

**Task history-specialist-s3-v1.** Reconstruct both directions of the Cauchy equivalence, name the completeness step and repair the strict-limit inequality. Prove the local version of continuity. Then show that S_n(x)=x^n on D=[0,1) converges pointwise to zero and locally uniformly, yet not uniformly on D. Use the exact moving point x_n=(1/2)^(1/n), not a decimal plot.

**S3 complete key.** The preceding proofs supply the equivalence and continuity arguments in full. For 0≤x<1, x^n→0: if x>0, set c=1/x-1>0 and use (1+c)^n≥1+nc, proved by induction; thus x^n≤1/(1+nc)→0. At x=0 the assertion is immediate for positive n. Every compact K⊂[0,1) has a maximum a<1, so sup_K x^n≤a^n→0; a relative compact neighbourhood works at every point, including 0. But 0<x_n<1 and S_n(x_n)=1/2 for every n, so no shared cutoff makes the error below 1/2. Equivalently sup_D x^n=1 even though the supremum is not attained. Continuity of the limit therefore does not require uniform convergence on the entire noncompact domain. A historical paragraph with one all-x cutoff is stronger than this local sufficient condition under our declared modern reading.

## Workshop S4 — Changed domains and a complete finite certificate

Keep S_n(x)=x^n but change the domain to [0,2/3]. The uniform error is exactly (2/3)^n, attained at the right endpoint. The smallest positive n giving an error strictly below 1/8 is 6:

| n | exact maximum error | strictly below 1/8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2/3 | no |
| 2 | 4/9 | no |
| 3 | 8/27 | no |
| 4 | 16/81 | no |
| 5 | 32/243 | no |
| 6 | 64/729 | yes |

The powers strictly decrease because multiplication by 2/3 shrinks every positive value. Cross multiplication gives 8·32=256>243 but 8·64=512<729, so the first successful index is proved, not guessed from rounded output. For general 0<a<1 the exact error on [0,a] is a^n. The Bernoulli argument above proves existence of a finite cutoff for every positive tolerance. On [0,1] the pointwise limit is zero below 1 and one at 1, hence discontinuous: x_k=1-1/k approaches 1 while the limit values remain zero for k≥2. This endpoint example and the punctured-domain example answer different questions.

For a series version define u_1(x)=x and u_k(x)=x^k-x^(k-1) for k≥2. Its partial sum through k=n is S_n=x^n by telescoping. A finite tail from n+1 through m is x^m-x^n, so the uniform-Cauchy theorem applies to its partial sums. This sign convention must be retained; replacing it by x^n-x^m changes the signed tail, though its magnitude agrees. These are our polynomials, not an attributed historical example.

## Workshop S5 — A derivative limit and a theorem that repairs it

The later source window changes to complex variables. Our following example is instead **a new real-variable test of the overbroad modern claim that uniform convergence permits every termwise operation**. It does not refute the original complex passage or establish what a historical technical term meant.

For positive integers N put

\[
 f_N(x)=\frac{x}{1+Nx^2}\qquad(-1\le x\le1).
\]

Every f_N is continuously differentiable, since its denominator is positive. The inequality (sqrt(N)|x|-1)^2≥0 gives 1+Nx²≥2sqrt(N)|x| and hence |f_N(x)|≤1/(2sqrt(N)). Equality occurs at x=±1/sqrt(N), which belongs to the domain. Therefore the exact supremum tends to zero and f_N→0 uniformly. Direct quotient-rule differentiation gives

\[
 f_N'(x)=\frac{1-Nx^2}{(1+Nx^2)^2}.
\]

At zero every derivative is 1. At a fixed nonzero x, divide numerator and denominator by N²x^4: the resulting numerator tends to zero and denominator to one, so f_N′(x)→0. The pointwise derivative limit is 1 at 0 and 0 elsewhere, whereas the derivative of the uniformly limiting zero function is zero everywhere. Failure occurs at an interior point. In particular derivative convergence is not uniform: each derivative is continuous, while their pointwise limit is discontinuous at zero, contradicting S3's theorem if it were uniform.

Here are the required exact checkpoints N=m²; the peak location is 1/m and the maximum is 1/(2m).

| m | N | peak location | peak value | derivative at 0 | derivative at 1 |
