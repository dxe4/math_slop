
# Counting and numerals across cultures

A number is not the marks used to write it. Thirty-seven counters, three bundles of ten with seven loose counters, and one bundle of twenty with seventeen loose counters can describe the same quantity. The written representation changes; the amount does not.

This L0 orientation needs only counting, addition and multiplication. Its single idea is to separate a quantity from the exchange rules and positions used to record it. It adapts the earlier [Ways to Write Thirty-Seven](counting-across-cultures.md), preserving that longer source and its exercises. For the historical narrative use the actual primary [Counting and Early Arithmetic](../books/history-of-mathematics/counting-and-early-arithmetic.md). The examples below are present classroom reconstructions, not photographs or transcriptions of ancient numeral signs.

## Bundles can carry value in two ways

Imagine a system with a special sign for one and another special sign for ten. Three ten-signs and seven one-signs record thirty-seven. Each sign carries its own value, and adding their values recovers the amount. An additive notation does not need the same place rule as modern decimal notation.

The longer seed discusses Egyptian additive numerals, whose different signs can represent different powers of ten. Our word labels “ten-sign” and “one-sign” are deliberately modern stand-ins. They let us compare the arithmetic without pretending that a keyboard symbol is an authentic historical drawing. The historical source chapter identifies the evidence and conventions used in its broader account.

Place value works differently. In the decimal writing 37, the left digit tells us how many tens and the right digit how many ones. The very same digit written in another position can have a different value. In 407, the four counts hundreds while the seven counts ones. The zero keeps the empty tens position visible. Dropping it would produce 47, a different number.

Thus both signs and positions can carry information, but they do not have to carry the same information. Calling a notation different from ours does not make it unable to calculate. Its exchange rules and the tasks for which it is used are the productive comparison.

## Twenty and sixty are choices, not different quantities

In a pure base-twenty place system, twenty ones exchange for one twenty, and twenty twenties exchange for one four-hundred. Thirty-seven is one twenty and seventeen ones. A position may contain any count from zero through nineteen before another exchange is needed.

The seed's Maya example uses dots and bars to describe counts within a level and stacked levels for positional value. A dot and bar arithmetic model can help us reconstruct such counting, but we must specify which positional rule is being modelled. Calendar conventions can use a modified place related to eighteen twenties. We therefore call the present calculation pure base twenty; we do not claim that this one classroom rule describes every Maya inscription or calendar use.

In base sixty, sixty ones exchange for a sixty, and sixty sixties exchange for a thirty-six-hundred. The seed relates this to Babylonian sexagesimal place value. Here we write the place counts in parentheses, such as “two sixties, five ones.” Those explanatory words are our notation. An actual historical reading can also depend on scale and context, because the surviving marks do not always select an absolute starting place unambiguously.

None of these base choices changes whether two collections have the same number of objects. They change how the count is grouped and how an algorithm records its carries. A good conversion finishes by reconstructing the original amount, rather than treating an unfamiliar string as correct by appearance alone.

## Worked example — The same collection of 125 counters

With groups of ten, first make twelve tens and five loose counters. Exchange ten of those tens for one hundred. The decimal result is one hundred, two tens and five ones: 125.

With groups of twenty, six full groups use 120 counters and leave five. The same amount is therefore six twenties and five ones. With groups of sixty, two full groups again use 120 counters and leave five. It becomes two sixties and five ones.

Reconstruction checks every answer. One hundred plus twenty plus five is 125. Six times twenty plus five is 125. Two times sixty plus five is 125. The count five happens to occupy the units position in all three descriptions, but the group count changes because the group size changes.

Now add one more counter. In each description the loose count becomes six, with no exchange yet needed. If there had already been nineteen loose counters in base twenty, adding one would replace all twenty by a single new twenty-group and leave an empty units place. That is the same underlying exchange idea as a decimal carry, with a different threshold.

## Read a source separately from a reconstruction

The arithmetic can be exact while the historical interpretation remains limited. A reconstructed exchange proves that a written description can encode an amount under a stated rule. It does not prove who first used that rule, how every surviving object was read, or which community borrowed from which other community. Identical numerical results alone cannot establish a transmission route.

The [source-reading chapter](../books/history-of-mathematics/reading-mathematical-sources.md) distinguishes an inspected witness, an editorial interpretation and our calculation. We have used the existing seed and book as our provenance here, without claiming a new inspection of a tablet or manuscript. The point is a mathematical reading habit: identify the unit, the place rule and the reconstruction before making a larger historical claim.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 {#history-counting-numerals-ex-1}

Describe 87 as tens and ones, then as twenties and ones. Check that both descriptions give the same quantity.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Eight tens use eighty counters.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Four twenties also use eighty counters.
</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>
The decimal grouping is eight tens and seven ones. The pure base-twenty grouping is four twenties and seven ones. The checks are eighty plus seven and four times twenty plus seven, both 87. The two group counts differ while the quantity stays the same. Each loose count is below its system's exchange threshold, so no further carry is needed.
</details>

### Exercise 2 {#history-counting-numerals-ex-2}

In a pure base-twenty system, three places count four-hundreds, twenties and ones. What do the place counts one, zero, five represent? Why can the zero not simply be removed?

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
There is one four-hundred and no twenties.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
A two-place list would count twenties and ones instead.
</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>
The three-place list gives four hundred plus zero plus five, which is 405. Removing the middle empty place gives a two-place list with one twenty and five ones, only 25. The zero's job here is to retain positional information. This explains a placeholder role without making an unsupported claim about the origin or every historical use of zero as a number.
</details>
