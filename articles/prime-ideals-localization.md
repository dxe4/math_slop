
# Prime Ideals and Localization: Algebra Under a Microscope

**Before reading.** Read [Ring Homomorphisms, Ideals, and Quotient Rings](../books/algebra-patterns-structure/ring-homomorphisms-ideals-quotients.md) and [Modules and Exact Sequences](../books/commutative-algebra/modules-exact-sequences.md). These are preparation for the ideas used below.

Calculus studies behavior near a point by ignoring distant features. Commutative algebra has a parallel operation: invert every element that is nonzero at a chosen prime ideal. The resulting local ring retains algebra visible near that prime and makes the rest harmlessly invertible.

This deep dive assumes commutative rings with identity, ideals, quotient rings, and modules. It states every condition needed for fractions to make sense and connects the construction to geometry without assuming algebraic geometry.

## Prime ideals are multiplicative fault lines

An ideal $P$ in a commutative ring $R$ is **prime** when $P\ne R$ and

$$ab\in P \Rightarrow a\in P \text{ or } b\in P$$

Equivalently, $R/P$ is an integral domain. The equivalence follows because $(a+P)(b+P)=0+P$ precisely when $ab\in P$.

An ideal $M$ is **maximal** when it is proper and no proper ideal strictly contains it. Equivalently, $R/M$ is a field. Every maximal ideal is prime because every field is a domain. The converse fails: $(0)$ is prime in $Z$, but not maximal because $Z$ is not a field.

In $Z$, prime ideals are $(0)$ and $(p)$ for primes $p$; maximal ideals are the $(p)$. In $k[x]$ for a field $k$, nonzero prime ideals are generated by irreducible polynomials.

## Choose what to invert

A subset $S\subseteq R$ is **multiplicatively closed** when $1\in S$ and products of members remain in $S$. Usually require $0\notin S$; if zero is inverted, the localization collapses to the zero ring.

The localization $S^{-1}R$ consists of formal fractions $r/s$ with $r\in R$, $s\in S$. When $R$ has zero divisors, equality cannot be defined only by cross multiplication. The correct relation is

$r/s = r'/s'$ if some $u\in S$ satisfies $u(rs'-r's)=0$.

If $R$ is a domain, $u$ is nonzero and cancellation reduces this to $rs'=r's$.

Addition and multiplication use familiar formulas:

$$r/s + r'/s' = (rs'+r's)/(ss')$$

$$(r/s)(r'/s') = rr'/ss'$$

One proves these operations are independent of representatives; the factor $u$ in the equivalence relation is exactly what handles annihilation by zero divisors.

## Universal property

The map $\iota:R\to S^{-1}R$, $r\mapsto r/1$, sends every $s\in S$ to a unit with inverse $1/s$. It is universal with this property: if $f:R\to T$ is a ring homomorphism and every $f(s)$ is invertible in $T$, there is a unique map

$f̃:S^{-1}R\to T$, $f̃(r/s)=f(r)f(s)^{-1}$,

with $f̃∘\iota=f$.

This property is safer than manipulating one chosen fraction model. It says localization is the most economical ring in which the selected denominators become invertible.

The map $\iota$ need not be injective. An element $r$ maps to zero exactly when some $s\in S$ annihilates it. For example, localizing $Z/6Z$ by powers of $2$ kills the class $3$, because $2\cdot 3=0 \bmod 6$.

## Localize at a prime

For a prime ideal $P$, its complement $S=R\setminus P$ is multiplicatively closed. If $a,b\notin P$ but $ab\in P$, primality would force one factor into $P$, a contradiction.

Write $R_{P}=(R\setminus P)^{-1}R$. This is a **local ring**: it has one maximal ideal, namely

$$PR_{P}=\{r/s:r\in P, s\notin P\}$$

Why is every fraction outside this ideal a unit? If $r/s$ has $r\notin P$, then $r\in S$, so its inverse $s/r$ exists. Thus nonunits are exactly the elements of $PR_{P}$; an ideal containing all nonunits is the unique maximal ideal.

**Integer example.** In $Z_{p}$, every integer not divisible by $p$ becomes invertible. Fractions have denominators coprime to $p$. The unique maximal ideal consists of fractions whose numerator is divisible by $p$. This ring remembers $p$-divisibility while ignoring every other prime.

For $p=3$, $10/14$ is a unit because its numerator is not divisible by three; $6/5$ lies in the maximal ideal.

## Localization and prime spectra

Prime ideals of $S^{-1}R$ correspond to prime ideals $P$ of $R$ disjoint from $S$. Extension sends $P$ to $S^{-1}P$; contraction takes a prime of the localization back along $\iota$.

If localizing at a prime $P$, the surviving primes are exactly those contained in $P$. Every element outside $P$ has become a unit, and no proper ideal can contain a unit.

For a single element $f$, let $S=\{1,f,f^{2},\ldots\}$ and write $R_{f}$. The surviving primes are those not containing $f$. In geometric language, this corresponds to the basic open set $D(f)$ of points where $f$ does not vanish.

The direction is worth checking: localizing at $f$ removes primes that contain $f$, because $f$ becomes invertible and a proper prime cannot contain it.

## Modules localize too

For an $R$-module $M$, define $S^{-1}M$ from fractions $m/s$, with the analogous equivalence. It is an $S^{-1}R$-module. Localization is exact: applying $S^{-1}(-)$ to an exact sequence of modules preserves exactness.

The mechanism is denominator clearing. If $m/s$ maps to zero, some denominator multiplier kills the image of $m$; exactness before localization supplies a preimage after multiplying, and that multiplier is invertible in the localization.

Localization can test equality and vanishing locally. An $R$-module $M$ is zero exactly when $M_{P}=0$ for every prime (equivalently every maximal) ideal $P$. If a nonzero element exists, its annihilator lies inside some maximal ideal, and localization there keeps the element visible.

Finite generation matters in many local-to-global statements, though not in this basic vanishing criterion for a module as a whole. Always inspect the precise theorem before exporting a local fact globally.

## Worked polynomial example

**Worked example.**

Let $R=k[x,y]$ and $P=(x,y)$, the ideal of polynomials with zero constant term. In $R_{P}$, every polynomial not in $(x,y)$ becomes invertible—in particular every polynomial with nonzero constant term.

Thus $1+x$ is a unit, but $x$ and $y$ lie in the maximal ideal. The fraction $x/(1+y)$ is a legitimate local element and remains nonunit because its numerator lies in $P$.

If instead localize at powers of $x$, then $x$ becomes a unit. The prime $(x,y)$ does not survive because it contains $x$; the prime $(y)$ does survive because it is disjoint from $\{1,x,x^{2},\ldots\}$.

## Localization is not quotienting

Quotienting $R/I$ declares elements of $I$ equal to zero. Localization declares elements of $S$ invertible. These operations can both simplify a ring, but in different directions.

For $Z$, quotienting by $(p)$ gives the finite field $F_{p}$; localizing at $(p)$ gives the infinite local domain $Z_{p}$. The quotient keeps arithmetic modulo $p$; the localization keeps exact integer information while permitting division by every number coprime to $p$.

## Common errors

- Forgetting to require $S$ be multiplicatively closed.
- Using ordinary cross multiplication in a ring with zero divisors without the annihilating factor.
- Assuming the canonical map into a localization is always injective.
- Reversing which primes survive: primes disjoint from $S$ survive.
- Saying $R_{P}$ keeps only elements of $P$; it contains fractions from all of $R$, but only $P$-numerators are nonunits.
- Confusing the quotient $R/P$ with the local ring $R_{P}$.
- Using a local-to-global conclusion without its finite-generation or finiteness hypotheses.

### 1. Inverting powers of two

**Exercise 1.**

Describe $S^{-1}Z$ for $S=\{1,2,4,8,\ldots\}$.

Fractions in the localization may use only denominators from the specified multiplicative set.
</details>

Reduce each fraction and characterize exactly which denominator prime factors can remain.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

It consists of rational numbers whose reduced denominator is a power of two: the dyadic rationals $Z[1/2]$. The universal map makes $2$ invertible.

### 2. A kernel appears

<a id="deep-dive-prime-ideals-localization-ex-2"></a>

**Exercise 2.**

In $R=Z/6Z$, localize at powers of the class $2$. Which element is forced to zero?


<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

An element maps to zero after localization if some allowed denominator annihilates it.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Multiply the class of three by an allowed power of the class of two in the ring modulo six.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The class $3$ maps to zero because $2\cdot 3=0$. The localization map is therefore not injective.

</details>

### 3. Units in a local ring

Which fractions are units in $Z_{5}$?

**Answer.** A fraction $a/b$ with $5∤b$ is a unit exactly when $5∤a$. If the numerator is divisible by five, it lies in the unique maximal ideal.

### 4. Surviving primes

Which prime ideals of $Z$ survive in $Z[1/6]$?

**Answer.** Model $Z[1/6]$ using the multiplicative set $\{2^{a}3^{b}:a,b\ge 0\}$; this gives the same localization as using powers of $6$. Surviving primes are disjoint from that set: $(0)$ and $(p)$ for primes $p\ne 2,3$. Ideals $(2)$ and $(3)$ disappear because they contain elements made invertible.

### 5. Universal factorization

If $f:R\to T$ sends every element outside a prime $P$ to a unit, write the forced extension to $R_{P}$.

**Answer.** It must send $r/s$ to $f(r)f(s)^{-1}$. This is well-defined and unique by the localization universal property.
