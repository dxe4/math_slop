
# From Finite Chance to Measure-Theoretic Probability

**Before reading.** Read [Outer Measure and Measurability](../books/measure-and-integration/outer-measure-and-measurability.md) and [Random Variables and Distributions](../books/introduction-to-probability/random-variables-distributions.md). These are preparation for the ideas used below.

Finite probability lets every subset be an event and every expectation be a sum. Continuous models and infinite sequences force harder questions: which subsets can receive probabilities consistently, when may a limit pass through an expectation, and what does “condition on the observed information” mean when that information has probability zero?

This bridge assumes proof-based real analysis and an introduction to measure. It explains why the machinery appears and proves the parts that fit in one sitting. It does not pretend to replace construction theorems or a full integration course.

## 1. Why not measure every subset?

A probability space is $(\Omega,F,P)$, where $F$ is a sigma-algebra and $P$ is countably additive. On a finite $\Omega$, taking $F$ to be every subset causes no problem. On an uncountable space such as $[0,1]$, no translation-invariant countably additive length extending interval length can be assigned to every subset, assuming the usual set-theoretic framework. Nonmeasurable sets force us to specify the event collection.

A sigma-algebra is closed under complements and countable unions. This matches the operations needed for statements such as “at least one event occurs,” “infinitely many occur,” and “a sequence converges.” The Borel sigma-algebra on the real line is generated by open intervals. Completing a measure adds all subsets of null sets; Borel and Lebesgue sigma-algebras are therefore not identical.

## 2. Probability measures and distribution functions

Countable additivity implies continuity. If $A_{n}\uparrow A$, then $P(A_{n})\uparrow P(A)$. If $A_{n}\downarrow A$, then $P(A_{n})\downarrow P(A)$. These are not optional technicalities: they justify approximating a complicated event by simpler nested ones.

Every CDF $F$ that is nondecreasing, right-continuous, and has limits zero and one at the ends determines a unique probability measure on the Borel real line. The measure may mix:

- atoms, visible as jumps of $F$;
- a density component;
- a singular continuous component, as in the Cantor distribution.

Therefore “discrete or continuous” is not an exhaustive dichotomy, and “continuous” does not always mean “has a density.”

## 3. Random variables are measurable maps

A random variable $X:\Omega\to R$ must be measurable: $\{\omega:X(\omega)\le x\}$ is an event for every $x$. This condition ensures questions about numerical output have probabilities.

The distribution or pushforward measure is

$$P_{X}(B)=P(X^{-1}(B))$$

Expectation depends only on this law:

$$E[g(X)]=\int_\Omega g(X(\omega))dP(\omega)=\int_{R} g(x)dP_{X}(x)$$

whenever either side is defined. This “law of the unconscious statistician” is a change-of-measure identity, not permission to integrate against a density that may not exist.

## 4. Building the integral

Start with indicators: $E[1_{A}]=P(A)$. Extend linearly to nonnegative simple functions. For a nonnegative measurable $X$, choose simple $X_{n}\uparrow X$ and define $E[X]=\lim E[X_{n}]$; the value may be infinity. For signed $X$, write $X=X^{+}-X^{-}$. Integrability means $E[|X|]<\infty$, ensuring neither side produces $\infty-\infty$.

The monotone convergence theorem states:

If $0\le X_{n}\uparrow X$, then $E[X_{n}]\uparrow E[X]$.

Fatou’s lemma gives

$$\mathbb E[\liminf X_{n}]\le\liminf\mathbb E[X_{n}]$$

for nonnegative variables. The dominated convergence theorem states that if $X_{n}\to X$ almost surely and $|X_{n}|\le Y$ for an integrable $Y$, then $E[X_{n}]\to E[X]$.

Every hypothesis earns its place. **Worked example.** Let $X_{n}=n\,1_{(0,1/n)}$ on $[0,1]$. Then $X_{n}\to 0$ almost everywhere but $E[X_{n}]=1$. There is no single integrable dominator; exchanging limit and expectation would be wrong.

## 5. Product measures and independence

Given spaces $(\Omega₁,F₁,P₁)$ and $(\Omega₂,F₂,P₂)$, the product measure is first specified on rectangles by

$$(P₁\otimes P₂)(A\times B)=P₁(A)P₂(B)$$

and then extended to the generated product sigma-algebra. Coordinate projections are independent by construction.

Fubini’s theorem permits iterated integration for integrable functions:

$$\int f\,d(P_1\otimes P_2)=\int\left(\int f(x,y)\,dP_2(y)\right)\,dP_1(x)$$

Tonelli permits the same for nonnegative functions even when the integral is infinite. Without nonnegativity or absolute integrability, changing integration order can change or destroy the answer.

Independent random variables satisfy factorization $E[f(X)g(Y)]=E[f(X)]E[g(Y)]$ when $f,g$ are measurable and $f(X)$ and $g(Y)$ are individually integrable; Tonelli then makes their product integrable. The proof begins with indicators of measurable sets, extends to simple functions, then uses convergence theorems. This shows why sigma-algebras and approximation underlie familiar algebra.

## 6. Conditional expectation replaces conditional density

For an integrable $X$ and sub-sigma-algebra $\mathcal{G}$, a conditional expectation $E[X|\mathcal{G}]$ is a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable such that

$\int_{A} E[X|\mathcal{G}]dP=\int_{A} XdP$ for every event $A\in \mathcal{G}$.

It is unique up to almost-sure equality. Existence follows from the Radon–Nikodym theorem: the signed set function $\nu(A)=\int_{A} XdP$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $P$ restricted to $\mathcal{G}$.

If $\mathcal{G}$ is generated by finite partition cells $\{G_{i}\}$, the definition reduces on each positive-probability cell to the familiar conditional average

$E[X|\mathcal{G}](\omega)=E[X1_{G_{i}}]/P(G_{i})$ for $\omega\in G_{i}$.

The abstract definition also handles conditioning on a continuous observation, where events $\{Y=y\}$ often have probability zero. A regular conditional distribution is an additional object whose existence relies on structural assumptions such as standard Borel spaces; one should not assume pointwise conditional densities always exist.

### Projection interpretation

If $X\in L^{2}$, then $E[X|\mathcal{G}]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto the closed subspace of square-integrable $\mathcal{G}$-measurable variables. For every such $Z$,

$$E[(X-E[X|\mathcal{G}])Z]=0$$

This proves optimal prediction: $E[X|\mathcal{G}]$ minimizes mean squared error among $\mathcal{G}$-measurable predictors.

## 7. Convergence modes and probability limits

Almost-sure convergence controls individual outcomes outside one null set. Convergence in probability controls the probability of an error exceeding each tolerance. $L^{p}$ convergence controls an average power of error. Convergence in distribution controls bounded continuous test functions, equivalently CDF values at continuity points on the line.

Implications run

$L^{p} \Rightarrow probability \Rightarrow distribution$, and $almost sure \Rightarrow probability$.

No general reverse arrow holds. Subsequence principles partly repair this: convergence in probability admits an almost-surely convergent subsequence. Uniform integrability helps turn convergence in probability plus tail control into $L^{1}$ convergence.

The central limit theorem asserts only convergence in distribution; it does not assert pointwise convergence of standardized sums to a normal variable on their original space. The strong law is an almost-sure statement and is strictly stronger than the weak law’s convergence in probability.

## 8. From information to martingales

A filtration $F_{0}\subseteq F_{1}\subseteq \ldots$ models growing information. If $X$ is integrable, then

$$M_{n}=E[X|F_{n}]$$

is a martingale. Indeed, the tower property gives

$$E[M_{n+1}|F_{n}]=E[E[X|F_{n+1}]|F_{n}]=E[X|F_{n}]=M_{n}$$

Thus martingales are not only “fair games”; they are consistent forecasts as information accumulates. Martingale convergence theorems require bounds such as uniform integrability or bounded $L^{p}$ norms. Merely having constant expectations is not enough to guarantee a well-behaved limit.

**Exercise 1.**

Let $X=0$ with probability $1/2$, and otherwise be uniform on $[0,1]$. Describe its CDF.

<summary>Hint 1</summary>

The distribution has a point mass at zero and a continuous part on the unit interval.
</details>

<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Add the jump from the atom to half of the uniform cumulative probability at each x in the interval.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$F(x)=0$ for $x<0$; for $0\le x\le 1$, $F(x)=1/2+x/2$; and $F(x)=1$ for $x\ge 1$. It has an atom of size $1/2$ at zero and density $1/2$ on $(0,1)$.

</details>

### 2. Monotone approximation

<a id="deep-dive-measure-probability-bridge-ex-2"></a>

**Exercise 2.**

For nonnegative $X$, let $X_{n}=\min(X,n)$. What does monotone convergence imply?


<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

The truncated variables increase pointwise to the nonnegative original variable.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Apply monotone convergence to their expectations, allowing an infinite limiting expectation.
</details>
<details>

$X_{n}\uparrow X$, so $E[\min(X,n)]\uparrow E[X]$, including when the final expectation is infinite.

### 3. Failed dominated convergence

Why can no integrable $Y$ dominate every $X_{n}=n\,1_{(0,1/n)}$?

**Answer.** If such $Y$ existed, dominated convergence would force $E[X_{n}]\to 0$, contradicting $E[X_{n}]=1$. Directly, the required values near zero create a nonintegrable envelope.

### 4. Finite conditional expectation

On four equally likely outcomes, $X=(0,2,4,10)$. Information reveals only whether the outcome lies in the first pair or second pair. Find $E[X|G]$.

**Answer.** It is constant 1 on the first pair and 7 on the second pair, the averages within the observable cells.

### 5. Forecast martingale

Verify $M_{n}=E[X|F_{n}]$ has constant expectation.

**Answer.** The tower property gives $E[M_{n}]=E[E[X|F_{n}]]=E[X]$. Constant expectation alone does not prove a process is a martingale; the conditional identity does.
