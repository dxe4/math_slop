
# Spectra, Tensors, and the Limits of Diagonalization

**Before reading.** Read [Eigenvalues, Diagonalization, and Field Extensions](../books/linear-algebra-structure-proofs/eigenvalues-and-diagonalization.md), [Inner Products and Orthogonal Decomposition](../books/linear-algebra-structure-proofs/inner-products-orthogonal-decomposition.md) and [Dual Spaces, Annihilators, and Transpose Maps](../books/linear-algebra-structure-proofs/dual-spaces-annihilators-transpose.md). These are preparation for the ideas used below.

Matrices invite a powerful hope: choose coordinates in which a complicated object becomes diagonal. For normal operators and symmetric bilinear forms, that hope succeeds spectacularly. For general operators it needs Jordan chains; for higher-order tensors it usually fails. This deep dive develops the successful theorems, the universal language of tensors, and the boundary where matrix intuition stops transferring.

It assumes finite-dimensional vector spaces over $R$ or $C$, duality, inner products, eigenvalues, and comfort with proofs.

## 1. One operator: polynomial structure

Let $T:V\to V$. Every polynomial $p$ acts by $p(T)$. The minimal polynomial $m_{T}$ records the shortest annihilating relation. If

$$m_{T}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{r} (x-\lambda_{j})$$

splits into distinct linear factors, then $T$ is diagonalizable. The Chinese remainder theorem produces polynomials $q_{j}$ satisfying

$$\sum q_{j}(x) \prod_{k\ne j}(x-\lambda_{k})=1$$

Substituting $T$ gives projection operators onto eigenspaces. Thus diagonalization is not merely finding enough lucky vectors; it is decomposition of the polynomial algebra generated by $T$.

If a factor repeats, nilpotent structure can remain. For $J=\lambda I+N$ with $N^{m}=0$, analytic or polynomial functions satisfy the finite Taylor formula

$$f(J)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1} f^{k}(\lambda)N^{k}/k!$$

whenever the derivatives make sense. The nilpotent part stores derivatives of $f$, explaining polynomial factors in $J^{n}$ and sensitivity near defective matrices.

## 2. Normality turns Schur form diagonal

Every complex matrix has a unitary Schur factorization $A=QUQ*$ with $U$ upper triangular. If $A$ is normal, so is $U$. Compare the squared norm of the first row and first column of $U$: normality makes them equal, but triangularity leaves only the diagonal in the first column, forcing all off-diagonal entries of the first row to vanish. Induction shows $U$ is diagonal.

Hence a complex normal operator has an orthonormal eigenbasis. Real symmetric matrices are the real specialization. The orthonormal condition matters computationally and geometrically: the change-of-basis condition number is 1, spectral projectors are orthogonal, and

$$\lVert A\rVert₂=\max_{i} |\lambda_{i}|$$

For a nonnormal diagonalizable matrix $A=PDP^{-1}$, eigenvalues alone can mislead because $P$ may be ill-conditioned. Short-time powers or exponentials can grow even when every eigenvalue suggests decay. Pseudospectra quantify how small perturbations move eigenvalues and belong in any advanced continuation.

## 3. SVD diagonalizes a map between different spaces

An arbitrary map $A:V\to W$ cannot be diagonalized by similarity when dimensions differ. But $A^{*}A:V\to V$ is positive semidefinite and self-adjoint. Choose orthonormal eigenvectors $v_{i}$ with eigenvalues $\sigma_{i}^{2}$. For $\sigma_{i}>0$, set $u_{i}=Av_{i}/\sigma_{i}$; then

$$\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle=\langle v_{i},A^{*}Av_{j}\rangle/(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})=\delta_{ij}$$

Extending the $u_{i}$ and $v_{i}$ to orthonormal bases yields

$$A=U\Sigma V^{*}$$

This is a two-sided diagonal form: right singular vectors are input directions, left singular vectors are output directions, and singular values are stretch factors. The SVD exists even for rectangular or defective matrices because it diagonalizes the normal operators $A^{*}A$ and $AA^{*}$, not $A$ itself.

The polar decomposition follows: $A=QH$, where $H=(A^{*}A)^{1/2}$ is positive semidefinite and $Q$ is a partial isometry, unitary when $A$ is invertible. It separates stretch from rigid motion.

## 4. Bilinear forms use congruence, not similarity

A bilinear form $B:V\times V\to F$ corresponds to a linear map $V\to V*$, $v\mapsto B(v,-)$. After choosing a basis its matrix changes as $M\mapsto PᵀMP$. Similarity describes one input and one output vector in the same space; congruence describes two vector slots feeding a scalar. For a complex sesquilinear or Hermitian form, conjugation in one slot changes the rule to $M\mapsto P*MP$.

For a real symmetric form, the spectral theorem supplies orthogonal diagonalization. Under general invertible congruence, diagonal magnitudes can be rescaled, leaving only counts of positive, negative, and zero entries. Sylvester’s law of inertia is therefore the correct canonical classification.

For a skew-symmetric form over characteristic not 2, canonical blocks are $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ plus zeros. A nondegenerate skew form exists only in even dimension—the linear algebra underlying symplectic geometry.

## 5. Tensors solve a universal problem

A map $f:V\times W\to X$ bilinear in both arguments is not linear on the Cartesian product as a vector space. The tensor product $V\otimes W$ is equipped with a bilinear map $(v,w)\mapsto v\otimes w$ such that every bilinear $f$ factors uniquely through a linear map $f̃:V\otimes W\to X$:

$$f(v,w)=f̃(v\otimes w)$$

Construct $V\otimes W$ from formal symbols $(v,w)$ and quotient by relations enforcing additivity and scalar compatibility. If $e_{i}$ and $f_{j}$ are bases, then $e_{i}\otimes f_{j}$ form a basis, so

$$\dim(V\otimes W)=\dim V \cdot \dim W$$

Pure tensors $v\otimes w$ do not fill the tensor product: sums of pure tensors are generally needed. In coordinates, a second-order tensor may look like a matrix, but the transformation law and the spaces attached to each slot determine its meaning.

## 6. Duals identify multilinear maps

There are natural finite-dimensional isomorphisms

$$Bil(V,W;F) \simeq V*\otimes W*$$

and

$$\operatorname{Hom}(V,W) \simeq W\otimes V*$$

The second sends $w\otimes\phi$ to the rank-one map $v\mapsto \phi(v)w$. In bases, this is the outer product of a column and a row. Trace is the contraction pairing the $W$ and $W*$ slots when $V=W$; it is basis independent because contraction is defined without coordinates.

Tensor notation should say which slots are vectors and which are covectors. In Euclidean coordinates an inner product silently identifies them, but under a nonorthonormal change of basis, confusing upper and lower slots gives the wrong transformation rule.

## 7. Symmetric and exterior powers

The tensor square splits into symmetric and antisymmetric parts when the field’s characteristic is not 2:

$$v\otimes w = 1/2(v\otimes w+w\otimes v) + 1/2(v\otimes w-w\otimes v)$$

Symmetric powers encode homogeneous polynomials. Exterior powers impose $v\land v=0$; $v₁\land \ldots\land v_{k}$ vanishes exactly when the vectors are dependent. The top exterior power is one-dimensional and an endomorphism acts on it by $\det T$.

The induced map $\land ^{k} T$ has eigenvalues equal to products $\lambda_{i_{1}}\ldots\lambda_{i_{k}}$ when $T$ is diagonalizable. This connects volume growth, minors, compound matrices, and dynamical expansion of $k$-dimensional subspaces.

## 8. Why higher-order tensors do not have one SVD

A matrix’s rank has equivalent definitions: column-space dimension, minimum number of rank-one outer products, and number of nonzero singular values. For order-three tensors these notions separate. Tensor rank can be difficult to compute, best low-rank approximations may fail to exist over some formats, and decompositions can be nonunique or ill-conditioned.

The CANDECOMP/PARAFAC model writes

$$T\approx\sum_{r=1}^{R} a_{r}\otimes b_{r}\otimes c_{r}$$

Tucker decomposition chooses subspaces in each mode and a core tensor. Tensor trains factor a high-order array into a chain of low-order cores. Each format imposes a different structural claim; calling all of them “the tensor SVD” hides essential distinctions.

Even symmetric tensors need not behave like symmetric matrices. Some admit orthogonal decompositions under special hypotheses, but there is no general spectral theorem with all the matrix properties learners may expect.

## 9. Worked example: a rank-two third-order tensor

**Worked example.**

Let $e₁,e₂$ be the standard basis of $R^{2}$ and

$$T=e₁\otimes e₁\otimes e₁ + e₂\otimes e₂\otimes e₂$$

As a trilinear form on covectors, it evaluates to

$$T(x,y,z)=x₁y₁z₁+x₂y₂z₂$$

Contracting the third slot with $z=(1,1)$ yields the identity matrix; contracting with $(1,-1)$ yields $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$. A change of basis acts in all three modes. Flattening $T$ into a $2\times 4$ matrix may help compute, but that flattening privileges one slot and can hide symmetries of the original tensor.

For $J=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}$, compute $\exp(J)$.

The nilpotent part squares to zero, so its exponential series stops after the linear term.

<summary>Solution</summary>

Write $J=\lambda I+N$ with $N^{2}=0$. Since the pieces commute, $\exp(J)=e^\lambda \exp(N)=e^\lambda(I+N)=e^\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

### 2. SVD versus eigenvalues

<a id="deep-dive-spectral-tensor-structure-ex-2"></a>

**Exercise 2.**

The shear $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ has both eigenvalues 1. Can its largest singular value grow with $|k|$?


<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Eigenvalues alone do not measure how much a nonnormal matrix stretches vectors.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Apply the shear to the second coordinate vector to obtain a lower bound for its operator norm.
</details>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Yes. Apply $A$ to $(0,1)$ to obtain $(k,1)$, so $\lVert A\rVert₂\ge \sqrt{k^{2}+1}$. Eigenvalues do not control norm for nonnormal matrices.

</details>

### 3. Tensor-product dimension

If $\dim V=3$ and $\dim W=4$, find $\dim(V\otimes W)$ and explain why twelve basis tensors suffice.

**Solution.** For bases $e_{i}$ and $f_{j}$, bilinearity expands every $v\otimes w$ into combinations of the twelve $e_{i}\otimes f_{j}$; the universal construction also shows these are independent. Hence dimension is 12.

### 4. Exterior dependence test

Why is $(1,0,1)\land (0,1,1)\land (1,1,2)=0$?

**Solution.** The third vector is the sum of the first two. Multilinearity and alternation give $u\land v\land (u+v)=u\land v\land u+u\land v\land v=0$.

### 5. Boundary of analogy

Give two matrix facts that fail or become ambiguous for order-three tensors.

**Solution.** Acceptable answers include equivalence of rank definitions, universal existence of best rank-$R$ approximations, uniqueness of singular directions, and a general orthogonal diagonalization theorem. A strong answer names the tensor format and field because behavior depends on both.
