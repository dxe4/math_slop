
# Noetherian rings and Hilbert's basis theorem

A ring may contain infinitely many elements and still have an important finiteness property: each ideal can be described using finitely many generators. Hilbert's basis theorem says this property survives adjoining a polynomial variable. It does not say that one generator suffices, or that an arbitrary infinite collection of polynomial variables is harmless.

This **Years 2–3 (L3)** orientation assumes [Rings and ideals by example](#/articles/commutative-algebra-rings-ideals-examples) and [polynomial algebra](#/books/algebra-patterns-structure/polynomial-rings-factorization). Rings are commutative with identity; the zero ring is allowed. We prove the one-variable theorem and the finite-module ingredient it uses. The primary [Rings and Ideals chapter](#/books/commutative-algebra/rings-and-ideals) supplies the ideal conventions, and [Modules and Exact Sequences](#/books/commutative-algebra/modules-exact-sequences) contains the developed Noetherian-module and Hilbert theorem arguments.

## Finite generation controls ascending chains

Call $R$ Noetherian if every ideal is generated by a finite set. Every field is Noetherian because its only ideals are zero and the entire field. The integers are Noetherian because every integer ideal is principal, using the least-positive-element argument for nonzero ideals.

If $I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots$ is an ascending ideal chain in a Noetherian ring, its union $I$ is an ideal. Choose finite generators of $I$. Each belongs to some chain member, and finitely many indices have a maximum $N$. Then all generators lie in $I_N$, giving $I=I_N$ and stabilization thereafter.

Conversely, an ideal without finite generators would allow us repeatedly to adjoin an element outside the ideal generated so far, producing a strictly ascending chain. Thus the finite-generation condition is equivalent to stabilization of every ascending ideal chain. The word finite describes ideal descriptions; it does not bound the number of ring elements.

## A finite-coordinate lemma

We need the following module fact: every submodule $K\subset R^n$ is finitely generated when $R$ is Noetherian.

Prove it by induction on $n$. For $n=0$ it is immediate. Project $K\subset R^n$ to its last coordinate. Its image is an ideal of $R$, so choose finitely many generators and lift each to a vector of $K$. The kernel of this projection on $K$ identifies with a submodule of $R^{n-1}$, which has finite generators by induction. Any vector of $K$ can have its last coordinate canceled by a linear combination of the chosen lifts; the remainder is in that kernel. Combining the two finite lists generates $K$.

This proof uses only Noetherianity of $R$, not the polynomial theorem we are about to establish. It is therefore a legitimate ingredient rather than a circular appeal to the desired conclusion.

## Hilbert's basis theorem

The primary [Noetherian Rings and Hilbert Basis Theorem chapter](#/books/commutative-algebra/noetherian-hilbert-basis) supplies the longer module, localisation and Nakayama continuation.

**Theorem.** If $R$ is Noetherian, then $R[x]$ is Noetherian.

*Proof.* Let $I\subset R[x]$ be an ideal. If $I=0$, its empty generating list suffices. Otherwise let $L$ consist of zero and the leading coefficients of nonzero polynomials in $I$.

This is an ideal of $R$. To add two leading coefficients, multiply their polynomials by powers of $x$ to align the degrees, then add. If the leading coefficients cancel, their sum is zero, already in $L$; otherwise their sum is the new leading coefficient. For a leading coefficient $a$ of $f$, if $ra\ne0$ then $rf$ has leading coefficient $ra$. If $ra=0$, that scalar product already belongs to $L$ as zero, even if $rf$ has lower nonzero terms. Negatives are included in this argument.

Choose nonzero finite generators $a_1,\ldots,a_s$ of $L$ and polynomials $f_i\in I$ with leading coefficients $a_i$. Put $N=\max_i\deg f_i$. The polynomials of degree less than $N$, including zero, form the finite free module $R^N$. Their intersection with $I$ is a submodule, so the lemma gives finite generators $g_1,\ldots,g_t$ over $R$. When $N=0$, this submodule is zero and no $g_j$ are needed.

For $f\in I$ of degree $d\ge N$, write its leading coefficient as $\sum_i r_i a_i$. Then

$$f-\sum_i r_i x^{d-\deg f_i}f_i$$

has degree strictly less than $d$, or is zero. Repeat this reduction. The nonnegative degree decreases at every nonzero step, so eventually the remainder has degree less than $N$ and is an $R$-linear combination of the $g_j$. Consequently every $f$ is an $R[x]$-linear combination of the finite list of $f_i$ and $g_j$. This proves every ideal finitely generated. ∎

No field assumption was used; the leading-coefficient ideal handles coefficients that cannot be divided. Iteration gives the theorem for finitely many variables. The argument is an existence proof of finite generators, not automatically an efficient procedure for computing them from arbitrary input descriptions.

## Worked example: finite generators need not reduce to one

Since $\mathbb Z$ is Noetherian, the theorem makes $\mathbb Z[x]$ Noetherian. Its ideal $I=(2,x)$ visibly has two generators, but is not principal.

Suppose $I=(h)$. Because $h$ divides the nonzero constant two in the domain $\mathbb Z[x]$, its degree must be zero, so $h$ is a nonzero integer. Because it also divides $x$, that integer divides the coefficient one of $x$ and must be $1$ or $-1$. Then $(h)$ would be the entire ring. But evaluating at $x=0$ and reducing modulo two kills every element of $(2,x)$ while sending one to one, proving $I$ proper. This is a contradiction.

Thus Noetherianity does not assert that all ideals are principal. It guarantees some finite generating list while allowing genuinely multigenerator ideals like this one.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 {#commutative-algebra-noetherian-ex-1}

If $R$ is Noetherian and $J$ is any ideal, prove $R/J$ is Noetherian.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Take an ideal in the quotient and pull it back along the quotient map.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Images of a finite generating list generate the quotient ideal.
</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>
For an ideal $K\subset R/J$, its preimage $I\subset R$ is an ideal containing $J$. Choose finite generators $a_1,\ldots,a_m$ of $I$. Any element of $K$ is the image of an element $\sum r_i a_i$ and therefore is an $R/J$-linear combination of the images of the $a_i$. Those images generate $K$. Since $K$ was arbitrary, the quotient is Noetherian. Combining this result with the theorem shows every quotient of a polynomial ring in finitely many variables over a field is Noetherian.
</details>

### Exercise 2 {#commutative-algebra-noetherian-ex-2}

Show $k[x_1,x_2,\ldots]$, with countably infinitely many variables over a field $k$, is not Noetherian. Each polynomial uses only finitely many variables.

<details><summary>Hint 1</summary>
Consider $(x_1)\subset(x_1,x_2)\subset\cdots$.
</details>
<details><summary>Hint 2</summary>
Evaluate the first $n$ variables at zero to prove $x_{n+1}$ is not in the preceding ideal.
</details>
<details><summary>Full solution</summary>
For each $n$, substitution $x_1=\cdots=x_n=0$ annihilates $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ but leaves the nonzero polynomial $x_{n+1}$. Hence $x_{n+1}$ is not in that ideal, and each inclusion in the ascending chain is strict. The chain therefore never stabilizes, contradicting the ascending-chain consequence of Noetherianity. A finite iteration of Hilbert's theorem cannot justify an infinite-variable conclusion.
</details>
