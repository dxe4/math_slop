01 / Prepare the objects

Three checks before computing

Try these without revealing the repair. Each check identifies a prerequisite used in the workshop.

Words: does abAB freely reduce to the empty word?

No adjacent inverse pair occurs, so it is a nonempty reduced word in the free group on a,b. Here A means a⁻¹ and B means b⁻¹. Nevertheless its signed counts of a and b are both zero. Review free reduction if these two operations look interchangeable.

Coefficients: can 3u=1 be solved over ℤ, ℚ and 𝔽₃?

There is no integer solution; over ℚ, u=⅓; in 𝔽₃, the left side is zero for every u and cannot equal 1. A field allows division by its nonzero elements. The integer 3 becomes zero in 𝔽₃.

Quotients: if d₂(x,y)=(3y,0), when do two 1-chains represent the same class?

Here d₁=0, so all 1-chains are cycles. Two cycles represent the same homology class precisely when their difference belongs to im(d₂). Over ℤ this requires equal b-coefficients and a-coefficients differing by a multiple of 3. The quotient identifies cycles with that difference; it does not identify all cycles.

Use the local homology chapter for kernels, images and quotient groups, and cell complexes for the construction. The disk-attachment theorem for π₁ and the cellular homology theorem are prerequisites here, rather than consequences proved by the displayed matrices.

02 / Specify a space

The same graph, two attaching maps

Start with one vertex v and two oriented circle edges a,b meeting there. Attach one closed disk σ by a continuous map from its boundary circle into this graph. Subdivide that boundary into arcs and traverse the indicated oriented edge on each arc. Uppercase letters reverse the edge. All arc endpoints map to v.

T: attach along abAB = aba⁻¹b⁻¹.
X: attach along aabAAB = a²ba⁻²b⁻¹.

The schematic shows X’s attaching word. The controls below change algebraic records, rather than turning this picture into a surface model. These are finite CW complexes with one vertex, two one-cells and the declared two-cells. They are not one-vertex simplicial complexes. X need not be a surface.

Why a column contains signed counts

For the a-entry of d₂σ, collapse the b-circle to the vertex. The attaching map becomes a map S¹→S¹. Its degree is the number of forward a-traversals minus reverse traversals. The same construction gives the b-entry. This is the two-dimensional case of the cellular boundary formula. For both displayed words those degrees are zero, so d₂σ=0a+0b.

Each one-cell begins and ends at v, so d₁a=d₁b=v−v=0. No cells occur in dimensions three or higher. The cellular homology theorem identifies the homology of this chain complex with the space’s ordinary homology. The theorem supplies the topological meaning; a rank calculation alone does not prove it.

For both T and X: 0 → ℤσ —d₂=0→ ℤa⊕ℤb —d₁=0→ ℤv → 0.
H₀=ℤ, H₁=ℤ², H₂=ℤ; Hₙ=0 for n≥3.

The zero 2-boundary means σ is a cellular 2-cycle. It does not, by itself, realize σ as the image of a sphere. Likewise the commutator’s associated 1-chain is already zero in the graph, even though its loop is not null-homotopic there.

04 / Recover a missing question

A finite nonabelian quotient separates the spaces

The disk-attachment theorem says that attaching a two-cell adds its word as a relation in the fundamental group. More precisely, it quotients the free group by the normal subgroup generated by that word, including its conjugates.

π₁(T)=⟨a,b | ab=ba⟩ ≅ ℤ².
π₁(X)=⟨a,b | a²b=ba²⟩.

To show these groups differ, send a to the transposition (12) and b to (23) in S₃. Since (12)²=e, the relation a²b=ba² holds. The assignment therefore induces a homomorphism from π₁(X). Its images do not commute: with permutations acting on the rightmost input first, ab sends 1 to 2, while ba sends 1 to 3. The two transpositions generate all of S₃, so this homomorphism is onto a nonabelian group.

The conclusion and the information it uses

A homomorphic image of an abelian group is abelian. Consequently π₁(X) is nonabelian, whereas π₁(T) is abelian. Homotopy equivalent path-connected spaces have isomorphic fundamental groups, so X and T are not homotopy equivalent despite their identical integral homology groups in every degree.

This argument exhibits a quotient; it does not identify π₁(X) with S₃, and it does not prove a²=1 in π₁(X). Only the chosen image of a has order two. Passing from the attaching word to its signed counts erased the order information needed to impose the relation on arbitrary group elements.

The fixed permutation test in the committed case

a=(12), b=(23). The X relation evaluates to the identity, so the assignment descends to π₁(X) and gives its nonabelian quotient.

The workbench checks this one assignment against the declared relators. Failure of this assignment alone does not classify a group or exclude other quotients.

For any path-connected space, the theorem H₁(−;ℤ)≅π₁(−)ab explains the relationship: integral first homology is the abelianization, which imposes commutation. It is not generally the entire fundamental group.

05 / Change a relation and a coefficient system

Three times a chain can bound while the chain does not

Add τ along a³ to either original space. In ordered cell bases (σ,τ) and (a,b),

d₂(xσ+yτ)=3ya.
H₁(−;ℤ)=(ℤ/3)[a]⊕ℤ[b],   H₂(−;ℤ)=ℤ[σ].

All 1-chains are cycles. Integer boundaries are exactly the multiples of 3a, so [a] is nonzero with order exactly three. The equation 3y=0 over ℤ forces y=0, leaving precisely the integer multiples of σ as 2-cycles. There is no d₃ image to quotient out.

Over ℚ, y=⅓ makes a a boundary; H₁ has basis [b] and H₂ has basis [σ]. Over 𝔽₂, 3=1 and the same dimensions hold, with d₂τ=a. Over 𝔽₃, d₂ is the zero map: H₁ and H₂ both have dimension two.

An extra cycle appears. The mod-3 class τ comes from an actual chain: d₂τ=3a is nonzero integrally but becomes zero modulo three. Simply tensoring H₂(−;ℤ)=ℤ with 𝔽₃ would give one dimension and miss this second class. Reduce the chain complex and recompute its kernel and image.

The changed fundamental groups happen to agree

For T plus τ the presentation is ⟨a,b | ab=ba, a³=1⟩, giving ℤ/3×ℤ. For X plus τ, a² commutes with b, so a⁴ also commutes with b. Since a³=1 implies a⁴=a, it follows that a and b commute. The resulting presentation is the same. Sending a,b to the standard generators of ℤ/3×ℤ and back gives inverse homomorphisms, so there are no additional relations and a has order exactly three.

The old S₃ assignment fails a³=1, because (12)³=(12). Its earlier quotient argument therefore cannot be carried over. Matching the changed fundamental groups and homology groups still does not, by itself, prove a homotopy equivalence between the spaces.

06 / Independent work

Change the word, the chain or the coefficients

Write an attempt before opening a hint. Written reasoning is not automatically proof-checked. The final two tasks change the attachments beyond the workbench’s presets.

1. Count signed traversals

Find the integer cellular boundary columns for aabAAB and aabAB. Explain why counting the total number of a-arcs gives the wrong answer. Which theorem turns signed counts into a cellular boundary?

Hint

Collapsing the other edge gives a circle map. Reverse traversal has degree −1.

Worked explanation

The columns are (0,0) and (1,0): the first has two a and two A; the second has two a and one A. Each has one b and one B. The cellular boundary formula uses the degrees after collapsing the other edge. The total number of traversals discards orientation and cannot compute these signed degrees.

2. A zero chain does not contract a loop

In the graph before any disk is attached, compare the loop abAB, its free reduction and its associated cellular 1-chain. Then compute H₂(T;ℤ) from the cells. Does its generator alone establish a sphere mapping onto that generator?

Hint

The graph has free fundamental group; cellular chains commute. A sphere map is additional topological data.

Worked explanation

abAB is a nonempty reduced word in the graph’s free group, so the loop is not null-homotopic there. Its associated 1-chain is a+b−a−b=0. After attachment in T, d₂=0 and C₃=0 give H₂(T;ℤ)=ℤ[σ]. This calculation provides a cellular homology class, not a map S²→T. One must not turn an arbitrary 2-cycle into a sphere without an additional argument.

3. Exhibit the difference

In X plus τ along a³, compare z=a+b, z′=4a+b and z″=2a+b. Which pairs z,z′ and z,z″ have the same class over ℤ, ℚ, 𝔽₂ and 𝔽₃? Give a filling chain or a precise obstruction.

Hint

The two differences are 3a and a. Solve 3y=3 or 3y=1 in the chosen coefficients.

Worked explanation

z′−z=3a=d₂τ over every listed coefficient system; in 𝔽₃ that difference is already zero as a chain and zero is also a filling. Thus z and z′ have the same class throughout. For z″−z=a there is no integer filling, since 3y=1 has no integer solution. Over ℚ use τ/3. Over 𝔽₂ use τ because 3=1. Over 𝔽₃ d₂ is zero and a is a nonzero chain, so there is no filling.

4. Test a changed assignment

Replace the permutation assignment by a↦(123), b↦(12). Does it respect X’s relation? Contrast the result with a↦(12), b↦(23). Explain what a successful nonabelian quotient proves and what a failed assignment leaves undecided.

Hint

For the changed assignment, compare a²b(1) and ba²(1), applying the rightmost permutation first.

Worked explanation

For a=(123), b=(12), a²b sends 1 first to 2 and then to 1, while ba² sends 1 first to 3 and then to 3. Thus the images of a² and b do not commute and the relation fails. In the original assignment a²=e, so the relation holds and the noncommuting transpositions generate S₃. That proves π₁(X) has a nonabelian quotient and is nonabelian. A failed assignment only rejects that proposed homomorphism; it does not show all nonabelian quotients are impossible.

5. Attach a² instead

Change X’s second attachment from a³ to a². Compute H₁ and H₂ over ℤ, 𝔽₂ and 𝔽₃. Does the proof that the fundamental group becomes abelian still work? Give a group presentation and test the original S₃ assignment.

Hint

The second column is (2,0). The relation [a²,b]=1 follows automatically from a²=1.

Worked explanation

Integrally d₂(x,y)=(2y,0), giving H₁=ℤ/2⊕ℤ and H₂=ℤ[σ]. Over 𝔽₂ the matrix is zero, so both H₁ and H₂ have dimension two. Over 𝔽₃, 2 is invertible, so each has dimension one. The group presentation reduces to ⟨a,b | a²=1⟩, namely ℤ/2∗ℤ. The original assignment a=(12), b=(23) respects both relations and has nonabelian image, so the group is not abelian. The a³ argument used a⁴=a; it does not apply under the changed relation a²=1.

6. Change the direction of the boundary

Instead attach τ along a³b³ to X. Find H₁(−;ℤ), the order of [a+b], and the order of [a−b]. Compute H₂ over ℤ and 𝔽₃. Explain why this problem does not require a classification of the new fundamental group.

Hint

Use the integer basis u=a+b, v=b. The boundary is 3u, while a−b=u−2v.

Worked explanation

The new column is (3,3), so d₂(xσ+yτ)=3y(a+b). The pairs (u,v)=(a+b,b) form an integer basis, with a=u−v. Hence H₁=ℤ/3[u]⊕ℤ[v]. The class [a+b] has order three. The class [a−b]=[u]−2[v] has infinite order because of its nonzero free component. Over ℤ, 3y=0 forces y=0, giving H₂=ℤ[σ]. Over 𝔽₃ the entire matrix vanishes, giving H₂=𝔽₃² with basis σ,τ. These conclusions follow from the declared cellular chain complex and the cellular homology theorem; no full π₁ calculation is needed.

Return later: say what the certificate certifies

Without the page, explain why the original spaces have identical homology but different fundamental groups. Then reconstruct why τ becomes a new 2-cycle modulo three. Name the exact theorem that connects each calculation to the space.
