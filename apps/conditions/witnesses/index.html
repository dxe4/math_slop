have a witness?

An equation in the visible parameters can be true even when no hidden variable solves the original system. Follow three different answers all the way back to an actual common root.

Preparation: polynomial algebra, substitution, and “there exists.” Determinants and ideals appear in optional explanations. The experiment uses exact rational numbers; the written classification works over both ℝ and ℂ. The general closure theorem later in the workshop requires an algebraically closed field.

The original question

Keep the hidden variable in view

For visible parameters a and b, ask whether there exists u such that both of the following polynomials vanish.

f=au2+u+b=0g=au+1=0

A witness is one value of u that makes both equalities true. An equation involving only a and b is a condition on parameters; its solutions do not automatically come with such a witness.

Predict: at a=0 and b=0, a symbolic elimination calculation returns b=0. Can any u solve the original system? Which original equation decides?

The generic degree-(2,1) Sylvester determinant specializes to 0. Its degrees have dropped; that zero is not a common-root certificate for the specialized pair.

A complete classification

One identity recovers the condition

f−ug=b.

If f=g=0, this identity forces b=0. If a=0, then g=1, so no u works. If b=0 and a≠0, the equation g=0 forces u=−1/a, and the identity gives f=ug+b=0. Thus a witness exists exactly when b=0 and a≠0, and it is unique.

This proves necessity and sufficiency for every real or complex parameter pair. Testing many rational pairs would not prove that classification.

Optional: verify the determinant without a computer algebra system

Using the generic degrees 2 and 1, the Sylvester matrix has determinant

|a1ba100a1|=a−a+a2b=a2b.

At a=0 the actual polynomials are u+b and 1. They have no common root, and their actual-degree resultant is 1. Specializing the generic determinant keeps the old degree layout and gives zero. The factor a² records a degeneracy of that layout; it does not count two affine witnesses.

Optional: why is the elimination ideal exactly (b)?

Work in k[a,b,u], with k=ℝ or ℂ. The identity above gives (f,g)=(b,au+1). In particular b belongs to the elimination ideal (f,g)∩k[a,b]. Conversely, if h(a,b) is in that ideal, substitution at b=0 and u=−1/a gives h(a,0)=0 for every nonzero a. A polynomial in a with infinitely many roots is zero, so h(a,0) is identically zero and b divides h. Therefore the elimination ideal is (b).

The exact projection is the punctured line b=0, a≠0. Its Zariski closure is the entire line b=0: any polynomial vanishing on the punctured line also vanishes at its origin by the same one-variable argument. “Closure” here means the smallest common zero set of polynomials containing the projection; it is not a claim that the missing witness has been recovered.

What does a theorem or tool promise?

Elimination and extension have hypotheses

Over an algebraically closed field, the zero set of the elimination ideal is the Zariski closure of the projection of the original algebraic zero set. It can contain parameter points that do not extend to full solutions. Over ℝ, polynomial equations alone can also miss inequalities; Task 6 makes that difference explicit.

The extension theorem gives a useful sufficient condition: after eliminating one variable from an ideal generated by specified polynomials, a solution of the elimination ideal extends if at least one of the generators’ leading coefficients in that eliminated variable is nonzero there, over an algebraically closed field. If all those leading coefficients vanish, this sufficient test gives no conclusion. It does not say extension is impossible.

In our presentation f and g have leading coefficient a in u. At a≠0 the test applies over ℂ. At a=0 it is silent; the direct equation g=1 supplies the actual impossibility proof. The elementary identity already proves this example over ℝ as well.

Read the question encoded in the command

Resultant[f,g,u] computes a polynomial from the generic inputs. With equations-only input, Wolfram Language’s Eliminate documentation describes the Zariski closure of a projection. Inputs that also contain inequations have different documented guarantees. To state actual existence, encode Exists[u, f==0 && g==0] and explicitly choose a domain, for example with Resolve[..., Reals].

Independent written practice

Explain what each answer guarantees

Write an attempt before opening a hint or worked answer. Your notes are not proof-checked. Task 6 changes the system and the mechanism; it is deliberately outside the experiment’s controls.

1. Classify three parameter pairs

For (a,b)=(0,1), (0,0), and (2,0), decide separately whether the resultant is zero, whether the elimination equation holds, and whether an actual common root exists. Supply a witness whenever possible. Explain why the three tests need not agree.

Hint

Check a²b=0, then b=0, then the original equation au+1=0.

Worked answer

At (0,1), the resultant is zero, but b=0 fails and g=1 prevents every witness. At (0,0), both polynomial tests pass, but g=1 still prevents a witness. At (2,0), all three tests pass; u=−1/2 makes f=2(1/4)−1/2=0 and g=2(−1/2)+1=0. Each later test retains information that the preceding candidate set lacks.

2. A good parameter pair, a bad proposed witness

At a=−3/2 and b=0, test u=0 directly. Then derive the unique correct witness from the equations. Prove the complete “if and only if” condition for this family without testing more parameter pairs.

Hint

Use f−ug=b for necessity. When a≠0, solve g=0 and substitute into that identity for sufficiency.

Worked answer

With u=0, f=0 but g=1, so this proposal fails despite the existence of a common root. Solving (−3/2)u+1=0 gives u=2/3, and f=(−3/2)(4/9)+2/3=0. In general any common root forces b=f−ug=0 and a≠0 because g would otherwise be 1. Conversely b=0 and a≠0 allow u=−1/a, giving g=0 and f=ug+b=0. The linear equation g=0 also proves uniqueness.

3. Does the squared factor count solutions?

At a=0 and b=7, compare the generic Sylvester determinant after substitution with the resultant formed from the actual specialized polynomials. A classmate says the factor a² means there are two common roots when a=0. Refute the claim using the original equations.

Hint

Write both specialized polynomials before assigning their degrees. One is a nonzero constant.

Worked answer

The generic determinant a²b specializes to zero. But the actual pair is f=u+7 of degree 1 and g=1 of degree 0. Its resultant is 1 and there are no common roots in either ℝ or ℂ. The old degree-(2,1) determinant has degenerated on specialization. Its factor a² is not a count of affine solutions of this specialized system.

4. Why equations include the origin

The exact projection is P={(a,0):a≠0}. Explain why every polynomial h(a,b) vanishing on P also vanishes at (0,0). What additional condition distinguishes P from its polynomial zero-set closure? Does failure of a sufficient extension test alone prove a point has no witness?

Hint

Restrict h to b=0 and use infinitely many roots. Separate “the theorem guarantees” from “the theorem does not decide.”

Worked answer

The one-variable polynomial h(a,0) vanishes for every nonzero a, so it is identically zero and also vanishes at a=0. Thus polynomial equalities holding on P cannot exclude the origin; its Zariski closure is the whole line b=0. The inequation a≠0 restores the exact projection. A sufficient extension condition failing is inconclusive. Here nonexistence at the origin follows separately from g=1, not from reversing the extension theorem.

5. Ask the tool the full question

Write three distinct mathematical requests: the generic resultant, the zero set described by equations-only elimination, and actual existence of u over ℝ. Predict the answer to each for this family. Which distinction must accompany a saved computer algebra result? Does changing to ℂ change this particular family’s exact projection?

Hint

An existential question includes the hidden variable and a domain. A command name alone does not specify the meaning of its output.

Worked answer

The generic resultant is a²b, whose vanishing admits either axis. The elimination ideal is (b), so its polynomial zero set is b=0. Actual existence asks ∃u∈ℝ with f=g=0 and has the answer b=0 and a≠0. Record whether an output is a generic polynomial, a closure described by equations, or an exact existential condition, together with its domain and input assumptions. Over ℂ the same direct identity and witness −1/a give the same condition for this family. This agreement is specific; it does not license dropping domains in general. These predictions come from the written argument, not an executed Wolfram session.

6. Changed system: real witnesses require inequalities

With visible real parameters x,y, consider u²+v²=x and uv=y. Determine exactly which (x,y) admit real witnesses u,v, and prove both directions. Then allow all four variables to be complex. Does every (x,y)∈ℂ² have a witness? Finally test (x,y)=(1,1) in each domain. This task has no control or preset in the experiment.

Hint

Compute (u+v)² and (u−v)². Conversely, choose values for u+v and u−v first, then use their half-sum and half-difference.

Worked answer

For real witnesses, (u+v)²=x+2y≥0 and (u−v)²=x−2y≥0. Together these give x≥2|y|. Conversely if that inequality holds, take real s=√(x+2y) and t=√(x−2y), then set u=(s+t)/2 and v=(s−t)/2. Direct calculation gives u²+v²=(s²+t²)/2=x and uv=(s²−t²)/4=y, proving sufficiency.

Over ℂ, both x+2y and x−2y always have square roots. The same construction works for every complex parameter pair. At (1,1) the real inequality fails, so there are no real witnesses. Complex witnesses are u=(√3+i)/2 and v=(√3−i)/2. Their product is 1 and their squared sum is 1. In this changed system the exact real projection needs inequalities; polynomial equations alone cannot express its restriction.

To prove that last assertion, the cone x≥2|y| contains the open rectangle 2<x<3, |y|<1/2. If a polynomial h(x,y) vanishes on the cone, then for each fixed y in that interval, h has infinitely many x-roots and is the zero polynomial in x. Each of its coefficient polynomials in y therefore vanishes throughout an interval and is itself zero. Thus h is identically zero. Every polynomial equality holding on the cone also holds on all of ℝ², which includes points outside this cone.
