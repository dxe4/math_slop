Predict · Bound · Separate the limits

What survives when the damping disappears?

One integral has a simple formula. That formula does not, by itself, justify differentiation, removal of damping, or a joint limit. Give each step its own proof.

Preparation: improper integrals, differentiation, integration by parts, and the definition of a limit. All parameters are real. The original damped integral has s>0; the undamped boundary is introduced separately. The exact experiment checks rational error budgets, not numerical quadrature or written proofs.

One formula, different questions

Which operation needs which hypothesis?

For a∈ℝ and s>0, define

F(a,s)=∫0∞e−sxsin(ax)xdx=arctan(as).

The value at x=0 is a by continuous extension. Here arctan is the real inverse tangent with values between −π/2 and π/2. We will also prove the ordinary improper integral J(a)=∫₀∞sin(ax)/x dx exists, with J(0)=0 and J(a)=π sign(a)/2 for a≠0.

1. Differentiate

Hold a positive s fixed. Control a difference quotient under the integral.

2. Remove damping

Hold a≠0 fixed. Bound the infinite tails uniformly in s≥0.

3. Approach the origin

Let both a and s vary. Compare paths and the order of limits.

4. Try the formal derivative

At s=0, ask whether the ordinary cosine integral exists.

Predict: if the formula for F is correct for every s>0, can you put s=0 into it, differentiate the undamped integral, and take (a,s)→(0,0) without further work?

Proof obligation 1

Fix positive damping before differentiating

For each fixed s>0, the integral is absolutely convergent: near zero, |sin(ax)|/x≤|a|; for x≥1, e⁻ˢˣ|sin(ax)|/x≤e⁻ˢˣ. Extend the integrand to x=0 with value a. These bounds handle the two endpoints separately.

For real u,v, the fundamental theorem gives sin(u+v)−sin u−v cos u=∫₀ᵛ(cos(u+r)−cos u)dr. The bound |cos(u+r)−cos u|≤|r| makes the absolute remainder at most v²/2 for either sign of v. Set u=ax and v=hx, then divide by |h|x for x>0. For every real h≠0, this gives

|e−sx[sin((a+h)x)−sin(ax)]hx−e−sxcos(ax)|≤|h|2xe−sx.

The right side integrates to |h|/(2s²). Thus the integrated difference quotient tends to ∫₀∞e⁻ˢˣcos(ax) dx as h→0. This is an explicit certificate for interchanging differentiation and integration at fixed s, not an appeal to the final formula.

∂F∂a(a,s)=∫0∞e−sxcos(ax)dx=ss2+a2.

The last integral follows by evaluating the elementary antiderivative e⁻ˢˣ(−s cos(ax)+a sin(ax))/(s²+a²) at zero and infinity. Now use F(0,s)=0 for s>0 and integrate the derivative from 0 to a. This gives F(a,s)=arctan(a/s). The factor 1/s² in the error bound warns us that this proof has not given a uniform estimate as s→0.

Proof obligation 2

Control the tails before removing damping

Fix a≠0. For s≥0, qₛ(x)=e⁻ˢˣ/x is positive, decreasing, and tends to zero as x→∞. Integration by parts using the primitive −cos(ax)/a gives, for every R>0,

|∫R∞e−sxsin(ax)xdx|≤2e−sR|a|R≤2|a|R.

Indeed the finite-interval boundary terms and ∫|qₛ′| sum to at most 2qₛ(R)/|a|. The same argument with s=0 first proves that the ordinary integral J(a) exists and bounds its tail. Uniformity here is in s≥0 for a fixed nonzero a; the denominator prevents a uniform claim near a=0.

On the finite window [0,R], use 0≤1−e⁻ˢˣ≤sx and |sin(ax)|≤1. The difference between damped and undamped window integrals is at most sR. Combine one finite-window bound and two tail bounds:

|F(a,s)−J(a)|≤sR+4|a|R.

Given ε>0, first choose R>8/(|a|ε), then choose 0<s<ε/(2R). The total is strictly below ε. Therefore F(a,s)→J(a) as s↓0. Taking that limit in the already justified arctangent formula identifies J(a)=π sign(a)/2. At a=0 the integrand is identically zero, so J(0)=0 directly. A single fixed R supplies a finite error certificate; it is not by itself a proof that the error tends to zero.

Why is the undamped nonzero-frequency integral only conditionally convergent?

Put y=|a|x in its absolute integral. On [nπ,(n+1)π], the integral of |sin y| is 2 and 1/y≥1/((n+1)π), except for the harmless endpoint y=0. Thus each interval contributes at least 2/((n+1)π) to ∫|sin y|/y dy. The harmonic series diverges. For a≠0, J converges but its absolute integral does not. For a=0 the zero integral is absolutely convergent.

Proof obligation 3

Pointwise boundary values do not give a joint limit

Let t>0 tend to zero. Along a=0,s=t, the value of F is 0. Along a=t,s=t, it is arctan(1)=π/4. Both paths approach (0,0) through positive damping, so F has no joint limit there.

These are schematic paths in parameter space, not sampled plots of the integral. Their captions are exact identities for every t>0.

The third path exposes a different failure. At a=t²,s=t, F=arctan(t)→0 while J(t²)=π/2. Hence |F(t²,t)−J(t²)|→π/2. The convergence F(a,s)→J(a) is not uniform on any neighborhood of a=0, even though it holds at each fixed a. The nested limits also disagree: first a→0 then s↓0 gives 0; first s↓0 then a↓0 through positive a gives π/2.

Proof obligation 4

The undamped formal derivative is not an ordinary integral

Differentiating sin(ax)/x pointwise suggests cos(ax). But for a≠0, its cutoff integral is

∫0Rcos(ax)dx=sin(aR)a.

Writing b=|a|, the sequences Rₙ=(π/2+2πn)/b and Tₙ=(3π/2+2πn)/b give cutoff values 1/b and −1/b. There is no ordinary limit. At a=0 the cutoff is R and diverges as well.

For a fixed a≠0, the damped derivative s/(s²+a²) tends to zero as s↓0. That is an Abel-regularized value of the divergent cosine integral, not its ordinary improper value. Separately, J is locally constant on each side of zero and has derivative 0 there. Its derivative agreeing with that Abel value does not validate the divergent ordinary integral. At a=0, J is discontinuous and the damped derivative 1/s diverges.

Independent written practice

Rebuild the needed justification

Write before revealing a hint or answer. These notes are not proof-checked. Task 6 changes the integrand and requires fresh bounds; it has no control or preset in the experiment.

1. Two endpoints, two convergence claims

Find the removable value of e⁻ˢˣsin(ax)/x at x=0. Prove absolute convergence for s>0, including negative a. What changes at s=0 when a≠0? Treat a=0 separately.

Hint

Near zero use |sin(ax)|≤|a|x. At infinity distinguish exponential decay from oscillatory cancellation.

Worked answer

The removable value is a, since sin(ax)/x→a and e⁻ˢˣ→1. The bound |a| controls a finite interval near zero; on x≥1 the absolute integrand is at most e⁻ˢˣ, integrable for s>0. These arguments use |a| and allow either sign. For s=0,a≠0 the tail integration-by-parts estimate proves convergence by cancellation, while ∫|sin y|/y diverges by the harmonic-interval argument. The undamped integral is conditional. At a=0 it is identically zero and absolutely convergent for every s≥0.

2. A step size is part of the certificate

At fixed s=1/100, require the integrated difference quotient to differ from the derivative by strictly less than 1/100. Give a sufficient strict inequality on |h| and one nonzero rational step satisfying it. Explain why this proof does not justify setting s=0.

Hint

Substitute s into |h|/(2s²). Keep the strict inequality and distinguish the frequency a from the differentiation step h.

Worked answer

The bound is 5000|h|, so |h|<1/500000 suffices. For example h=1/1000000 gives the bound 1/200, strictly less than 1/100. For each positive fixed s the bound tends to zero as h→0. Its coefficient 1/(2s²) blows up as s↓0, and the undamped cosine integral in fact diverges. This estimate supplies no undamped differentiation theorem.

3. A finite budget and a limiting argument

Use a=1, s=1/100000 and R=100 to certify |F−J|<1/20. Then give an ε-argument proving F(a,s)→J(a) for an arbitrary fixed a≠0. If your chosen upper bound exceeds ε, what can you conclude about the actual error?

Hint

Add sR and 4/(|a|R). For the limit, choose the tail cutoff before choosing how small the damping must be.

Worked answer

The window bound is 1/1000 and the two-tail bound is 1/25, totaling 41/1000<1/20. For general ε>0 first take R>8/(|a|ε), so the two tails contribute less than ε/2; then take 0<s<ε/(2R), so the window contributes less than ε/2. This proves the limit for fixed nonzero a. An upper bound larger than ε merely fails to supply this certificate. It is not a lower bound and cannot show the actual error exceeds ε or that convergence fails.

4. Pointwise, uniform, or joint?

Compute F on the paths (a,s)=(0,t), (t,t), and (t²,t) for t>0. Use two paths to settle the joint limit at the origin. Use the remaining comparison with J to decide uniform convergence on a neighborhood of a=0.

Hint

Divide a by s only where s=t>0. On the parabolic path, a remains positive even as it approaches zero.

Worked answer

The three exact values are 0, π/4 and arctan(t). The first two approach different values at the same parameter-space origin, so the joint limit does not exist. On the third path J(t²)=π/2 while F(t²,t)→0. Every neighborhood of a=0 contains t² for all sufficiently small positive t, and the error approaches π/2 rather than zero. Thus the fixed-a convergence is not uniform on such a neighborhood.

5. Same number, different justification

At a=2, show the ordinary integral ∫₀∞cos(2x) dx does not exist. Compare the derivative of J at a=2 with the limit as s↓0 of the damped derivative. Why does their agreement not prove the formal interchange?

Hint

The cutoff integral is sin(2R)/2. Find two sequences of cutoffs giving different fixed values.

Worked answer

At Rₙ=π/4+πn the cutoff is 1/2; at Tₙ=3π/4+πn it is −1/2. Therefore the ordinary improper cosine integral does not converge. J is constant π/2 near a=2 and has derivative 0. The damped derivative s/(s²+4) also tends to 0, an Abel value. This equality of two correctly obtained numbers does not make the third, divergent ordinary integral exist.

6. Changed integrand: continuity survives, differentiation fails

For real a and s≥0, define G(a,s)=∫₀∞e⁻ˢˣ(1−cos(ax))/x² dx. Derive a fresh integrable bound, prove G is jointly continuous at (0,0), and evaluate G(a,0) by integration by parts. Is a↦G(a,0) differentiable at zero? Compare with the pointwise derivative of its integrand there. This problem is outside all controls.

Hint

Use 0≤1−cos y≤min(y²/2,2). For a≠0 split the bound at x=2/|a|. In integration by parts, check the boundary term at both zero and infinity.

Worked answer

For s≥0 the nonnegative integrand is at most min(a²/2,2/x²), which is integrable. At a=0 it is zero. For a≠0, split at 2/|a|: the constant-bound part integrates to |a| and the tail-bound part to |a|. Hence 0≤G(a,s)≤2|a|. This proves absolute convergence even without damping and proves joint continuity at the origin, uniformly over all s≥0.

At s=0 integration by parts on [η,R] gives the boundary term −(1−cos(ax))/x and the remaining integral a∫ηᴿsin(ax)/x dx. The boundary term tends to zero at infinity because the numerator is bounded, and at zero because 1−cos(ax)≤a²x²/2. Thus G(a,0)=aJ(a)=π|a|/2, including a=0 directly.

The right derivative at zero is π/2 and the left derivative is −π/2, so there is a cusp and no derivative. Yet for every fixed x>0 the integrand’s a-derivative at a=0 is sin(0)/x=0. Absolute convergence and joint continuity do not supply the uniform control needed to differentiate under this undamped integral.
