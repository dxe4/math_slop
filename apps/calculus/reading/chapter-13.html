1. The piece determines the integral

Integration becomes a modeling tool when we stop asking only which antiderivative to use. Begin instead with an object, a quantity to measure, and a partition into pieces. Describe one piece using quantities that are nearly constant there. Add those contributions, then take the limit as the largest piece shrinks. Chapter 10 supplied the meaning of this limit; chapter 11 supplied a way to evaluate it. The new challenge is choosing the contribution correctly.

For the region between continuous curves y=f(x)y=f(x) and y=g(x)y=g(x) on [a,b][a,b], a thin vertical rectangle has width Δx\Delta x and height ∣f(x)−g(x)∣|f(x)-g(x)|. Its area is approximately height times width. Therefore the geometric area is A=∫ab∣f(x)−g(x)∣ dxA=\int_a^b|f(x)-g(x)|\,dx. If f≥gf\ge g throughout, this becomes A=∫ab(f(x)−g(x)) dxA=\int_a^b(f(x)-g(x))\,dx. If the curves cross, find their intersections and split into intervals with a fixed ordering. A signed integral of f−gf-g can cancel positive and negative pieces even though neither piece has zero area.

For example, y=xy=x and y=x2y=x^2 meet at x=0x=0 and x=1x=1. Between those points, x≥x2x\ge x^2, so the enclosed area is ∫01(x−x2) dx=12−13=16\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx=\frac12-\frac13=\frac16 square coordinate units. The inequalities matter as much as the antiderivative. Drawing a representative slice and naming its top and bottom often prevents more mistakes than additional algebra.

Horizontal rectangles give an equally valid description when right and left boundaries are easier to express as functions of yy. Their contributions are [right⁡(y)−left⁡(y)] dy[\operatorname{right}(y)-\operatorname{left}(y)]\,dy. There is no obligation to integrate with respect to xx. Choose the coordinate that describes the whole piece most simply, and split the region whenever its boundary description changes. Physical coordinates measured in metres give an area in square metres because both the height and the width carry a length unit.

A slice formula should include its orientation. For vertical slicing, write ΔA≈[top⁡(x)−bottom⁡(x)]Δx\Delta A\approx[\operatorname{top}(x)-\operatorname{bottom}(x)]\Delta x. For horizontal slicing, write ΔA≈[right⁡(y)−left⁡(y)]Δy\Delta A\approx[\operatorname{right}(y)-\operatorname{left}(y)]\Delta y. These are statements about a representative small rectangle; the integral follows only after the full interval and any switching points are identified. If a curve is given implicitly, horizontal slices may avoid solving two branches as functions of xx. Conversely, a region described by simple top and bottom functions is usually easiest vertically. Choosing the slice is a modeling decision made before antiderivatives enter.

For y=xy=x and y=−xy=-x on [−1,1][-1,1], neither curve stays on top throughout. Their vertical separation is ∣2x∣|2x|. Splitting at x=0x=0 gives area ∫−10(−2x) dx+∫012x dx=1+1=2\int_{-1}^0(-2x)\,dx+\int_0^1 2x\,dx=1+1=2. The signed integral ∫−112x dx=0\int_{-1}^1 2x\,dx=0 describes cancellation, not enclosed area. A diagram can reveal the switch, but the inequality x≥−xx\ge -x exactly when x≥0x\ge0 proves where it occurs. This distinction matters for any two crossing curves, even when their drawn lobes look roughly symmetric.

An intersection point is found by solving f(x)=g(x)f(x)=g(x), but not every algebraic solution lies in the region under study. For y=x2y=x^2 and y=2−xy=2-x, solve x2=2−xx^2=2-x to obtain x=−2x=-2 and x=1x=1. On the interval [−2,1][-2,1], the line lies above the parabola, so the enclosed area is ∫−21[(2−x)−x2] dx=92\int_{-2}^1[(2-x)-x^2]\,dx=\frac92 square units. The sign can be checked at x=0x=0, where the line has height 22 and the parabola height 00. A representative test point is a quick ordering check after solving intersections.

If the axes carry different physical units, the integral still records the product of those units. Integrating a velocity graph over time gives metres rather than square metres, although the drawn region looks like a geometric area on paper. Integrating a force graph over displacement gives joules. The same mathematical rectangle height×width\text{height}\times\text{width} can model different quantities; its units and sign convention determine the interpretation. This is why labeling both axes and a sample slice is part of a correct application solution, not merely a presentational flourish.

2. Volume by cross-sections and shells

A solid can be sliced into thin slabs perpendicular to an axis. If A(x)A(x) is its cross-sectional area, the volume of a slab is approximately A(x)ΔxA(x)\Delta x, so V=∫abA(x) dxV=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx. Continuity of AA is a convenient sufficient hypothesis; piecewise continuous cross-sections can be handled by splitting. This principle applies to square and triangular sections as well as circles. It is the area of the section, not the height of a boundary curve, that belongs in the volume integrand.

Rotating a region about the xx-axis produces disks when the region touches the axis and washers when a hole remains. With outer radius R(x)R(x) and inner radius r(x)r(x), where 0≤r(x)≤R(x)0\le r(x)\le R(x), the section has area π[R(x)2−r(x)2]\pi[R(x)^2-r(x)^2]. A disk is the case r(x)=0r(x)=0. Radii are distances to the rotation axis: about y=cy=c, a boundary at height yy has distance ∣y−c∣|y-c|. If the axis passes through the region, inspect the whole rotated section; subtracting two squared boundary heights blindly can create a fictitious hole.

Cylindrical shells use slices parallel to the axis of revolution. A vertical strip at x≥0x\ge0 rotated about the yy-axis has radius xx, height h(x)h(x), and thickness Δx\Delta x. Unrolling its thin shell suggests volume 2πxh(x)Δx2\pi xh(x)\Delta x, circumference times height times thickness. Over the strip interval [a,b][a,b], the limit is V=2π∫abxh(x) dxV=2\pi\int_a^b xh(x)\,dx. For a shifted axis replace xx with distance to that axis. The usual shell formula assumes these strips sweep distinct radial layers. If a region crosses the axis, opposite strips can sweep overlapping material; separate the geometry before adding volumes.

Disks and shells are different partitions of the same solid, not different answers. For the region under y=x2y=x^2 from x=0x=0 to x=1x=1, rotating about the yy-axis gives shells of height x2x^2. A horizontal washer instead runs from x=yx=\sqrt y to x=1x=1, with radii y\sqrt y and 11. Both approaches give π/2\pi/2 cubic coordinate units. The lab checks this agreement numerically. Every volume contribution has units length squared times length, or circumference times height times thickness: length cubed.

For a solid of revolution, sketch the cross-section at a typical coordinate and label radii from the actual axis. Rotating the region 1≤y≤31\le y\le3, 0≤x≤20\le x\le2 about the xx-axis produces a washer with outer radius 33 and inner radius 11, so V=∫02π(32−12) dx=16πV=\int_0^2\pi(3^2-1^2)\,dx=16\pi. The region never touches the axis, so treating it as a solid disk would overcount the central cylinder. The order outer squared minus inner squared is forced by the cross-sectional area, not by whichever boundary equation appears first in the problem.

A shifted axis can reverse which boundary is farther away. Rotate the region 0≤y≤x0\le y\le x on 0≤x≤10\le x\le1 about y=2y=2. At each xx the outer radius to y=0y=0 is 22, while the inner radius to y=xy=x is 2−x2-x. The washer area is π[4−(2−x)2]=π(4x−x2)\pi[4-(2-x)^2]=\pi(4x-x^2), and the volume is π[2x2−x3/3]01=5π/3\pi[2x^2-x^3/3]_0^1=5\pi/3. Using radii xx and 00, appropriate for rotation about y=0y=0, would answer a different question. A single labeled cross-section prevents this common axis error.

Crossing the axis can make shells overlap. Rotate −1≤x≤1-1\le x\le1, 0≤y≤10\le y\le1 about the yy-axis. The solid is a cylinder of radius and height 11, so V=πV=\pi. If both sides are added as distinct shells, 2π∫−11∣x∣ dx=2π2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}|x|\,dx=2\pi, because each positive radial layer is counted twice. Take one representative radius instead: 2π∫01r dr=π2\pi\int_0^1r\,dr=\pi. Horizontal washers give the independent check π∫01dy=π\pi\int_0^1dy=\pi. The issue is coverage, not a failure of the shell formula on distinct layers.

Overlaps require care. If a region crosses the rotation axis, slices on opposite sides may sweep the same radial layer. Adding their shell volumes would double-count material. A washer description may still work after finding the actual inner and outer radii, or the region may need to be split into nonoverlapping pieces. This is analogous to area between crossing curves: the integral must measure each physical piece once. The shell expression 2π⋅radius⋅height⋅thickness2\pi\cdot\text{radius}\cdot\text{height}\cdot\text{thickness} is a local approximation, not a license to sum overlapping shells as though they were disjoint.

A dimension check distinguishes volume methods from area methods. Washer area is π(R2−r2)\pi(R^2-r^2) for outer and inner radii RR and rr, and has square-length units; multiplying by slice thickness gives cubic length. A shell contribution has circumference times height times thickness, again cubic length. If a proposed volume integrand is merely a radius or a height, it is missing a geometric factor. This check is independent of the final antiderivative and applies even when the rotated shape is too complicated to visualize perfectly. It should accompany a labeled sketch of the slice.

3. Length and the skin of a solid

An area uses vertical height, but a curve length follows the curve. Join nearby points (x,f(x))(x,f(x)) by straight segments. A segment has length (Δx)2+(Δy)2=1+(Δy/Δx)2 Δx\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}=\sqrt{1+(\Delta y/\Delta x)^2}\,\Delta x for positive Δx\Delta x. For a continuously differentiable function on [a,b][a,b], the polygonal lengths converge to ∫ab1+(f′(x))2 dx\int_a^b\sqrt{1+(f^{\prime}(x))^2}\,dx. This is the arc-length formula. Piecewise continuously differentiable curves can be split at finitely many corners and their lengths added.

The expression ds=1+(f′(x))2 dxds=\sqrt{1+(f^{\prime}(x))^2}\,dx is a useful shorthand for the length element. It is not simply dydy: a horizontal segment still has positive length. When xx and yy are physical lengths in the same unit, the slope is dimensionless, making the square root dimensionless and dsds a length. A graph whose axes represent time and temperature does not acquire a physically meaningful length merely by applying this formula; a metric or scaling must first be chosen.

Rotating a short curve segment about the xx-axis creates approximately a narrow conical band. Its area is circumference times slant length, approximately 2π∣f(x)∣ ds2\pi|f(x)|\,ds. For a nonnegative continuously differentiable graph, the resulting lateral surface area is S=2π∫abf(x)1+(f′(x))2 dxS=2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+(f^{\prime}(x))^2}\,dx. A general distance to the axis replaces f(x)f(x) when appropriate. This measures the swept skin only; closing disks at the endpoints are extra surfaces. A curve that touches the axis at an endpoint can still satisfy the formula, since the radius there is zero.

As a check, rotating y=xy=x for 0≤x≤10\le x\le1 around the xx-axis gives a cone with radius 11 and slant height 2\sqrt2. The formula gives 2π2∫01x dx=π22\pi\sqrt2\int_0^1x\,dx=\pi\sqrt2, matching the elementary lateral-area formula. Adding its base would give π2+π\pi\sqrt2+\pi. If a derivative becomes unbounded at an endpoint, the continuously differentiable hypothesis no longer applies directly. The integral may nevertheless converge as an improper integral, a subject for chapter 14; simply inserting an infinite endpoint value into software is not a solution.

Here is the secant-to-integral step behind the formula. On each partition interval [xi,xi+1][x_i,x_{i+1}], the mean value theorem gives Δyi/Δxi=f′(ξi)\Delta y_i/\Delta x_i=f'(\xi_i) for some ξi\xi_i in that interval. Thus the polygonal length is exactly ∑i1+f′(ξi)2 Δxi\sum_i\sqrt{1+f'(\xi_i)^2}\,\Delta x_i. If f∈C1[a,b]f\in C^1[a,b], then f′f' is uniformly continuous on that closed interval (Chapter 10 Lesson 3); consequently the continuous function q(x)=1+f′(x)2q(x)=\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2} is uniformly continuous and Riemann integrable. Its Riemann sums, including these choices of ξi\xi_i, approach ∫abq(x) dx\int_a^bq(x)\,dx as the maximum panel width tends to zero. This explains the convergence for arbitrary fine partitions, not just a chosen sequence of equal chords. For a straight line y=mx+cy=mx+c, it reduces to (b−a)1+m2(b-a)\sqrt{1+m^2}.

For y=x2y=x^2 on [0,1][0,1], length is L=∫011+4x2 dxL=\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4x^2}\,dx. It is greater than 11 because the curve has at least the horizontal displacement 11, and less than 5\sqrt5 because the integrand is at most 5\sqrt5. A closed form exists but is less informative than these first bounds for checking a numerical value. The endpoint chord has length 2\sqrt2, so in fact L≥2L\ge\sqrt2. These comparisons catch a result below the straight-line distance without any advanced integration technique.

Surface area requires the slant length of a strip, not its horizontal width. For y=xy=x on [0,1][0,1], the circumference at xx is 2πx2\pi x and ds=2 dxds=\sqrt2\,dx, giving S=π2S=\pi\sqrt2. If dxdx were used instead, the result would be π\pi, too small by the slope factor. At x=0x=0 the circumference vanishes, but that single endpoint contributes no finite area; the integral remains valid. End caps are separate planar areas and should be included only when the question asks for a closed surface rather than the lateral skin.

A graph in time-temperature coordinates illustrates a modeling limit of geometric formulas. Numerical arc length of the plotted trace changes if temperature is measured in Celsius instead of Fahrenheit, or if hours are changed to seconds, because the vertical and horizontal scales are not comparable physical lengths. The formula still computes a length in the chosen coordinate plane, but its physical meaning depends on a specified metric or scale. In a spatial plane where both axes use metres, the ordinary Euclidean distance is natural. Always ask what notion of distance the application intends.

4. Work adds force times displacement

A constant force parallel to motion does work FdFd. If the force varies along a straight path, divide the displacement into short intervals. The force is approximately constant on each, giving a contribution F(x)ΔxF(x)\Delta x and work W=∫abF(x) dxW=\int_a^b F(x)\,dx. Here FF is the signed component along the chosen positive direction. A resisting force does negative work on the moving object. An external agent moving the object slowly may do positive work of the same magnitude. Specify whose force is being integrated before assigning a sign.

For an ideal spring, the restoring force is −kx-kx when xx measures extension from equilibrium. To stretch it slowly from extension aa to bb, an external agent supplies approximately +kx+kx, so its work is Wext=∫abkx dx=k(b2−a2)/2W_{\mathrm{ext}}=\int_a^b kx\,dx=k(b^2-a^2)/2. This assumes the spring remains in its linear range and ignores dissipative losses. With kk in newtons per metre, xx and dxdx in metres, the result is newton metres, called joules. The final force multiplied by the total displacement is generally too large because it applies the maximum force to every part of the motion.

Lifting a distributed material requires another kind of partition. For water pumped from a tank, a horizontal layer at height yy has volume A(y) dyA(y)\,dy, mass ρA(y) dy\rho A(y)\,dy, and weight ρgA(y) dy\rho gA(y)\,dy. If the outlet is at height HH above the same reference level, that layer travels distance H−yH-y. Thus the ideal lifting work is Wpump=∫y0y1ρgA(y)(H−y) dyW_{\mathrm{pump}}=\int_{y_0}^{y_1}\rho gA(y)(H-y)\,dy, where y0y_0 and y1y_1 bound the occupied water heights and y1≤Hy_1\le H. We assume constant density, constant gravitational acceleration, slow motion, and no pipe friction. A real pump can consume more energy; the integral describes the stated gravitational model.

Work is a dot-product idea in one dimension. If motion is along positive xx and the force component F(x)F(x) is positive, the force adds energy; a negative component removes it. For a spring with restoring force −kx-kx, moving slowly from x=0x=0 to x=b>0x=b>0 makes the spring’s work −kb2/2-kb^2/2 while the external agent’s ideal work is +kb2/2+kb^2/2. Both statements are correct because they describe different forces. In a faster motion, kinetic energy may change, so the external work need not equal the stored spring-energy change at every instant. State the quasistatic assumption when using the simple positive work formula.

A force law need not be linear. If an external force along a path is F(x)=(3 N/m2)x2F(x)=(3\,\mathrm{N/m^2})x^2, with xx measured in metres, the work from x=0x=0 to x=2 mx=2\,\mathrm m is W=∫02 m(3 N/m2)x2 dx=8 JW=\int_{0}^{2\,\mathrm m}(3\,\mathrm{N/m^2})x^2\,dx=8\,\mathrm J. Multiplying the final force F(2 m)=12 NF(2\,\mathrm m)=12\,\mathrm N by 2 m2\,\mathrm m gives 24 J24\,\mathrm J, an overestimate because the force was smaller earlier. The mean force over the path is work divided by distance, here 4 N4\,\mathrm N. That average provides an intuitive cross-check: it lies between the initial 0 N0\,\mathrm N and final 12 N12\,\mathrm N for an increasing force.

The tank-lifting formula can be checked at limiting layers. A layer close to the outlet at height HH travels almost no distance and contributes little work despite its mass. A layer near the bottom travels nearly HH and costs more. For a cylinder with constant horizontal area AA and water from y=0y=0 to y=h≤Hy=h\le H, ideal work is W=ρgA∫0h(H−y) dy=ρgA(Hh−h2/2)W=\rho gA\int_0^h(H-y)\,dy=\rho gA(Hh-h^2/2). The result has joule units and grows with hh. If the outlet were below some water layers, the assumed upward-lifting model would need revision rather than silently using negative travel distance.

The work-energy theorem offers another consistency check: net work on a particle equals the change in kinetic energy, under the usual Newtonian assumptions. If a 3 kg3\,\mathrm{kg} object slows from 4 m/s4\,\mathrm{m/s} to 2 m/s2\,\mathrm{m/s}, net work is Wnet=12(3 kg)[(2 m/s)2−(4 m/s)2]=−18 JW_{\mathrm{net}}=\frac12(3\,\mathrm{kg})[(2\,\mathrm{m/s})^2-(4\,\mathrm{m/s})^2]=-18\,\mathrm J. This does not identify which individual force did the work; friction, a motor, and gravity could contribute. It also does not replace a force integral when the problem asks how work varies along a path. The two formulations connect when force and motion models are both known.

Work by a variable force recovers kinetic-energy change

For one-dimensional motion on a closed time interval with constant mass mm, continuously differentiable signed velocity v(t)v(t), position x′(t)=v(t)x^{\prime}(t)=v(t), and net force F(t)=mv′(t)F(t)=mv^{\prime}(t), work equals the change in kinetic energy.

W=∫t0t1F(t)v(t) dt=m2(v(t1)2−v(t0)2)W=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}F(t)v(t)\,dt=\frac{m}{2}\bigl(v(t_1)^2-v(t_0)^2\bigr)
Reasoning and proof

Parameterize the work integral by time rather than assuming velocity stays positive. Along the path, a small displacement is dx=v(t)dtdx=v(t)dt, so W=∫t0t1F(t)v(t) dtW=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}F(t)v(t)\,dt. The continuous velocity and acceleration make the proper integrand continuous. Newton’s law gives F=ma=mv′F=ma=mv^{\prime}. The chain rule identifies mv′v=(m/2)(v2)′mv^{\prime} v=(m/2)(v^2)^{\prime}. The fundamental theorem therefore yields W=(m/2)[v(t1)2−v(t0)2]W=(m/2)[v(t_1)^2-v(t_0)^2]. The time form remains meaningful through a reversal of motion because signed displacement is retained.

If a 2 kg2\,\mathrm{kg} cart changes speed from 3 m/s3\,\mathrm{m/s} to 5 m/s5\,\mathrm{m/s}, net work is (2/2)(25−9)=16 J(2/2)(25-9)=16\,\mathrm J. The formula uses the vector force component along the one-dimensional motion; in several dimensions work is a dot-product line integral, developed later.

This is a theorem within a model, not a universal statement about energy. It assumes constant mass, continuously differentiable velocity and x′=vx^{\prime}=v, and the stated force law. Friction or propulsion can be included in the net force, while other forms of energy enter separate accounting. Units check: kg⋅m2/s2\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s^2} equals a joule.

5. Mass and balance as weighted accumulation

A rod need not have uniform mass per unit length. If λ(x)\lambda(x) is its nonnegative linear density on [a,b][a,b], a short piece has mass approximately λ(x)Δx\lambda(x)\Delta x. Total mass is M=∫abλ(x) dxM=\int_a^b\lambda(x)\,dx. Density measured in kilograms per metre produces mass in kilograms. A volume density ρ\rho, measured in kilograms per cubic metre, must instead be multiplied by a cross-sectional area before integrating along a length. Density is always density with respect to some measure; the units tell you which one.

To locate the balance point, first take moments about the origin. A small mass at xx contributes xx times its mass, so the total first moment is Q=∫abxλ(x) dxQ=\int_a^b x\lambda(x)\,dx. A single mass MM placed at xˉ\bar x has the same moment when Mxˉ=QM\bar x=Q. Hence xˉ=Q/M\bar x=Q/M, provided M>0M>0 and the integrals are finite. This is a weighted average of positions. For nonnegative density it lies between the rod endpoints, an immediate check on a computed answer. If M=0M=0, there is no defined center of mass from this ratio.

A uniform thin plate between continuous curves f≥gf\ge g on [a,b][a,b] also reduces to one-dimensional integrals. Each vertical strip has its own center halfway between g(x)g(x) and f(x)f(x). With constant positive area density σ\sigma, M=σ∫ab(f−g) dxM=\sigma\int_a^b(f-g)\,dx and xˉ=(σ/M)∫abx(f−g) dx\bar x=(\sigma/M)\int_a^b x(f-g)\,dx. The other coordinate is yˉ=(σ/(2M))∫ab(f2−g2) dx\bar y=(\sigma/(2M))\int_a^b(f^2-g^2)\,dx. The factor one half comes from the strip midpoint, not from an arbitrary rule. The constant density cancels in each coordinate. Density varying only with xx can be included inside both moment integrals; general planar density leads to double integrals in chapter 23.

Center of mass is a ratio of two integrals, so numerator and denominator have different units. For a rod, M=∫abλ(x) dxM=\int_a^b\lambda(x)\,dx has kilograms, while Q=∫abxλ(x) dxQ=\int_a^b x\lambda(x)\,dx has kilogram-metres. Their ratio Q/MQ/M has metres, as a position should. If λ(x)≥0\lambda(x)\ge0 and M>0M>0, multiply a≤x≤ba\le x\le b by λ(x)\lambda(x), integrate, and divide by MM to prove a≤xˉ≤ba\le\bar x\le b. This bound is stronger than a casual sketch: any computed center outside the rod contradicts either the algebra or the nonnegative-density assumption.

For λ(x)=1+x\lambda(x)=1+x on [0,2][0,2] with consistent units, mass is M=∫02(1+x) dx=4M=\int_0^2(1+x)\,dx=4, and first moment is Q=∫02x(1+x) dx=2+8/3=14/3Q=\int_0^2x(1+x)\,dx=2+8/3=14/3. Therefore xˉ=(14/3)/4=7/6\bar x=(14/3)/4=7/6, slightly to the right of the midpoint 11 because density increases with xx. Treating the density formula’s numerical coefficients without their units is shorthand; in a physical model the constant term and slope carry compatible units.

Let yy measure metres downward from a calm free surface in a static fluid of constant density ρ=1000 kg/m3\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}, with g=9.81 m/s2g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}. Placing the force at the gate center would give the right total magnitude but the wrong turning effect on a hinge.

The model assumes a static constant-density fluid, level free surface, and constant gate width on the stated interval. If the width changes, replace 2 m2\,\mathrm m by w(y)w(y) in both force and moment integrals. Atmospheric pressure is omitted from gauge pressure only when equal pressure on the other side cancels it; otherwise integrate the actual pressure difference across the gate. These assumptions govern the force direction and magnitude as much as the antiderivative does.

The same slice method transfers to other physical integrals: identify a small piece, write its contribution with units, integrate over the actual geometry, and check whether the output is total amount or a moment. The antiderivative is usually the shortest part of a sound modeling solution.

The hydrostatic force formula is pressure times area integrated over depth. With a general vertical gate width w(y)w(y), a strip contributes dF≈ρgy w(y) dydF\approx\rho gy\,w(y)\,dy. The width is not necessarily the horizontal coordinate of a drawn boundary; it is the total length of the gate at that depth. For a triangular gate widening downward with w(y)=kyw(y)=ky on 0≤y≤H0\le y\le H, the force is F=ρgk∫0Hy2 dy=ρgkH3/3F=\rho gk\int_0^H y^2\,dy=\rho gkH^3/3. The cubic dependence on HH is a useful design warning: doubling depth under this geometry multiplies force by eight, assuming kk and fluid properties remain fixed.

The center of pressure is a weighted average of depth with weight equal to force per depth. For the same triangular gate, ycp=∫0Hy(ρgky2) dy∫0Hρgky2 dy=H4/4H3/3=3H4y_{\mathrm{cp}}=\frac{\int_0^H y(\rho gk y^2)\,dy}{\int_0^H\rho gk y^2\,dy}=\frac{H^4/4}{H^3/3}=\frac{3H}{4}. The geometric centroid of the triangular shape is at 2H/32H/3 from the surface when it widens downward, so pressure pushes the resultant deeper than the area centroid. The reason is not a memorized formula: pressure itself increases with depth, giving lower strips more force per unit area.

Pressure is a scalar, while the actual force on a small surface element acts normal to the surface. On the flat vertical gate in the case study, all elemental forces point in the same horizontal direction, so their magnitudes add directly. A curved gate would require resolving directions before finding the net force vector, even if the scalar pressure law p=ρgyp=\rho gy still held at each depth. This is an example of how the same slice idea survives while the contribution changes. The geometry of each piece determines what must be summed.

An engineering result should report the measurement reference. The depth yy is measured downward from the free surface; the outlet height HH in a pumping problem is measured from the same datum as the water layers; a moment arm is measured from a named hinge. Shifting the coordinate origin does not change physical force or work if all distances are transformed consistently, but mixing reference levels does. Units, direction, and datum are part of the model specification that makes an integral interpretable.

For pressure calculations, two different weighted averages can appear. Average pressure over a flat gate is total force divided by gate area. Center of pressure is depth weighted by force, so it generally lies below the depth associated with average pressure. On a constant-width rectangular gate under a linear pressure law, average pressure equals pressure at mean depth, but the resultant force acts deeper. Confusing the location of an equivalent uniform pressure with the line of action of the total force leads to an incorrect torque even when the total force is correct.

Deeper derivation · The differential element carries the geometry and units

A volume integral is built from cross-sectional area times thickness. If a solid has area A(x)A(x) at position xx over [a,b][a,b], then a thin slice contributes about A(x)ΔxA(x)\Delta x and V=∫abA(x) dxV=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx. The visual diagram plots area against position; the shaded area under that curve represents volume, not a two-dimensional slice of the solid itself.

Shells reorganize the same solid by cylindrical layers. A shell has circumference 2πr2\pi r, height h(r)h(r), and thickness drdr, giving dV≈2πrh(r) drdV\approx2\pi r h(r)\,dr. Washers use area π(R2−r2)\pi(R^2-r^2) times thickness dxdx when a slice has an outer and inner radius. Choosing a slice direction is part of the setup: it determines the variable, radius, height, and bounds. A plausible looking formula with a missing square or missing radius factor has wrong units.

The same dimensional check helps in physical applications. A force F(x)F(x) in newtons integrated over displacement dxdx in metres produces work in joules. Chapter synthesis

  • Build an integral by naming a representative piece, its contribution, the bounds, and the units.
  • Area uses positive strip height; volume uses cross-sectional area or distinct shell layers.
  • Curve length contributes ds, and a surface of revolution contributes circumference times ds.
  • Work accumulates signed force over displacement; mass and moments accumulate density-weighted pieces.
