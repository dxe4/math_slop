Change of variables and probability · part 2

Written practice and solutions

  1. Problem 1. Fluency Find the area of the ellipse x2/9+y2/4≤1x^2/9+y^2/4\le1 using a change of variables.

    Hint. Scale the unit disk by x=3ux=3u and y=2vy=2v.

    Solution. The transformed domain is u2+v2≤1u^2+v^2\le1, and the absolute Jacobian determinant is 66. Thus the ellipse area is ∬u2+v2≤16 du dv=6π\iint_{u^2+v^2\le1}6\,du\,dv=6\pi.

  2. Problem 2. Interpretation Explain why using 0≤θ≤4π0\le\theta\le4\pi in a polar integral over a disk doubles its value, despite using the correct Jacobian.

    Hint. Count how many parameter pairs represent a typical disk point.

    Solution. Over 0≤θ≤4π0\le\theta\le4\pi, a typical disk point is represented twice. The polar Jacobian rr handles local area scale, but not repeated global coverage. Splitting into two full turns gives two copies of the intended integral.

  3. Problem 3. Fluency Find the Jacobian determinant of T(u,v)=(2u,−3v)T(u,v)=(2u,-3v).

    Hint. Differentiate both coordinate outputs.

    Solution. The Jacobian matrix is (200−3)\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-3\end{pmatrix}, so its determinant is −6-6. The area scale is the absolute value, 66.

  4. Problem 4. Fluency Find the Jacobian determinant of T(u,v)=(u+v,v)T(u,v)=(u+v,v).

    Hint. Differentiate both coordinate outputs.

    Solution. The Jacobian matrix is (1101)\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, whose determinant is 11. Thus the shear preserves area.

  5. Problem 5. Interpretation Why does 0≤θ≤4π0\le\theta\le4\pi double a disk integral?

    Hint. Check global coverage as well as local scaling.

    Solution. Most disk points are represented twice when θ\theta covers 00 to 4π4\pi. The Jacobian rr is locally correct, but the integral doubles through repeated coverage.

  6. Problem 6. Fluency Under x=2u,y=3vx=2u,y=3v, what region maps to x2/4+y2/9≤1x^2/4+y^2/9\le1?

    Hint. Substitute the coordinate relations into the ellipse inequality.

    Solution. With x=2ux=2u and y=3vy=3v, x2/4+y2/9=u2+v2x^2/4+y^2/9=u^2+v^2. Thus the preimage is precisely the unit disk u2+v2≤1u^2+v^2\le1.

  7. Problem 7. Proof Can h(x,y)=1/(x2+y2)h(x,y)=1/(x^2+y^2) on 0<x2+y2≤10<x^2+y^2\le1 be normalized as a probability density?

    Hint. Include the polar Jacobian in the improper integral.

    Solution. In polar coordinates the total mass is 2π∫01drr2\pi\int_0^1\frac{dr}{r}. Truncating at ε\varepsilon gives −2πln⁡ε→∞-2\pi\ln\varepsilon\to\infty as ε→0+\varepsilon\to0^+. No positive finite normalizing constant can make the mass one.

  8. Problem 8. Fluency Use polar coordinates to find the area of the annulus 1≤x2+y2≤91\le x^2+y^2\le9.

    Hint. Turn squared-radius bounds into radial bounds.

    Solution. The region has 1≤r≤31\le r\le3 and 0≤θ≤2π0\le\theta\le2\pi. Its area is ∫02π∫13r dr dθ=2π(9−1)/2=8π\int_0^{2\pi}\int_1^3r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi(9-1)/2=8\pi.

  9. Problem 9. Fluency Use polar coordinates to find area of annulus 1≤r≤31\le r\le3.

    Hint. Integrate the polar area element over one full turn.

    Solution. Since dA=r dr dθdA=r\,dr\,d\theta, the area is ∫02π∫13r dr dθ=π(32−12)=8π\int_0^{2\pi}\int_1^3r\,dr\,d\theta=\pi(3^2-1^2)=8\pi. The inner radial integral measures the difference of the two squared radii divided by two.

  10. Problem 10. Fluency Find area of x2+y2≤2xx^2+y^2\le2x using polar coordinates.

    Hint. Use 0≤r≤2cos⁡θ0\le r\le2\cos\theta where ∣θ∣≤π/2|\theta|\le\pi/2.

    Solution. The disk inequality becomes r2≤2rcos⁡θr^2\le2r\cos\theta. Thus its area is ∫−π/2π/2∫02cos⁡θr dr dθ=2∫−π/2π/2cos⁡2θ dθ=π\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_0^{2\cos\theta}r\,dr\,d\theta=2\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\pi.

  11. Problem 11. Interpretation For R>0R>0, why does sampling a radius uniformly on [0,R][0,R] fail to give a uniform-area point in a disk of radius RR?

    Hint. Compare equal radial intervals with their annular areas.

    Solution. An annulus of radius rr and width drdr has area approximately 2πr dr2\pi r\,dr, which increases with rr. Uniform radius therefore overweights the center relative to uniform area. The required radial density is 2r/R22r/R^2, obtained by r=RUr=R\sqrt U for uniform UU.

  12. Problem 12. Modeling For a disk of radius R>0R>0, how can UU uniform on [0,1][0,1] generate a uniform-area radius?

    Hint. Match the area fraction within radius ss.

    Solution. Uniform area requires P(r≤s)=s2/R2P(r\le s)=s^2/R^2 for 0≤s≤R0\le s\le R. If UU is uniform on [0,1][0,1] and r=RUr=R\sqrt U, then P(r≤s)=P(U≤s2/R2)=s2/R2P(r\le s)=P(U\le s^2/R^2)=s^2/R^2. Use an independent uniform angle.

  13. Problem 13. Fluency Set up and evaluate the volume of the portion of the ball x2+y2+z2≤9x^2+y^2+z^2\le9 inside the cone 0≤ϕ≤π/30\le\phi\le\pi/3.

    Hint. Use the spherical volume factor and the stated angle ranges.

    Solution. The bounds are 0≤ρ≤30\le\rho\le3, 0≤ϕ≤π/30\le\phi\le\pi/3, and 0≤θ≤2π0\le\theta\le2\pi. Hence V=∫02π∫0π/3∫03ρ2sin⁡ϕ dρ dϕ dθ=9(1/2)(2π)=9πV=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/3}\int_0^3\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta=9(1/2)(2\pi)=9\pi cubic units.

  14. Problem 14. Fluency Find volume of a cylinder radius 22, height 33 using cylindrical coordinates.

    Hint. Integrate rr over 0≤r≤20\le r\le2, then use the height.

    Solution. In cylindrical coordinates, V=∫02π∫02∫03r dz dr dθ=2π⋅2⋅3=12πV=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_0^3r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot2\cdot3=12\pi cubic units. The height integral contributes three, while the radial integral contributes two before the angular factor.

  15. Problem 15. Fluency Find volume of a ball of radius aa.

    Hint. Use the full spherical angular ranges and 0≤ρ≤a0\le\rho\le a.

    Solution. For a≥0a\ge0, V=∫02π∫0π∫0aρ2sin⁡ϕ dρ dϕ dθ=(a3/3)(2)(2π)=4πa3/3V=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^a\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta=(a^3/3)(2)(2\pi)=4\pi a^3/3. The angular factors are two and 2π2\pi, and the radial factor is a3/3a^3/3.

  16. Problem 16. Fluency Find volume inside ρ≤a\rho\le a and 0≤ϕ≤π/30\le\phi\le\pi/3.

    Hint. Integrate ρ2sin⁡ϕ\rho^2\sin\phi over the cone's angle range.

    Solution. For a≥0a\ge0, the radial, polar, and azimuthal factors are a3/3a^3/3, 1−cos⁡(π/3)=1/21-\cos(\pi/3)=1/2, and 2π2\pi. Their product is πa3/3\pi a^3/3.

  17. Problem 17. Interpretation What spherical ϕ\phi range describes the upper hemisphere here?

    Hint. Use the convention that ϕ\phi is measured down from +z+z.

    Solution. Since z=ρcos⁡ϕz=\rho\cos\phi, the upper hemisphere has z≥0z\ge0, or cos⁡ϕ≥0\cos\phi\ge0. Within 0≤ϕ≤π0\le\phi\le\pi, this means 0≤ϕ≤π/20\le\phi\le\pi/2.

  18. Problem 18. Fluency For a point (X,Y)(X,Y) uniformly distributed in a disk of radius 22 centered at the origin, let R=X2+Y2R=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}. Find P(R≤1)P(R\le1) and E[R]E[R].

    Hint. Derive the radial density from the disk's uniform area density.

    Solution. The planar density is 1/(4π)1/(4\pi), so a ring gives fR(r)=r/2f_R(r)=r/2 on 0≤r≤20\le r\le2. Therefore P(R≤1)=∫01r/2 dr=1/4P(R\le1)=\int_0^1r/2\,dr=1/4 and E[R]=∫02r2/2 dr=4/3E[R]=\int_0^2r^2/2\,dr=4/3.

  19. Problem 19. Fluency Let f(x,y)=6xf(x,y)=6x on 0≤y≤1−x0\le y\le1-x, 0≤x≤10\le x\le1, and zero elsewhere. Verify normalization and find the marginal density and expectation of XX. Now replace the supplied coefficient 66 by an unknown real constant cc, keeping exactly the same region and zero density outside it. Derive the normalization equation, determine every allowed cc, and check nonnegativity.

    Hint. The vertical slice at xx has length 1−x1-x.

    Solution. Integrating over yy gives fX(x)=6x(1−x)f_X(x)=6x(1-x) for 0≤x≤10\le x\le1 and zero elsewhere. Its integral is 6(1/2−1/3)=16(1/2-1/3)=1. Then E[X]=6∫01(x2−x3) dx=6(1/3−1/4)=1/2E[X]=6\int_0^1(x^2-x^3)\,dx=6(1/3-1/4)=1/2. For the unknown coefficient, the total mass is ∫01∫01−xcx dy dx=c∫01(x−x2) dx=c/6\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}cx\,dy\,dx=c\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx=c/6. Normalization forces c/6=1c/6=1, so the only value is c=6c=6. With that value the density is nonnegative throughout its support; negative constants cannot define a nonnegative density and no other constant has mass one.

  20. Problem 20. Fluency For R>0R>0, find the planar density of a uniform disk of radius RR.

    Hint. Normalize a constant over disk area πR2\pi R^2.

    Solution. For R>0R>0, the planar density is 1/(πR2)1/(\pi R^2) inside the disk and zero outside. Multiplying by area πR2\pi R^2 confirms total probability one.

  21. Problem 21. Fluency For a point uniformly distributed in a disk of radius R>0R>0, find the density of its distance from the center.

    Hint. Differentiate the area fraction within radius rr.

    Solution. For 0≤r≤R0\le r\le R, the radial cumulative law is P(R≤r)=r2/R2P(\mathcal R\le r)=r^2/R^2. Its derivative gives pR(r)=2r/R2p_{\mathcal R}(r)=2r/R^2, and it is zero outside the radius interval.

  22. Problem 22. Fluency For the joint density f(x,y)=6xf(x,y)=6x on 0≤x≤1, 0≤y≤1−x0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le1-x, and zero elsewhere, find fX(x)f_X(x).

    Hint. Integrate over the allowed yy interval at fixed xx.

    Solution. For 0≤x≤10\le x\le1, fX(x)=∫01−x6x dy=6x(1−x)f_X(x)=\int_0^{1-x}6x\,dy=6x(1-x). Outside [0,1][0,1], the marginal is zero.

  23. Problem 23. Interpretation Does zero covariance alone prove independence?

    Hint. Use a centered disk as a counterexample.

    Solution. No. Symmetry gives Cov⁡(X,Y)=0\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0 for a uniform centered disk, yet X2+Y2≤R2X^2+Y^2\le R^2 constrains one coordinate when the other is near the edge. Thus the coordinates are dependent despite zero covariance.

  24. Problem 24. Fluency For p(x,y)=π−1e−(x2+y2)p(x,y)=\pi^{-1}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}, find the probability of radius greater than 22.

    Hint. Use the complementary radial cumulative probability.

    Solution. The radial cumulative law is P(r≤R)=1−e−R2P(r\le R)=1-e^{-R^2}. Thus P(r>2)=e−4≈0.0183156P(r>2)=e^{-4}\approx0.0183156. Truncating a numerical integral at radius 22 loses this tail.

  25. Problem 25. Fluency Find the radius where the radial density pR(r)=2re−r2p_R(r)=2r e^{-r^2} is largest.

    Hint. Differentiate the radial density and compare endpoint behavior.

    Solution. Differentiation gives pR′(r)=2e−r2(1−2r2)p_R^{\prime}(r)=2e^{-r^2}(1-2r^2). The positive critical radius is r=1/2r=1/\sqrt2; density is zero at r=0r=0 and tends to zero at infinity, so this is its maximum.

  26. Problem 26. Fluency For the planar density p(x,y)=π−1e−(x2+y2)p(x,y)=\pi^{-1}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}, find the probability that the distance from the origin exceeds 22.

    Hint. Integrate the radial tail with the substitution u=r2u=r^2.

    Solution. The radial density is 2re−r22re^{-r^2}, so P(r>2)=∫2∞2re−r2 dr=[−e−r2]2∞=e−4P(r>2)=\int_2^\infty2re^{-r^2}\,dr=[-e^{-r^2}]_2^\infty=e^{-4}. The antiderivative vanishes at infinity and leaves the lower-endpoint tail.

  27. Problem 27. Fluency Find the radial mode of 2re−r22r e^{-r^2}.

    Hint. Differentiate with respect to rr and compare the endpoints.

    Solution. The derivative is 2e−r2(1−2r2)2e^{-r^2}(1-2r^2). It changes from positive to negative at r=1/2r=1/\sqrt2, which is the radial mode.

  28. Problem 28. Fluency Find the median radius for 2re−r22r e^{-r^2}.

    Hint. Set the cumulative radial probability equal to one half.

    Solution. The cumulative law is 1−e−r21-e^{-r^2}. Solving 1−e−r2=1/21-e^{-r^2}=1/2 gives r2=ln⁡2r^2=\ln2, so the median radius is ln⁡2\sqrt{\ln2}.

  29. Problem 29. Interpretation Why can planar density peak at zero while radial density peaks away from zero?

    Hint. Account for the circumference of each radial ring.

    Solution. Planar density measures probability per unit area, while radial density includes the ring factor 2πr2\pi r. That factor vanishes at the origin and can shift the radial peak away from zero.

  30. Problem 30. Fluency For the planar density p(x,y)=(πσ2)−1e−(x2+y2)/σ2p(x,y)=(\pi\sigma^2)^{-1}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/\sigma^2}, where σ>0\sigma>0, find P(r>R)P(r>R) for distance r=x2+y2r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2} and R≥0R\ge0.

    Hint. Integrate the radial tail after including the Jacobian.

Thus P(r>R)=∫R∞(2r/σ2)e−r2/σ2 dr=e−R2/σ2P(r>R)=\int_R^\infty(2r/\sigma^2)e^{-r^2/\sigma^2}\,dr=e^{-R^2/\sigma^2}. The substitution u=r2/σ2u=r^2/\sigma^2 converts the integral to an exponential tail.

  31. Problem 31. Fluency What is the polar area element?

    Hint. A thin angular wedge grows with radius.

    Solution. dA=r dr dθdA=r\,dr\,d\theta for r≥0r\ge0, where rr is the current integration radius.

  32. Problem 32. Interpretation Why is a determinant’s absolute value used for unsigned area?

    Hint. Can a reflection make the determinant negative?

    Solution. A negative determinant reverses orientation but does not create negative physical area. Its magnitude gives local area scaling.

  33. Problem 33. Interpretation A constant joint density is supported on [0,1/4]×[0,1/2][0,1/4]\times[0,1/2] and zero elsewhere. Normalize it, then find P(X≤1/8)P(X\le1/8). Is a density value above 11 invalid?

    Hint. The support area is 1/81/8; probability is density times event area.

    Solution. Normalization requires c(1/4)(1/2)=1c(1/4)(1/2)=1, so the density is c=8c=8. The event occupies area (1/8)(1/2)=1/16(1/8)(1/2)=1/16, giving probability 8/16=1/28/16=1/2. A density may exceed 11; its integral over an event, rather than its pointwise height, must lie between 00 and 11.

  34. Problem 34. Fluency Transform ∬E(x2+y2) dA\iint_E(x^2+y^2)\,dA for E:x2/4+y2/9≤1E:x^2/4+y^2/9\le1 using x=2u,y=3vx=2u,y=3v. Give transformed domain, integrand, absolute Jacobian, and integral.

  36. Problem 36. Modeling Estimate total mass for uniform density 1/π1/\pi on the unit disk by midpoint cells on an n-by-n grid over [−1,1]2[-1,1]^2, with n=2 and n=4. Compare exact normalization and explain boundary error.

Concept checks

  1. Which Jacobian factor must multiply dr dθdr\,d\theta when a planar integral is converted to polar coordinates?

    • No extra factor
    • rr
    • r2r^2

    Answer. rr. A thin sector has radial width drdr and arc length about r dθr\,d\theta, so dA=r dr dθdA=r\,dr\,d\theta.

  2. What is the polar radial range for a disk of radius RR centered at the pole?

    • 0≤r≤R0\le r\le R
    • −R≤r≤R-R\le r\le R
    • r=Rr=R only

    Answer. 0≤r≤R0\le r\le R. The interior includes all nonnegative radii from center to boundary.

  3. What must a probability density integrate to over its support?

    • 00
    • 11
    • its maximum

    Answer. 11. Normalization requires total probability one.

  4. What does a marginal density do?

    • Integrates out another variable
    • Differentiates the joint density
    • Always becomes constant

    Answer. Integrates out another variable. It accumulates joint density over the unobserved coordinate.

  5. Why use ∣det⁡J∣|\det J| in an ordinary change of variables?

    • To retain nonnegative area scaling
    • To cancel all units
    • Because det⁡J\det J is always positive

    Answer. To retain nonnegative area scaling. Orientation may reverse, but unsigned area scaling uses the determinant magnitude.

Chapter synthesis

  • A coordinate change transforms the domain, the integrand, and the area or volume element together.
  • Absolute Jacobian determinants measure local scaling; parameter ranges control global coverage.
  • Nonnegative densities with total integral 1 turn integration into probability, marginals, and expectations.
  • Exact normalization and geometric bounds provide useful checks on numerical experiments.

Common mistakes to check

  • “The Jacobian can be negative, so the area can be negative.” Ordinary area and volume integrals use its absolute value; the sign records orientation. Test with problem 3
  • “Uniform angle and uniform radius produce a uniform disk.” For a disk of radius R>0R>0, uniform area requires radial density 2r/R22r/R^2 for 0≤r≤R0\le r\le R, because outer annuli have more area. Test with problem 11
  • “A density must stay below 1.” Its integral over a region is a probability; a density can exceed 1 on a small enough support. Test with problem 33
