Study routes and prerequisite check

Prerequisite questions

  1. Solve 3x−7=113x-7=11.

    • x=4x=4
    • x=6x=6
    • x=18x=18

    Answer. x=6x=6. Add 7 to get 3x=183x=18, then divide by 3. If this was uncertain, review linear equations before limits.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • x=4x=4 — Substituting x=4x=4 gives 3(4)−7=53(4)-7=5, not 11.
    • x=18x=18 — The value 18 is 3x3x after adding 7; it is not xx. Divide by 3.

  2. Factor x2−9x^2-9.

    • (x−3)(x+3)(x-3)(x+3)
    • (x−9)(x+1)(x-9)(x+1)
    • (x−3)2(x-3)^2

    Answer. (x−3)(x+3)(x-3)(x+3). Difference of squares: a2−b2=(a−b)(a+b)a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b). This is used to simplify removable-limit examples.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • (x−9)(x+1)(x-9)(x+1) — Expanding (x−9)(x+1)(x-9)(x+1) gives x2−8x−9x^2-8x-9, with an extra linear term.
    • (x−3)2(x-3)^2 — Expanding (x−3)2(x-3)^2 gives x2−6x+9x^2-6x+9; both the linear term and constant differ.

  3. For real xx, when is x−2\sqrt{x-2} defined?

    • x>2x>2 only
    • x≥2x\geq 2
    • all real xx

    Answer. x≥2x\geq 2. A real square root needs x−2≥0x-2\geq 0, including the endpoint x=2x=2.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • x>2x>2 only — The endpoint x=2x=2 is allowed because its radicand is zero and 0=0\sqrt{0}=0.
    • all real xx — For x<2x<2, the radicand x−2x-2 is negative, so the square root is not real.

  4. Simplify x2−1x−1\frac{x^2-1}{x-1} for x≠1x\ne 1.

    • x−1x-1
    • x+1x+1
    • 11

    Answer. x+1x+1. Factor (x−1)(x+1)(x-1)(x+1), then cancel only where x−1x-1 is nonzero.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • x−1x-1 — The factor x−1x-1 cancels with the denominator; the other factor, x+1x+1, remains.
    • 11 — Cancellation does not make the quotient 1: at x=2x=2 the original quotient is 3.

  5. What is sin⁡(π/2)\sin(\pi/2)?

    • 00
    • 11
    • −1-1

    Answer. 11. The unit-circle point at angle π/2\pi/2 has vertical coordinate 1. Trigonometric calculus uses radians.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • 00 — Zero is the horizontal coordinate of the unit-circle point at π/2\pi/2; sine is its vertical coordinate.
    • −1-1 — The vertical coordinate is −1-1 at 3π/23\pi/2, not at π/2\pi/2.

  6. Which identity is correct?

    • sin⁡2x+cos⁡2x=1\sin^2 x+\cos^2 x=1
    • sin⁡(x+y)=sin⁡x+sin⁡y\sin(x+y)=\sin x+\sin y
    • cos⁡2x−sin⁡2x=1\cos^2 x-\sin^2 x=1

    Answer. sin⁡2x+cos⁡2x=1\sin^2 x+\cos^2 x=1. The Pythagorean identity follows from the unit circle; the other formulas are generally false.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • sin⁡(x+y)=sin⁡x+sin⁡y\sin(x+y)=\sin x+\sin y — For x=y=π/2x=y=\pi/2, the left side is sin⁡π=0\sin\pi=0 while the proposed right side is 2.
    • cos⁡2x−sin⁡2x=1\cos^2 x-\sin^2 x=1 — The difference cos⁡2x−sin⁡2x\cos^2x-\sin^2x equals cos⁡(2x)\cos(2x), which is not always 1.

  7. What is 2−32^{-3}?

    • −8-8
    • 18\frac{1}{8}
    • 83\frac{8}{3}

    Answer. 18\frac{1}{8}. A negative exponent takes a reciprocal: 2−3=1232^{-3}=\frac{1}{2^3}.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • −8-8 — A negative exponent takes a reciprocal; it does not change the sign of a positive base.
    • 83\frac{8}{3} — The exponent −3-3 does not mean divide 232^3 by 3; it means take the reciprocal of 232^3.

  8. If f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2, what is f(−3)f(-3)?

    • −9-9
    • 66
    • 99

    Answer. 99. Square the entire input: (−3)2=9(-3)^2=9. Function notation treats the input as one object.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • −9-9 — The entire input is squared: f(−3)=(−3)(−3)f(-3)=(-3)(-3), not −(32)-(3^2).
    • 66 — A square multiplies the input by itself; it does not double its magnitude.

  9. Which interval describes ∣x∣<2|x|<2?

    • [−2,2][-2,2]
    • (−2,2)(-2,2)
    • x>2x>2

    Answer. (−2,2)(-2,2). The strict absolute-value inequality means −2<x<2-2<x<2; neither endpoint is included.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • [−2,2][-2,2] — The brackets include x=±2x=\pm2, but each endpoint has absolute value 2 and fails the strict inequality.
    • x>2x>2 — Absolute value measures distance from zero; distance less than 2 includes values on both sides of zero.

  10. What is the slope through (1,2)(1,2) and (3,8)(3,8)?

    • 22
    • 33
    • 66

    Answer. 33. Change in output divided by change in input is 8−23−1=3\frac{8-2}{3-1}=3.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • 22 — The input change is 2, but slope is output change divided by input change.
    • 66 — The output change is 6; divide it by the input change of 2 to get the slope.

  11. A speed is 5 metres per second for 4 seconds. What distance is travelled?

    • 1.25 m1.25\,\mathrm{m}
    • 9 m9\,\mathrm{m}
    • 20 m20\,\mathrm{m}

    Answer. 20 m20\,\mathrm{m}. Rate ×\times time gives 5 m/s×4 s=20 m5\,\mathrm{m/s}\times4\,\mathrm{s}=20\,\mathrm{m}. Unit checks catch many calculus setup errors.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • 1.25 m1.25\,\mathrm{m} — Dividing speed by time gives units of metres per second squared, not distance.
    • 9 m9\,\mathrm{m} — Adding 5 metres per second to 4 seconds mixes unlike units; multiply rate by time.

  12. What is the domain of ln⁡x\ln x as a real-valued function?

    • x>0x>0
    • x≥0x\geq 0
    • all real xx

    Answer. x>0x>0. The natural logarithm takes positive real inputs; ln⁡0\ln 0 is undefined.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • x≥0x\geq 0 — The endpoint x=0x=0 is excluded: no finite real exponent makes ey=0e^y=0.
    • all real xx — For real yy, eye^y is positive, so no nonpositive input has a real logarithm.

Calculus I · 14-week route

Foundations, limits, derivatives, and the first integral. A typical week combines chapter reading, hand calculations, a visual investigation, the code lab, and the solved practice. Move ahead only after you can explain a result without the graph.

1 · Diagnose and repair prerequisites · 01–03 · Take the diagnostic. Review function domains, algebra, radians, and basic JavaScript for labs. Review real hyperbolic branches in lesson 02.7.

2 · Limits from tables to definitions · 04 · Distinguish a value from a limit and prove a linear ε–δ statement.

3 · Continuity and existence · 04 · Use one-sided limits and the intermediate value theorem with its full hypotheses.

4 · Derivative from first principles · 05 · Derive derivatives by limits and interpret units of rates.

5 · Rules and nested functions · 06 · Practise product, quotient, chain, implicit, and inverse rules. Check inverse-hyperbolic derivative domains and the arcosh endpoint in lesson 06.6.

6 · Rates and local models · 07 · Set up related rates and quantify linearization error.

7 · The shape of functions · 08 · Use mean value theorem reasoning, sign charts, concavity, and endpoint checks.

8 · Optimization models · 09 · Translate constraints into feasible domains and compare all candidates.

9 · Root finding and numerical evidence · 09 · Run bisection and Newton methods; identify failure modes and error evidence.

10 · Accumulation as a limit · 10 · Build signed Riemann sums and separate displacement from distance.

11 · Integral properties and averages · 10 · Use bounds, orientation, additivity, and weighted averages.

12 · Fundamental theorem · 11 · Explain why an accumulation derivative returns the rate and evaluate definite integrals.

13 · Substitution and synthesis · 11–12 · Change bounds correctly under substitution and select a suitable method. Verify the radical substitution, square-root sign, and definite bounds in lesson 12.7.

14 · Review and project · 04–12 · Model a rate, calculate its total change, verify units, and compare a numerical result with exact reasoning.

Calculus II · 14-week route

Integration, sequences and series, approximations, and curves. Keep a separate log of conditions for each convergence test and error bound.

1 · Integration method selection · 12 · Review substitution, integration by parts, and differentiation checks. Include the branch-safe radical method of lesson 12.7.

2 · Partial fractions and trigonometry · 12 · Decompose rational functions and state domain intervals for logarithms.

3 · Area and volume · 13 · Derive disk, washer, and shell integrals from physical slices.

4 · Work, mass, and arc length · 13 · Label units for force, density, and path length.

5 · Numerical quadrature · 14 · Compare trapezoidal and Simpson rules and justify refinement.

6 · Improper integrals · 14 · Classify infinite tails and endpoint singularities separately.

7 · Sequences and partial sums · 15 · Prove that small terms need not give a convergent series.

8 · Positive-term tests · 15–16 · Select comparisons, integral tests, ratios, or roots with conditions.

9 · Alternation and absolute convergence · 16 · Bound an alternating remainder and distinguish conditional convergence.

10 · Power-series intervals · 17 · Find radius and test both endpoints independently.

11 · Taylor models · 17 · Build polynomials and certify a remainder on a stated interval.

12 · Parametric curves · 18 · Compute tangent, speed, and arc length; inspect retracing. Classify focus-directrix conics and translated equations in lessons 18.7–8.

13 · Polar calculus · 18 · Trace curves and choose area and length bounds carefully. Trace both polar hyperbola branches and exclude denominator-zero directions in lesson 18.9.

14 · Review and project · 12–18 · Publish a numerical approximation with exact bounds, code, assumptions, and an error discussion.

Calculus III · 14-week route

Geometry, multivariable differentiation and integration, and vector theorems. Draw domains and orientations before setting up each integral.

1 · Vectors and 3D geometry · 19 · Practise dot and cross products, lines, planes, and units.

2 · Motion and curvature · 19 · Separate speed, velocity, tangential acceleration, and normal acceleration. Compare world-coordinate motion with its projection in lesson 19.7.

3 · Surfaces and level sets · 20 · Read two-variable domains and level curves. Classify axis-aligned quadric relations using world-height traces in lessons 20.7–9.

4 · Multivariable limits · 20 · Use path counterexamples and uniform radial bounds.

5 · Gradients and tangent planes · 21 · Distinguish partial derivatives from total differentiability. State the regularity hypothesis before using a level-surface normal in lesson 21.6.

6 · Chain rule and Jacobian · 21 · Track dependence through several variables and compose derivatives.

7 · Optimization in two variables · 22 · Classify critical points and check compact boundaries.

8 · Constraints and multipliers · 22 · State regularity and compare candidate values.

9 · Double integrals · 23 · Draw regions, set bounds, and reverse order.

10 · Triple integrals and mass · 23 · Interpret volume elements and density units.

11 · Coordinate changes · 24 · Derive and use polar, cylindrical, and spherical Jacobians.

12 · Probability and joint density · 24 · Normalize a density and find marginals and expectations.

13 · Fields and line integrals · 25 · Calculate work and test path independence with domain conditions.

14 · Boundary theorems and project · 26 · Choose Green, Stokes, or divergence theorem with orientation and a verified direct case.

Self-paced route

Use mastery rather than calendar time. Each chapter has a visual, a derivation, worked examples, solved exercises, concept checks, and a code lab. Complete a chapter when you can reconstruct a key calculation on paper, state the relevant theorem conditions, and explain what the experiment cannot prove.

Prepare · 01–03 · Take the diagnostic. If fewer than ten answers are secure, work through algebra, functions, radians, and the computing primer.

Change · 04–09 · Move from limits to derivatives and applications. Keep a list of proof obligations: domain, continuity, differentiability, endpoints.

Accumulation · 10–14 · Build integrals from sums, then use the fundamental theorem, techniques, applications, and numerical checks.

Infinity · 15–17 · Distinguish term size from total sum, and attach a remainder bound to approximations.

Geometry · 18–19 · Trace parameterized curves and connect vectors, speed, and curvature. Include complete conic branches and world-versus-projected curves in chapters 18–19.

Several variables · 20–24 · Draw domains; verify path-independent claims and Jacobian factors. Compare quadric traces and regular implicit-surface normals in chapters 20–21.

Fields and models · 25–28 · State orientation and domain hypotheses; compare numerical trajectories with analytic checks. Construct forced and series ODE solutions, then compare stability with accuracy in chapters 27–28.
