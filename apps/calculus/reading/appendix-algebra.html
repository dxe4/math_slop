Appendix A · Algebra and trigonometry for calculus

A compact repair path for the operations most often used in limits, derivatives, and integration. Each identity has conditions; those conditions remain part of any simplified expression.

Domains, equations, and transformations

A real-valued formula has a domain. Fractions exclude zero denominators; even roots require nonnegative radicands; logarithms require positive arguments. A composition f(g(x))f(g(x)) requires xx in the domain of gg and g(x)g(x) in the domain of ff. State these restrictions before canceling a factor, taking a reciprocal, or applying a derivative rule.

Factoring a2−b2=(a−b)(a+b)a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b) often turns a 0/00/0 limit into a simpler nearby formula. The cancellation is valid only where the canceled factor is nonzero. Completing the square x2+bx+c=(x+b/2)2+c−b2/4x^2+bx+c=(x+b/2)^2+c-b^2/4 reveals vertex location and positivity. The quadratic formula applies to ax2+bx+c=0ax^2+bx+c=0 when a≠0a\ne0; real roots require discriminant b2−4ac≥0b^2-4ac\geq0.

A graph transformation g(x)=af(b(x−h))+kg(x)=a f(b(x-h))+k changes input and output in different orders. For b≠0b\ne0, its horizontal effect is a shift by hh and scale by 1/∣b∣1/|b|, with reflection if b<0b<0. The outside multiplier aa scales vertically and reflects if negative; kk shifts vertically. Check a landmark point or an inverse mapping rather than memorizing a direction rule alone.

Powers, exponentials, and logarithms

For positive bases aa, ax=exln⁡aa^x=e^{x\ln a}; for a>0a>0 and a≠1a\ne1, log⁡ax=ln⁡x/ln⁡a\log_a x=\ln x/\ln a on x>0x>0. Laws such as ln⁡(xy)=ln⁡x+ln⁡y\ln(xy)=\ln x+\ln y require positive real xx and yy. The tempting formula ln⁡(x+y)=ln⁡x+ln⁡y\ln(x+y)=\ln x+\ln y is false. Negative bases raised to arbitrary real exponents are not generally real-valued, so state a positive-base assumption when using the standard continuous exponential laws.

Radians, identities, and coordinates

A radian is arc length divided by radius. It is numerically dimensionless, and 180∘=π180^\circ=\pi radians. The standard derivative of sine is cosine only when the input is a radian measure. On the unit circle, the point at angle θ\theta is (cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta); hence sin⁡2θ+cos⁡2θ=1\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1. A useful addition formula is sin⁡(u+v)=sin⁡ucos⁡v+cos⁡usin⁡v\sin(u+v)=\sin u\cos v+\cos u\sin v.

Polar coordinates relate to Cartesian coordinates by x=rcos⁡θx=r\cos\theta, y=rsin⁡θy=r\sin\theta, and x2+y2=r2x^2+y^2=r^2. Angle is not unique: adding 2π2\pi changes no point, and (−r,θ)=(r,θ+π)(-r,\theta)=(r,\theta+\pi). For polar area with r≥0r\geq0, the differential element is r dr dθr\,dr\,d\theta, because angular arcs lengthen with radius. Dimensional analysis still applies: the argument of sin⁡\sin, exp⁡\exp, or ln⁡\ln should be dimensionless after suitable scaling.

Hyperbolic functions and real inverse branches

For real tt, sinh⁡t=(et−e−t)/2\sinh t=(e^t-e^{-t})/2, cosh⁡t=(et+e−t)/2\cosh t=(e^t+e^{-t})/2, and tanh⁡t=sinh⁡t/cosh⁡t\tanh t=\sinh t/\cosh t. Thus cosh⁡2t−sinh⁡2t=1\cosh^2t-\sinh^2t=1. The ranges are sinh⁡(R)=R\sinh(\mathbb R)=\mathbb R, cosh⁡(R)=[1,∞)\cosh(\mathbb R)=[1,\infty), and tanh⁡(R)=(−1,1)\tanh(\mathbb R)=(-1,1). The hyperbolic parameter is dimensionless; it is neither a circular angle nor arc length.

The real inverses use specified branches: arsinh⁡x=ln⁡(x+1+x2)\operatorname{arsinh}x=\ln(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}) for every real xx; arcosh⁡x=ln⁡(x+x2−1)\operatorname{arcosh}x=\ln(x+\sqrt{x^2-1}) for x≥1x\ge1, returning a nonnegative value; and artanh⁡x=12ln⁡((1+x)/(1−x))\operatorname{artanh}x=\frac12\ln((1+x)/(1-x)) for −1<x<1-1<x<1.
