Appendix F · Notation and calculus glossary

Use this reference when a symbol or technical word interrupts the argument. A symbol takes its meaning from context, so each entry names the object and the chapter range where it first becomes important.

Core notation

Letters such as xx and tt usually denote variables, while aa, bb, and cc often denote fixed values within one argument. The expression f:A→Bf:A\to B says that ff maps inputs in AA to outputs in BB; the actual range may be a smaller subset of BB. Brackets [a,b][a,b] include endpoints, parentheses (a,b)(a,b) exclude them, and mixed interval notation includes exactly the bracketed endpoint.

An equality is a claim that two quantities are the same. The symbols ≈\approx, ≤\le, and ∈\in mean approximately equal, less than or equal, and is an element of. The arrow x→ax\to a describes a limiting process rather than an input ever having to equal aa. The symbol ∞\infty describes unbounded behavior and is not an ordinary real endpoint on which arithmetic rules may be used without a limit.

Derivatives, integrals, and approximation

The notations f′(x)f^{\prime}(x), df/dxdf/dx, and Df(x)D f(x) can describe the same one-variable derivative while emphasizing different roles. In a partial derivative such as ∂f/∂x\partial f/\partial x, the other independent coordinates are held fixed. A differential such as dxdx participates in linear approximation and integral notation; its meaning should come from the definition being used rather than informal cancellation alone.

The definite integral ∫abf(x) dx\int_a^b f(x)\,dx is a signed accumulation. An indefinite integral denotes an antiderivative family and needs a constant on each connected interval. The symbols O(hp)O(h^p) and o(hp)o(h^p) describe controlled asymptotic size: big O gives a constant multiple bound near the limiting point, while little o means the ratio to hph^p tends to zero.

Multivariable, vector, and numerical language

A scalar has magnitude; a vector has components and transforms as a directed quantity. The gradient ∇f\nabla f is a vector, divergence ∇⋅F\nabla\cdot F is a scalar, and curl ∇×F\nabla\times F is a vector in three dimensions. Orientation chooses a direction around a curve or a normal direction on a surface and determines the sign in the boundary theorems.

In numerical work, a step size is the input spacing used by an algorithm. Truncation error comes from replacing an exact process by finitely many terms or steps; roundoff comes from finite number representation. Consistency describes the local equation in the small-step limit, stability controls amplification of disturbances, and convergence says the computed solution approaches the intended exact solution under refinement.

Inverse hyperbolic notation

arsinh⁡\operatorname{arsinh}, arcosh⁡\operatorname{arcosh}, and artanh⁡\operatorname{artanh} denote inverse functions on the real branches stated in Appendix A. In particular, arcosh⁡\operatorname{arcosh} chooses the nonnegative input to cosh⁡\cosh, and artanh⁡\operatorname{artanh} accepts only (−1,1)(-1,1). The letters “ar” indicate inverse functions, not reciprocals.

Conic geometry

A conic compares distance to a focus with distance to a directrix by a positive, dimensionless eccentricity ee. For focus (0,0)(0,0) and directrix x=d>0x=d>0, the locus is x2+y2=e∣x−d∣\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=e|x-d|: 0<e<10<e<1 gives an ellipse, e=1e=1 a parabola, and e>1e>1 a hyperbola with two branches. A circle of radius pp is the limit e→0+e\to0^+ with ed=ped=p and d→∞d\to\infty; setting e=0e=0 at finite dd gives only the focus.

For X=x−h,Y=y−kX=x-h,Y=y-k, a horizontal ellipse X2/a2+Y2/b2=1X^2/a^2+Y^2/b^2=1, a>b>0a>b>0, has c2=a2−b2c^2=a^2-b^2. A horizontal hyperbola X2/a2−Y2/b2=1X^2/a^2-Y^2/b^2=1 has c2=a2+b2c^2=a^2+b^2, vertices X=±aX=\pm a, domains X≤−aX\le-a and X≥aX\ge a, and asymptotes Y=±(b/a)XY=\pm(b/a)X. A horizontal parabola Y2=4pXY^2=4pX, p≠0p\ne0, has focus (h+p,k)(h+p,k) and directrix x=h−px=h-p. These are translated, axis-aligned forms; a mixed XYXY term needs separate rotation analysis.

For e>1e>1, a positive radius on the focus-side branch is R=ed/(1+ecos⁡θ)R=ed/(1+e\cos\theta) where 1+ecos⁡θ>01+e\cos\theta>0; the other branch needs R=ed/(ecos⁡ϕ−1)R=ed/(e\cos\phi-1) where ecos⁡ϕ>1e\cos\phi>1. A zero denominator gives no finite point. A signed-radius convention can encode both, but its negative radius is not a negative distance.
