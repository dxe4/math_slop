Read the structure before calculating

The limit definition from Chapter 05 explains what a derivative means. Applying that definition afresh to every expression would hide the structure of a calculation beneath repeated algebra. Differentiation rules package arguments about limits so we can reuse them. Before calculating, identify the outermost operation: is the function a sum, a product, a quotient, or one function applied to another? Then record where the original expression is defined. A simplified derivative does not silently enlarge that domain.

If uu and vv are differentiable at xx and cc is constant, then (u+v)′=u′+v′(u+v)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}+v^{\prime} and (cu)′=cu′(cu)^{\prime}=cu^{\prime}. Constants have derivative zero. The power rule gives (xn)′=nxn−1(x^n)^{\prime}=nx^{n-1} for positive integers nn on the whole real line; negative integer powers require x≠0x\ne0. For an arbitrary real exponent rr, the rule (xr)′=rxr−1(x^r)^{\prime}=rx^{r-1} is valid for x>0x>0. Some rational powers have additional real domain, which must be checked separately. In particular, x1/3x^{1/3} is defined at zero but has no finite derivative there.

The product rule is (uv)′=u′v+uv′(uv)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}v+uv^{\prime}. It accounts for change in each factor while retaining the other factor. Here is a proof sketch: add and subtract u(x+h)v(x)u(x+h)v(x) in the numerator of the product difference quotient. The result is u(x+h)v(x+h)−v(x)h+v(x)u(x+h)−u(x)hu(x+h)\frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}{h}+v(x)\frac{u(x+h)-u(x)}{h}. Differentiability implies continuity, so the first unchanged factor tends to u(x)u(x), and the limit gives the rule. Multiplying the two derivatives would instead describe an entirely different expression.

For v(x)≠0v(x)\ne0, the quotient rule is (uv)′=u′v−uv′v2\left(\frac uv\right)^{\prime}=\frac{u^{\prime}v-uv^{\prime}}{v^2}. One way to remember the sign is to differentiate the identity u=(u/v)vu=(u/v)v using the product rule and solve for (u/v)′(u/v)^{\prime}. This argument applies where the quotient is differentiable; its differentiability also follows by the reciprocal limit rule. For instance, differentiating x/(x+1)x/(x+1) gives (x+1)−x(x+1)2=1(x+1)2\frac{(x+1)-x}{(x+1)^2}=\frac{1}{(x+1)^2}, with x≠−1x\ne-1. The derivative formula has the same excluded point as the original function.

For three differentiable factors, the product rule applied twice gives (uvw)′=u′vw+uv′w+uvw′(uvw)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}vw+uv^{\prime}w+uvw^{\prime}. Each term differentiates exactly one factor and leaves the others unchanged. This is easier to check than expanding a complicated product first. For (x2+1)e3xcos⁡x(x^2+1)e^{3x}\cos x, a correct derivative has three contributions: changing the polynomial, changing the exponential, and changing the cosine. Omitting any one can still produce a plausible-looking expression. Check a proposed rule at a simple input or against a difference quotient before trusting a long symbolic result.

The quotient rule can be derived from the reciprocal derivative. If v(a)≠0v(a)\ne0, then 1/v1/v is defined near aa and (1/v)′=−v′/v2(1/v)^{\prime}=-v^{\prime}/v^2. Apply the product rule to u(1/v)u(1/v) to obtain (u/v)′=u′/v−uv′/v2(u/v)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}/v-uv^{\prime}/v^2. This derivation makes the squared denominator and minus sign less arbitrary. It also explains why cancellation before differentiation can be useful: (x2−1)/(x−1)(x^2-1)/(x-1) equals x+1x+1 only where x≠1x\ne1, so its derivative is one on that punctured domain, not automatically at the missing point.

Choose the least complicated valid route through an expression. For (x2+1)(x3−2)(x^2+1)(x^3-2), expanding first is possible but obscures the two-factor structure; for (x2−1)/(x−1)(x^2-1)/(x-1), factoring first is much shorter. Neither choice changes the underlying derivative as long as every algebraic identity is used on the correct domain. A good final answer gives the original domain, an unsimplified rule application that exposes the method, and then a simplified form when it helps interpretation. This habit makes sign and domain errors easier to find.

Differentiation is local. A quotient can be differentiable at an input even if its displayed factors behave awkwardly elsewhere; conversely, a simplified derivative expression can be finite at a point excluded from the original function. For example, f(x)=x/xf(x)=x/x is the constant one on the domain x≠0x\ne0. Its derivative is zero at every point of that domain, but f′(0)f^{\prime}(0) is meaningless until a value and a new function domain are specified. Extend f(0)=1f(0)=1 and the derivative at zero becomes zero; extend it to another value and continuity, hence differentiability, fails.

The reciprocal rule also has a direct limit proof worth seeing once. At a point aa with v(a)≠0v(a)\ne0, continuity of vv supplies a neighborhood in which v(a+h)≠0v(a+h)\ne0. For nonzero hh there, the quotient for 1/v1/v is 1/v(a+h)−1/v(a)h=−v(a+h)−v(a)h v(a+h)v(a)\frac{1/v(a+h)-1/v(a)}{h}=-\frac{v(a+h)-v(a)}{h\,v(a+h)v(a)}. The difference quotient v(a+h)−v(a)h\frac{v(a+h)-v(a)}{h} tends to v′(a)v^{\prime}(a) and the denominator factors tend to v(a)2v(a)^2. Hence (1/v)′(a)=−v′(a)/v(a)2(1/v)^{\prime}(a)=-v^{\prime}(a)/v(a)^2. Applying the product rule to u(1/v)u(1/v) proves the quotient rule without memorizing a sign pattern. The continuity step is what licenses every nearby denominator and cannot be skipped merely because the final formula looks familiar. A symbolic system may simplify the result, but it does not change the original expression's domain.

Units can expose a wrong product rule before any algebra is simplified. If uu is a displacement in metres and vv is a force in newtons, their product has energy units of joules. Both terms u′vu^{\prime}v and uv′uv^{\prime} have joules per second when the input is time, as the derivative must. The tempting but false product u′v′u^{\prime}v^{\prime} has joules per second squared. This check cannot prove a formula, but it is an effective independent test of a symbolic calculation in a physical model.

Compositions, exponentials, and logarithms

Suppose gg is differentiable at xx and ff is differentiable at g(x)g(x). The chain rule says that the derivative of f(g(x))f(g(x)) is f′(g(x))g′(x)f^{\prime}(g(x))g^{\prime}(x). Think of gg as converting an input change into an intermediate change, and ff as converting that intermediate change into an output change. The corresponding rates multiply. This interpretation also checks units: if the intermediate variable has units UU, then (output/U)(U/input)(\text{output}/U)(U/\text{input}) has the required output/input units. The familiar notation dy/dx=(dy/du)(du/dx)dy/dx=(dy/du)(du/dx) expresses this theorem, rather than permission to cancel arbitrary symbols.

For (3x2+1)5(3x^2+1)^5, the outer function raises its input to the fifth power and the inner function is 3x2+13x^2+1. The derivative is 5(3x2+1)4⋅6x5(3x^2+1)^4\cdot6x. Differentiating a nest with several layers repeats this procedure from outside inward, leaving each inner expression in place until its own derivative is taken. A useful proof idea writes f(b+k)=f(b)+f′(b)k+r(k)f(b+k)=f(b)+f^{\prime}(b)k+r(k), where r(k)/k→0r(k)/k\to0 as k→0k\to0, then substitutes k=g(x+h)−g(x)k=g(x+h)-g(x). This also handles increments where kk is zero; a naive division by kk would not.

The natural exponential satisfies (ex)′=ex(e^x)^{\prime}=e^x, so (eu)′=euu′(e^u)^{\prime}=e^uu^{\prime}. For a fixed base a>0a>0, ax=exln⁡aa^x=e^{x\ln a}, giving (ax)′=axln⁡a(a^x)^{\prime}=a^x\ln a, including the constant case a=1a=1. The natural logarithm has derivative 1/x1/x on x>0x>0. Consequently, (ln⁡u)′=u′/u(\ln u)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}/u when u>0u>0, and (ln⁡∣u∣)′=u′/u(\ln|u|)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}/u on any interval where uu is differentiable and nonzero. A logarithm with base a>0a>0, a≠1a\ne1 has derivative 1/(xln⁡a)1/(x\ln a), for x>0x>0.

These are real-variable formulas. Writing ln⁡∣u∣\ln|u| does not make a zero of uu permissible, and differentiating ln⁡(x2)\ln(x^2) gives 2/x2/x only for x≠0x\ne0. In a physical model, logarithm and exponential inputs should be dimensionless: ln⁡(t/t0)\ln(t/t_0) is meaningful for positive times tt and a fixed reference time t0t_0. Its derivative with respect to tt is 1/t1/t, with units inverse time. An expression like e−kte^{-kt} needs kk in inverse-time units so that ktkt is dimensionless.

The chain rule is best read as a dependency graph. In y=exp⁡(−kt2)y=\exp(-kt^2), time changes t2t^2, that changes −kt2-kt^2, and the exponential changes the output. Differentiating from the outside gives dy/dt=exp⁡(−kt2)(−2kt)dy/dt=\exp(-kt^2)(-2kt). If tt has seconds and the exponent must be dimensionless, kk has units s−2\mathrm{s}^{-2}; the derivative then has output units per second. A symbolic answer with a missing inner factor would fail both the dependency story and often the units check.

For a deeper nest such as sin⁡(1+x2)\sin(\sqrt{1+x^2}), name intermediate values u=1+x2u=1+x^2 and v=uv=\sqrt u. Then dy/dx=cos⁡(v) [1/(2u)] (2x)=xcos⁡(1+x2)/1+x2dy/dx=\cos(v)\,[1/(2\sqrt u)]\,(2x)=x\cos(\sqrt{1+x^2})/\sqrt{1+x^2}. Writing intermediate names can prevent a common error: differentiating only the outside sine or only the inner square without following every link in the composition.

The logarithm of an absolute value extends the useful derivative identity to negative nonzero inputs. On an interval where u(x)<0u(x)<0, ln⁡∣u(x)∣=ln⁡(−u(x))\ln|u(x)|=\ln(-u(x)), whose derivative is −u′(x)−u(x)=u′(x)u(x)\frac{-u^{\prime}(x)}{-u(x)}=\frac{u^{\prime}(x)}{u(x)}. Trigonometric derivatives and inverse functions

Angles must be measured in radians for the basic formulas (sin⁡x)′=cos⁡x(\sin x)^{\prime}=\cos x and (cos⁡x)′=−sin⁡x(\cos x)^{\prime}=-\sin x. They follow from the addition identities and the limits sin⁡h/h→1\sin h/h\to1 and (cos⁡h−1)/h→0(\cos h-1)/h\to0. Define tan⁡x=sin⁡x/cos⁡x\tan x=\sin x/\cos x and sec⁡x=1/cos⁡x\sec x=1/\cos x where cos⁡x≠0\cos x\ne0; define cot⁡x=cos⁡x/sin⁡x\cot x=\cos x/\sin x and csc⁡x=1/sin⁡x\csc x=1/\sin x where sin⁡x≠0\sin x\ne0. The quotient rule now gives (tan⁡x)′=sec⁡2x(\tan x)^{\prime}=\sec^2x and (cot⁡x)′=−csc⁡2x(\cot x)^{\prime}=-\csc^2x on their respective domains. Reciprocal differentiation gives (sec⁡x)′=sec⁡xtan⁡x(\sec x)^{\prime}=\sec x\tan x and (csc⁡x)′=−csc⁡xcot⁡x(\csc x)^{\prime}=-\csc x\cot x on those same respective domains. If a numerical angle dd is in degrees, sin⁡(πd/180)\sin(\pi d/180) has derivative (π/180)cos⁡(πd/180)(\pi/180)\cos(\pi d/180) with respect to dd.

An inverse function reverses the input and output of a one-to-one function. Let ff be continuously differentiable near aa, with f′(a)≠0f^{\prime}(a)\ne0. On a sufficiently small interval about aa, ff has a differentiable inverse gg near b=f(a)b=f(a), and g′(b)=1/f′(a)g^{\prime}(b)=1/f^{\prime}(a). To understand the formula, differentiate f(g(y))=yf(g(y))=y: the chain rule gives f′(g(y))g′(y)=1f^{\prime}(g(y))g^{\prime}(y)=1. Why an inverse exists here uses one interval-wide step: continuity of f′f^{\prime} keeps its nonzero sign on a smaller interval, and the mean value theorem, proved in Chapter 08 Lesson 2, makes ff strictly monotone there. This is an explicit preview of that theorem; the identity alone does not establish existence. Reflecting a tangent in the line y=xy=x exchanges its rise and run.

The inverse sine arcsin⁡\arcsin has values in [−π/2,π/2][-\pi/2,\pi/2] and input domain [−1,1][-1,1]. If y=arcsin⁡xy=\arcsin x, then sin⁡y=x\sin y=x and cos⁡y⋅y′=1\cos y\cdot y^{\prime}=1. On the interior, cos⁡y\cos y is positive, so cos⁡y=1−x2\cos y=\sqrt{1-x^2} and (arcsin⁡x)′=1/1−x2(\arcsin x)^{\prime}=1/\sqrt{1-x^2}. The derivative is finite only for −1<x<1-1<x<1; the endpoint slopes diverge. Similarly, arccos⁡\arccos takes values in [0,π][0,\pi] and has derivative −1/1−x2-1/\sqrt{1-x^2} on that open interval. The chosen branches determine the signs.

For y=arctan⁡xy=\arctan x, the range (−π/2,π/2)(-\pi/2,\pi/2) makes tangent one-to-one. Differentiating tan⁡y=x\tan y=x gives sec⁡2y⋅y′=1\sec^2y\cdot y^{\prime}=1, hence (arctan⁡x)′=1/(1+x2)(\arctan x)^{\prime}=1/(1+x^2) for every real xx. For arccot⁡\operatorname{arccot} with range (0,π)(0,\pi), the derivative is −1/(1+x2)-1/(1+x^2). Always specify branches when these less common inverse functions appear.

The radian factor can be derived by changing units. If dd is a number of degrees, the corresponding radian angle is θ=πd/180\theta=\pi d/180. Therefore d[sin⁡(πd/180)]/dd=(π/180)cos⁡(πd/180)d[\sin(\pi d/180)]/dd=(\pi/180)\cos(\pi d/180). The familiar derivative of sine is cosine only when the numerical input is a radian measure. This is not a cosmetic convention: the derivative records output change per input unit, so changing degrees to radians changes its numerical value by the conversion factor. The same check applies to trigonometric models of angular motion.

Inverse differentiation must be applied at the correct corresponding points. If f(1)=3f(1)=3 and f′(1)=5f^{\prime}(1)=5 for a locally invertible function, then (f−1)′(3)=1/5(f^{-1})^{\prime}(3)=1/5, not (f−1)′(1)=1/5(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1)=1/5. The inverse swaps input and output. The reciprocal formula also requires the original derivative to be nonzero at the point. For f(x)=x3f(x)=x^3 at zero, the inverse is the real cube root, which exists as a function, but has no finite derivative at zero. One-to-one behavior alone does not guarantee a finite inverse slope.

For inverse sine, the branch [−π/2,π/2][-\pi/2,\pi/2] ensures cos⁡y≥0\cos y\ge0. In the open interior it is strictly positive, allowing cos⁡y=1−sin⁡2y\cos y=\sqrt{1-\sin^2 y} and the derivative 1/1−x21/\sqrt{1-x^2}. By contrast, arctan⁡x\arctan x has derivative 1/(1+x2)1/(1+x^2) for every real input, since its tangent branch has no finite endpoint of the input domain. Writing the domain beside each inverse formula prevents a rule from being applied at a branch boundary.

Trigonometric products combine several rules. For y=e−2tcos⁡(3t)y=e^{-2t}\cos(3t), the product rule gives one contribution from decay and one from oscillation: y′=e−2t[−2cos⁡(3t)−3sin⁡(3t)]y^{\prime}=e^{-2t}[-2\cos(3t)-3\sin(3t)]. The negative signs have different origins: decay has derivative −2e−2t-2e^{-2t} and cosine has derivative −sin⁡(3t)-\sin(3t) times inner rate three. A proposed derivative can be checked at t=0t=0: the original function initially equals one and its rate is −2-2, matching the displayed formula.

The reciprocal slope formula for an inverse can also be read from difference quotients. Let b=f(a)b=f(a) and g=f−1g=f^{-1} on a suitable local interval. That supporting fact is why a zero derivative or a branch choice cannot be ignored. At an endpoint, a one-sided version may apply, but it is not the ordinary interior theorem.

The two basic trigonometric limits have a geometric explanation in radians. For a small positive angle hh inside the first quadrant, comparing a unit-circle sector with its inscribed and circumscribed triangles gives sin⁡h<h<tan⁡h\sin h<h<\tan h. Rearranging yields cos⁡h<sin⁡h/h<1\cos h<\sin h/h<1. Because 0≤sin⁡h≤h0\le\sin h\le h, the identity 1−cos⁡h=sin⁡2h/(1+cos⁡h)1-\cos h=\sin^2h/(1+\cos h) gives 0≤1−cos⁡h≤h20\le1-\cos h\le h^2. Thus cos⁡h→1\cos h\to1 independently, and the squeeze theorem proves sin⁡h/h→1\sin h/h\to1 from the right; symmetry gives the same left limit. Also (cos⁡h−1)/h=−sin⁡2h/[h(1+cos⁡h)](\cos h-1)/h=-\sin^2h/[h(1+\cos h)], whose magnitude tends to zero because one sine factor divided by hh stays bounded while the other tends to zero. These limits establish the derivatives of sine and cosine from the difference quotient and addition formulas. Before applying a differentiation rule, record the domain of the original composition. For a logarithm of an inner expression, the inner expression must be positive; for a reciprocal it must be nonzero; for an inverse trigonometric function the input must lie in its permitted domain. The chain rule is also a unit check. If an inner map converts time to distance and an outer map converts distance to energy, multiplying their derivatives gives energy per time. A missing inner factor has both the wrong numerical value and the wrong units. When the inner derivative vanishes, the composite derivative may vanish even if the outer derivative does not. Conversely, an undefined outer derivative can sometimes be masked by a vanishing inner change, so the standard chain-rule hypotheses should be checked before quoting the formula.
