1. One relationship, several changing quantities

Suppose a nearly spherical balloon expands. A camera measures its radius, but we want its volume and the rate at which that volume grows. The geometry gives V=(4π/3)r3V=(4\pi/3)r^3. This is a relationship between quantities, not yet between rates. If rr is a differentiable function of time tt, the chain rule turns it into dV/dt=4πr2 dr/dtdV/dt=4\pi r^2\,dr/dt. Both the current radius and its current rate of change matter. Two balloons whose radii increase equally quickly need not gain equal volumes each second.

A related-rates calculation starts by identifying what varies and writing a relationship that holds throughout an interval of motion. Then differentiate with respect to a common independent variable, usually time. Only after differentiating should you insert values that are true at a particular instant. Substituting r=10r=10 before differentiation would turn a changing radius into a constant and destroy the rate information. A quantity genuinely fixed throughout the motion, such as a rigid ladder's length, can be substituted immediately.

Units provide a useful audit. If radius is in centimetres and time in seconds, then 4πr24\pi r^2 has units cm2\mathrm{cm}^2 and dr/dtdr/dt has units cm/s\mathrm{cm}/\mathrm{s}. Their product has units cm3/s\mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}, as a volume rate should. The derivative dV/drdV/dr and the derivative dV/dtdV/dt answer different questions: one measures volume gained per unit radius, the other volume gained per unit time. A sign is information too. A negative dr/dtdr/dt describes shrinking; for a positive radius it produces a negative volume rate.

The equation assumes the balloon remains spherical and that the measured radius describes its whole shape. A changing ellipsoid cannot generally be represented by the same single radius. Nor is geometric volume automatically the amount of gas: pressure and temperature can change. Before differentiating, say what the variables measure and which physical effects the model neglects. Calculus transfers the consequences of an assumed relationship; it does not establish that relationship from observations.

A fixed geometric constraint can eliminate a variable before differentiation. For a cone whose water surface stays similar to the container, the radius and height may satisfy r=h/2r=h/2. Substituting into V=πr2h/3V=\pi r^2h/3 gives V=πh3/12V=\pi h^3/12 and therefore dV/dt=(πh2/4) dh/dtdV/dt=(\pi h^2/4)\,dh/dt. If one instead differentiates the three-variable formula, both rr and hh vary and both derivative terms must appear; inserting r=h/2r=h/2 afterward gives the same result. Substituting a relationship that holds for all times is valid. Substituting a one-time measured height before differentiating is not.

Related-rates equations inherit domain and sign restrictions. A rigid ladder of length LL against a wall satisfies x2+y2=L2x^2+y^2=L^2, with x,y≥0x,y\ge0 while it stays in contact. Differentiation gives xx′+yy′=0x x^{\prime}+y y^{\prime}=0. If the foot moves outward, x′>0x^{\prime}>0 and the top moves downward, y′<0y^{\prime}<0 wherever y>0y>0. At y=0y=0 the formula for y′y^{\prime} divides by zero and the idealized ladder reaches a boundary of this configuration. A rate calculated just before that event need not remain valid after contact is lost or the geometry changes.

Use a diagram to decide what is changing, but let equations—not visual resemblance—determine the rates. An increasing radius and an increasing volume are linked by the chain rule; their numerical rates need not be equal. A cylindrical tank with fixed cross-sectional area AA satisfies V=AhV=Ah, so V′=Ah′V^{\prime}=Ah^{\prime}. If the tank narrows or widens with height, AA is no longer a constant and this relation fails. A model of shape is part of the mathematics, not a preface that can be discarded once differentiation starts.

Measurements at one instant determine only instantaneous conclusions when paired with an exact relation. Knowing a sphere's radius and radius rate at noon gives its volume rate at noon, not its volume tomorrow. Forecasting a finite change requires information about how the radius rate evolves. If it is constant over a specified interval, integrate or use the resulting radius function; otherwise report the derivative as a local rate. This distinction prevents a common misuse of related rates: multiplying an instantaneous rate by a long duration as if the rate had been proved constant.

The conical-tank rate can be checked without eliminating the radius first. Differentiate V=πr2h/3V=\pi r^2h/3 while both rr and hh depend on time: dVdt=π3(2rhdrdt+r2dhdt)\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\pi}{3}\left(2rh\frac{dr}{dt}+r^2\frac{dh}{dt}\right). Similar triangles give r=h/2r=h/2 and drdt=12dhdt\frac{dr}{dt}=\frac12\frac{dh}{dt} at every instant. Substitution reduces the expression to πh24dhdt\frac{\pi h^2}{4}\frac{dh}{dt}, exactly the result from V=πh3/12V=\pi h^3/12. The agreement is an algebraic cross-check. Keeping r=h/2r=h/2 but incorrectly setting dr/dt=0dr/dt=0 would omit a real contribution from the expanding water surface. This example shows why every variable in an uneliminated relation must be treated as changing unless a physical constraint makes it constant.

2. The derivative supplies a local replacement

Let ff be differentiable at an interior point aa of its domain. For a small increment hh, its derivative definition says that [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h[f(a+h)-f(a)]/h approaches f′(a)f^{\prime}(a). Rearranging suggests replacing f(a+h)f(a+h) by f(a)+f′(a)hf(a)+f^{\prime}(a)h. This tangent-line model is called the linearization at aa. As a function of xx, it is L(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)L(x)=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)(x-a). It agrees with ff in both value and slope at aa, but generally not at other points.

Here is the precise local guarantee, obtained directly from differentiability: f(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)h+hε(h)f(a+h)=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)h+h\varepsilon(h), where ε(h)→0\varepsilon(h)\to0 as h→0h\to0. Thus the absolute discrepancy divided by ∣h∣|h| tends to zero. This does not supply a numerical error tolerance for a particular hh, nor does it promise that every visually small increment is small enough for your application. The relevant scale depends on the function and the point.

The notation dxdx denotes an input increment in the linear model, and dy=f′(a) dxdy=f^{\prime}(a)\,dx is its predicted output increment. Taking dx=Δxdx=\Delta x gives dy≈Δydy\approx\Delta y, where Δy=f(a+Δx)−f(a)\Delta y=f(a+\Delta x)-f(a) is the actual change. The definition of dydy is exact; its use as a replacement for Δy\Delta y is approximate. These symbols help organize changes without requiring us to treat dxdx as a mysterious number smaller than all ordinary positive numbers.

For a dimensionless example, take f(x)=xf(x)=\sqrt{x} at a=4a=4. Since f(4)=2f(4)=2 and f′(4)=1/4f^{\prime}(4)=1/4, 4.08≈2+0.08/4=2.02\sqrt{4.08}\approx2+0.08/4=2.02. Squaring 2.022.02 gives 4.08044.0804, which already shows that the estimate is slightly high. The exact square root is approximately 2.0199009882.019900988, so the absolute output error is about 0.0000990120.000099012. An approximation becomes useful when this discrepancy is acceptable for the task, not merely when the decimals look close.

Differentiability promises a first-order approximation, not a universal absolute error. The exact statement f(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)h+o(h)f(a+h)=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)h+o(h) says the error divided by ∣h∣|h| tends to zero. It does not say the error is always smaller than h2h^2; a function can be differentiable yet have a remainder whose size lies between ∣h∣|h| and h2h^2 in order. For example, f(x)=∣x∣3/2f(x)=|x|^{3/2} has derivative zero at the origin and tangent model zero there, but its exact error is ∣h∣3/2|h|^{3/2}. That is o(∣h∣)o(|h|) while it is not bounded by a fixed multiple of h2h^2 near zero.

Here is the compactness step behind the theorem bridge. Take any sequence of inputs in a closed bounded interval [u,v][u,v]. Bisect the interval and keep a closed half containing infinitely many terms; repeat inside that half. The halves are nested and their widths tend to zero. Chapter 04's nested-interval argument, which uses the least-upper-bound completeness of R\mathbb R, gives one common point c∈[u,v]c\in[u,v]. From the kkth retained half choose a sequence term with index larger than the previously chosen index; infinitely many terms there make this possible. Its distance from cc is at most that half's width, so these chosen terms form a subsequence converging to cc. This proves the compactness property used here, including retention of the limit in the original closed interval. If a continuous function on [u,v][u,v] were unbounded, inputs with function-value magnitudes exceeding successive integers would have such a convergent subsequence, contradicting continuity at its limit. Its values are therefore bounded.

Choose inputs whose values approach their supremum; another convergent subsequence and continuity show that the supremum is attained. Repeat for the infimum: this is the extreme value theorem. At an interior extremum where a derivative exists, the right and left difference quotients force that derivative to be zero. A continuous function on [u,v][u,v] differentiable on (u,v)(u,v) with equal endpoint values is then either constant or has an interior extremum, proving Rolle's theorem. For a nondegenerate interval u<vu<v, subtracting the secant line from a general ff gives equal endpoint values and yields f′(c)=[f(v)−f(u)]/(v−u)f'(c)=[f(v)-f(u)]/(v-u) for some interior cc: the mean value theorem. Each use keeps closed-interval continuity and interior differentiability. Chapter 08 revisits these results and their applications.

The differential has units. If ff maps metres to square metres, then f′(a)f^{\prime}(a) has metres and dy=f′(a) dxdy=f^{\prime}(a)\,dx has square metres. The tangent estimate can be on either side of the true value. For a convex function such as x2x^2, the tangent at aa lies below the curve: f(a+h)−[f(a)+f′(a)h]=h2≥0f(a+h)-[f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)h]=h^2\ge0. For the square-root model, an exact remainder is available without invoking a general theorem. Let s=4+hs=\sqrt{4+h} with h>−4h>-4. Rationalization gives s−2=h/(s+2)s-2=h/(s+2). 3. Measurement errors and guaranteed intervals

Suppose an instrument reports x=ax=a and the true input differs by an unknown δ\delta with ∣δ∣≤η|\delta|\le\eta. The number η\eta is an absolute uncertainty bound in the input's units. The differential estimate predicts an output uncertainty of approximately ∣f′(a)∣η|f^{\prime}(a)|\eta. This is a worst-case first-order estimate, not automatically a rigorous bound and not a probability statement. A tolerance interval does not say that errors are normally distributed or that a particular confidence level applies.

When the model is continuous and monotone over the entire closed allowed input interval, evaluating its endpoints gives its exact output range within that model. Monotonicity alone gives sharp endpoint bounds, but continuity ensures that every value between them is attained. For a sphere measured as r=10.00 cmr=10.00\,\mathrm{cm} with ∣δr∣≤0.05 cm|\delta r|\le0.05\,\mathrm{cm}, the permitted radius interval is [9.95,10.05] cm[9.95,10.05]\,\mathrm{cm}. Since r3r^3 increases for positive rr, the volume lies between (4π/3)(9.95)3 cm3(4\pi/3)(9.95)^3\,\mathrm{cm}^3 and (4π/3)(10.05)3 cm3(4\pi/3)(10.05)^3\,\mathrm{cm}^3. This interval is slightly asymmetric about the volume computed from 10.00 cm10.00\,\mathrm{cm}. Nonlinearity produces that asymmetry even when the input tolerance is symmetric.

For the sphere, expanding the cubic makes the omitted terms visible: ΔV=4πr2h+4πrh2+(4π/3)h3\Delta V=4\pi r^2h+4\pi rh^2+(4\pi/3)h^3. The differential keeps only the first term. If ∣h∣≤η|h|\le\eta and r>ηr>\eta, the absolute remainder is at most 4πrη2+(4π/3)η34\pi r\eta^2+(4\pi/3)\eta^3. This algebraic bound needs no numerical sampling. It also explains why reducing the radius tolerance by a factor of ten makes the leading approximation error roughly one hundred times smaller.

Relative uncertainty compares an absolute uncertainty with the size of the reported quantity. It is meaningful only when that reference value is nonzero. For the sphere, dV/V=3 dr/rdV/V=3\,dr/r, so a small 0.5%0.5\% radius uncertainty produces approximately 1.5%1.5\% volume uncertainty. A better instrument cannot remove uncertainty caused by a poor geometric model. Report measurement error and model error separately; a precisely measured nonspherical object can still have an inaccurately inferred spherical volume.

An uncertainty interval is a set of possible true inputs, not a probability distribution. If a radius is reported as 2.00±0.01 m2.00\pm0.01\,\mathrm{m} under an absolute tolerance specification, any radius in [1.99,2.01] m[1.99,2.01]\,\mathrm{m} is allowed. There is no basis from that statement alone to call the midpoint most likely or to assign a 95% confidence level. To propagate the interval through a monotone function, evaluate the endpoints exactly. For a nonmonotone function, endpoint evaluation may miss an interior maximum or minimum; one must also inspect interior critical points or use another bound.

For a square's area A(s)=s2A(s)=s^2, with side s=5±0.02 ms=5\pm0.02\,\mathrm{m}, the exact allowed area interval is [4.982,5.022]=[24.8004,25.2004] m2[4.98^2,5.02^2]=[24.8004,25.2004]\,\mathrm{m}^2. 4. Sensitivity, elasticity, and where the model fails

The derivative f′(a)f^{\prime}(a) is a signed local sensitivity: near aa, an extra unit of input produces approximately f′(a)f^{\prime}(a) units of output. Its magnitude measures response size, while its sign gives response direction. Absolute sensitivity depends on units. Reporting volume in cubic millimetres rather than cubic centimetres multiplies its numerical derivative by one thousand, although the physical response has not changed. Comparing bare derivative magnitudes across unrelated measurement conventions can therefore be misleading.

When xx and f(x)f(x) are nonzero, define elasticity E(x)=xf′(x)/f(x)E(x)=xf^{\prime}(x)/f(x). It compares fractional output change with fractional input change: Δf/f(x)≈E(x)Δx/x\Delta f/f(x)\approx E(x)\Delta x/x. For positive xx and positive output, it is also the derivative of log⁡f\log f with respect to log⁡x\log x. A power law f(x)=Cxpf(x)=Cx^p with C>0C>0 and x>0x>0 has constant elasticity pp. Sphere volume has elasticity 3, and circle area has elasticity 2. Multiplicative changes of measurement units leave these elasticities unchanged.

As an illustrative demand model, let Q(p)=Cp−2Q(p)=Cp^{-2} for price p>0p>0, with CC fixed and positive. Its signed elasticity is −2-2: a small 1% price increase predicts approximately a 2% quantity decrease. Some economic conventions report the positive magnitude of price elasticity instead, so state your sign convention. A finite 10% increase actually multiplies quantity by 1/1.121/1.1^2, about 0.826450.82645, a decrease of about 17.36%. The differential's 20% decrease is a local estimate extended too far to be exact.

Small derivatives do not always mean zero uncertainty. At a=0a=0, f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 has f′(0)=0f^{\prime}(0)=0, so the first-order prediction vanishes while an input error δ\delta produces the positive output δ2\delta^2. Relative output error based on f(0)f(0) is undefined. At a corner such as ∣x∣|x| at zero, no single tangent slope supplies a two-sided linearization. At a physical endpoint, only allowed one-sided changes may make sense. These examples tell us to check differentiability, domain restrictions, nonzero denominators, and neglected higher-order terms before trusting a sensitivity calculation.

Elasticity can be derived by differentiating logarithms on a positive domain. If x>0x>0 and f(x)>0f(x)>0, then d(ln⁡f)/d(ln⁡x)=[f′(x)/f(x)]/[1/x]=xf′(x)/f(x)d(\ln f)/d(\ln x)=[f^{\prime}(x)/f(x)]/[1/x]=xf^{\prime}(x)/f(x). This is a local ratio of percentage rates, not a fixed percentage rule over every finite interval. If f(x)=Cxpf(x)=Cx^p, the exact finite relation is f(x(1+ρ))/f(x)=(1+ρ)pf(x(1+\rho))/f(x)=(1+\rho)^p, whereas elasticity predicts the first-order ratio 1+pρ1+p\rho. Their difference is governed by higher powers of ρ\rho.

A negative elasticity records opposite directions of change. For Q(p)=Cp−2Q(p)=Cp^{-2}, raising price by 10% changes quantity by the exact factor 1/1.12≈0.826451/1.1^2\approx0.82645. The local differential predicts a factor 1−2(0.1)=0.81-2(0.1)=0.8, a larger decrease. Halving the percentage change generally reduces the discrepancy, but the sign and size of the error still depend on the model and interval. Do not substitute a large percentage into a derivative formula without checking the exact finite relation when it is available.

Sensitivity can blow up near a boundary. For f(x)=xf(x)=\sqrt{x}, f′(x)=1/(2x)f^{\prime}(x)=1/(2\sqrt{x}) grows without bound as x↓0x\downarrow0, even though f(x)f(x) remains continuous and small. A fixed absolute input uncertainty can have a much larger first-order output effect when the nominal input is close to zero. At exactly zero there is no finite right derivative. Similarly, 1/x1/x becomes highly sensitive near its excluded point zero. A reported nominal value close to a domain boundary should trigger a direct interval calculation rather than blind use of a tangent.

A first-order sensitivity may vanish without the model being insensitive. At x=0x=0, the function x2x^2 has derivative zero, yet an uncertainty interval [−η,η][-\eta,\eta] produces output range [0,η2][0,\eta^2]. The correct conclusion is that the linear contribution vanishes and the leading change is second order. This distinction matters in optimization too: a stationary point may be a minimum, maximum, or neither. The derivative identifies the missing linear term; it does not classify the remaining terms by itself.

Power-law elasticity has a transparent finite-change error. At a measured input aa, the linearization L(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)hL(a+h)=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)h predicts a changed output. Chapter synthesis

  • Related rates come from differentiating a relationship valid throughout the motion, with respect to one common variable.
  • Linearization uses the tangent slope to estimate nearby values; a differential is the linear model's change, not the full change.
  • Absolute sensitivity carries units, while elasticity compares fractional changes and requires nonzero reference values.
  • Measurement tolerance, approximation remainder, and model inadequacy are distinct sources of uncertainty.

Common mistakes to check

  • ‘dydy and Δy\Delta y are the same.’ The differential is the exact change in the tangent model; the function's actual change includes a remainder. For f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 at x=1x=1, taking dx=Δx=0.1dx=\Delta x=0.1 gives dy=0.2dy=0.2 but Δy=0.21\Delta y=0.21. Test with problem 10
  • ‘A symmetric input tolerance gives an exactly symmetric output tolerance.’ Nonlinear transformations can make the two endpoint deviations unequal, as sphere volume demonstrates. Test with problem 12
  • ‘Elasticity 3 means the output rises by three units.’ For a differentiable model at a positive input with positive output, elasticity 3 predicts that a small 1% input increase gives roughly a 3% output increase; this percentage comparison is unchanged by multiplicative unit conversions.
