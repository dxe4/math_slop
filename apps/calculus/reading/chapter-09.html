From a practical question to an objective

Optimization asks for the largest or smallest value of a quantity among admissible choices. The objective is the quantity being compared; constraints describe the admissible, or feasible, choices. Before differentiating, define variables and units, translate the constraints, and state the domain. Maximizing area under a fixed perimeter is different from maximizing area with no size restriction. In the latter problem there may be no largest area at all.

A constraint often eliminates a variable. If a rectangular enclosure uses 100 m of fencing on three sides beside a straight wall, let xx metres be its width perpendicular to the wall and yy metres its length along the wall. Then 2x+y=1002x+y=100, so its area is A(x)=x(100−2x)A(x)=x(100-2x) square metres. Positive dimensions require 0<x<500<x<50. Including the degenerate endpoints gives a convenient continuous extension to [0,50][0,50]; we must subsequently verify that the maximizing design has positive dimensions.

The model assumes a straight wall long enough for the design, negligible fence thickness, no gate allowance, and a fixed fence supply. Calculus can optimize this model exactly without making those assumptions true. A useful conclusion therefore includes both a mathematical certificate and a statement about the design it describes. If a regulation imposes a minimum width, that additional boundary belongs in the feasible set before any comparison is made.

A reliable translation starts with a sentence: “Choose xx in this interval to make this measurable quantity as large as possible.” That sentence forces us to separate choices from outcomes. In the fence problem, xx is a width in metres and A(x)A(x) is an area in square metres. The equality 2x+y=1002x+y=100 is a resource constraint, while x>0x>0 and y>0y>0 are feasibility constraints. If the wall is only 40 m long, a further restriction y≤40y\le 40 changes the answer; algebraically it requires x≥30x\ge 30. The calculus on the old interval remains correct, but it answers the wrong design question. Recheck the feasible set whenever a real condition changes.

A diagram is often the quickest way to expose a mistaken formula. In the enclosure, only three sides need fence; charging for the wall side would replace 2x+y=1002x+y=100 by 2x+2y=1002x+2y=100 and solve a different problem. Once the formula is written, check limiting cases before differentiating: xx near zero makes a narrow enclosure with almost no area, while xx near 5050 makes yy nearly zero. The area expression A(x)=100x−2x2A(x)=100x-2x^2 is nonnegative on the feasible interval and has square-metre units because each term multiplies two lengths. These checks do not prove the optimum, but they catch many modeling errors cheaply.

Equality constraints sometimes offer several valid parameterizations. Solving the fence equation for xx gives x=(100−y)/2x=(100-y)/2 and area B(y)=y(100−y)/2B(y)=y(100-y)/2 for 0<y<1000<y<100. Its derivative vanishes at y=50y=50, the same design as x=25x=25. The numerical coordinates differ because the decision variable changed; the physical dimensions and 1250 m21250\,\mathrm{m}^2 maximum do not. If two parameterizations give different designs, inspect their domains and algebra. This is a useful independent cross-check, especially when a more complicated model includes a cost per metre, a fixed width, or a minimum required area.

Optimization is more than setting a derivative to zero. The mathematical problem has a set SS of allowable inputs and an objective F:S→RF:S\to\mathbb{R}. A maximum is a feasible x∗x^* with F(x∗)≥F(x)F(x^*)\ge F(x) for every x∈Sx\in S. A stationary point only compares behavior infinitesimally close to one input. In a practical report, state the selected input, the objective value, the units, and whether the solution lies on a constraint boundary. These are different deliverables: a correct area without the corresponding fence lengths cannot be built, and a plausible fence layout without a global comparison need not be best.

A global certificate on a closed interval

The extreme value theorem says that a continuous real-valued function on a closed bounded interval [a,b][a,b] attains both its maximum and its minimum. It guarantees existence, not the location. At any interior local extremum where the derivative exists, the derivative is zero. Consequently global extrema can occur at stationary points, interior points where the derivative does not exist, or endpoints. Endpoints do not need horizontal tangents.

The closed-interval method combines these facts: check continuity and the interval; find all interior critical points; evaluate the objective at these points and at both endpoints; compare the resulting values. For the functions in our examples the candidate list is finite. A function can instead have infinitely many critical points, in which case a sign argument or another global comparison may be needed. Solving f′(x)=0f^{\prime}(x)=0 is only one part of optimization.

For example, f(x)=∣x−1∣f(x)=|x-1| on [0,3][0,3] has its minimum at the nondifferentiable point x=1x=1 and its maximum at x=3x=3. Ignoring corners loses the minimum; ignoring boundaries loses the maximum. A second derivative test can classify an interior stationary point but cannot replace the comparison needed to establish a global result.

If the feasible set is open or unbounded, the existence guarantee no longer applies. The function f(x)=xf(x)=x on (0,1)(0,1) has supremum 1 but no maximum, because 1 is approached and never attained. Likewise a singular objective needs separate analysis near excluded points. When we extend a physical model to a closed interval, an extremum at a degenerate endpoint may be only a limiting value, not a usable design.

Why must an interior differentiable maximum have derivative zero? Suppose ff has a local maximum at cc. For small positive hh, f(c+h)−f(c)≤0f(c+h)-f(c)\le 0, so the right difference quotients are nonpositive. For small negative hh, division reverses the inequality, so the left quotients are nonnegative. If one two-sided derivative exists, its common value must be both nonpositive and nonnegative, hence zero. The same reasoning holds for a local minimum with reversed inequalities. The theorem says nothing about an endpoint because only one side of the interval is available there. It also says nothing about a corner where the derivative fails to exist.

A critical number is a point in the interior of the domain where f′f^{\prime} is zero or fails to exist. Domain membership matters: the expression f′f^{\prime} may be undefined at a vertical asymptote, but a point where ff itself is absent is not a critical number. For f(x)=x2/3f(x)=x^{2/3} on [−1,1][-1,1], x=0x=0 is in the domain and is a cusp with a minimum of zero. For f(x)=1/xf(x)=1/x on [−1,1][-1,1] excluding zero, the origin is not a candidate value at all; the pieces require separate analysis and the function is unbounded near zero. A candidate list is only meaningful after the domain is fixed.

The extreme value theorem needs both continuity and a compact interval. Removing either condition can destroy attainment. On [0,1)[0,1), f(x)=xf(x)=x approaches one but has no maximum. On [0,1][0,1], define g(0)=0g(0)=0 and g(x)=1/xg(x)=1/x for x>0x>0; the interval is closed, but gg is discontinuous and has no finite maximum. Conversely, a function can attain an optimum without those hypotheses: x2x^2 has an absolute minimum at zero on all real numbers. Thus the theorem is a guarantee under sufficient conditions, not an “if and only if” test for every particular function.

The candidate method can be strengthened by monotonicity. For ff continuous on [a,b][a,b], if f′>0f^{\prime}>0 everywhere in (a,b)(a,b), the mean value theorem makes ff strictly increasing, so its minimum and maximum on [a,b][a,b] occur at aa and bb. If f′f^{\prime} changes from positive to negative at cc and there are no other changes, ff rises to cc and then falls, making cc a global maximum on the interval. This derivative-sign argument is often clearer than a large table of values. Still compare limits at excluded boundaries on open intervals, and check discontinuities separately; a sign chart cannot silently bridge a hole in the domain.

Bisection: preserve a bracket and measure uncertainty

Root finding seeks rr with f(r)=0f(r)=0. It enters optimization when solving an equation such as A′(x)=0A^{\prime}(x)=0, but also occurs whenever two quantities must agree. If ff is continuous on [a,b][a,b] and f(a)f(a) and f(b)f(b) have opposite signs, the intermediate value theorem guarantees at least one root inside. Check first whether an endpoint is already a root. Continuity is essential: 1/x1/x changes sign across zero but has no root there.

Bisection evaluates the midpoint m=a+(b−a)/2m=a+(b-a)/2. If f(m)=0f(m)=0, the mathematical problem is solved. Otherwise retain the half whose endpoints have opposite signs. The root remains bracketed after each update, and the interval width halves. There may be several roots initially; the method guarantees convergence to a root, not a prescribed choice among them. A monotonicity argument can establish uniqueness separately.

After nn completed halvings of an initial interval of length LL, the remaining width is L/2nL/2^n. The midpoint of that remaining interval is within L/2n+1L/2^{n+1} of a root. Thus a requested absolute error ε\varepsilon in xx has a direct stopping rule: stop when half the width is at most ε\varepsilon. This bound measures uncertainty in the input variable, with that variable’s units. It is different from the residual ∣f(x)∣|f(x)|, which has the units of the output.

Finite precision eventually prevents further halving when the computed midpoint equals an endpoint. Software should detect that event and report stagnation. A maximum iteration count protects edited experiments too. A sign change is sufficient but not necessary for a root: f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 has a root at zero without changing sign, so an ordinary sign-bracketing search across zero cannot start. This is a limitation of this method, not evidence that no root exists.

The bisection invariant is a promise maintained at every step: aa and bb are ordered, ff is continuous on [a,b][a,b], and either an endpoint is a root or f(a)f(a) and f(b)f(b) have opposite signs. The midpoint evaluation decides which half can inherit that promise. If f(a)f(a) and f(m)f(m) have opposite signs, keep [a,m][a,m]; otherwise keep [m,b][m,b], provided f(m)f(m) is not zero. This is an example of an algorithm with a mathematical certificate attached to its state. Merely plotting a curve that seems to cross the axis gives no comparable bound on where the root lies.

The width guarantee is independent of how steep ff is. Starting from [1,2][1,2], after ten completed halvings the retained width is 1/10241/1024 and its midpoint is within 1/20481/2048 of some root in that interval. The residual at the midpoint may be much larger or much smaller than this input error, depending on vertical scaling. Multiplying ff by a million leaves the root and bisection decisions unchanged but multiplies every residual by a million. Thus choose stopping rules in the units that matter: a tolerance in xx for a physical parameter, perhaps also a tolerance in ff for an engineering balance equation.

When ff has multiple roots in the initial bracket, bisection still encloses at least one root but need not return a particular one. For f(x)=x3−xf(x)=x^3-x on [−2,2][-2,2], the midpoint is exactly the root zero; another implementation with a slightly different bracket could isolate −1-1 or 11. If the task asks for all roots, first separate the domain into monotonic pieces or scan for candidate brackets and then certify each. A sign scan by itself can miss even-multiplicity roots such as the zero of x2x^2, so derivative information or analytic factorization may be needed.

Floating-point arithmetic adds a practical limit. Computing m=a+(b−a)/2m=a+(b-a)/2 avoids some overflow that can occur in (a+b)/2(a+b)/2, but b−ab-a can still overflow for extreme finite endpoints, and eventually mm rounds to aa or bb. Stop on that stagnation rather than reporting unlimited precision. A displayed interval is certified only if function evaluations and signs are reliable at the chosen precision; cancellation or uncertain data can undermine the invariant. For the elementary polynomials in this chapter, ordinary double precision is adequate well before stagnation, but numerical libraries sometimes use interval arithmetic when rigorous machine-certified bounds are required.

Newton iteration: let the tangent suggest the next point

At an estimate xnx_n, replace ff locally by its tangent model f(xn)+f′(xn)(x−xn)f(x_n)+f^{\prime}(x_n)(x-x_n). Setting that model to zero gives Newton’s update. Unlike bisection, the step uses the local slope and does not maintain an enclosing interval. A tangent that crosses the axis close to the true root can make a dramatic improvement; a misleading tangent can send the next estimate outside the function’s domain.

For a twice continuously differentiable function near a simple root rr, where f′(r)≠0f^{\prime}(r)\ne 0, all sufficiently close initial guesses converge quadratically: the next error is bounded by a constant times the square of the current error. This is a local statement. It does not promise convergence from every starting point. At a repeated root the derivative vanishes at the root, and ordinary Newton iteration generally loses its quadratic rate. For f(x)=(x−1)2f(x)=(x-1)^2, the error is merely halved at each step.

Newton can fail conspicuously. For f(x)=x3−2x+2f(x)=x^3-2x+2, starting at 0 gives 1, and starting at 1 gives 0, producing a two-cycle even though a real root exists elsewhere. For f(x)=x2−2f(x)=x^2-2, the initial guess 0 causes division by zero. A tiny nonzero slope can produce a huge step. Implementations should check finite values, small slopes, domain restrictions, iteration limits, and stagnation rather than treating any returned number as success.

A small residual alone need not mean a small root error. For f(x)=10−12(x−1)f(x)=10^{-12}(x-1), x=100x=100 has residual only 9.9×10−119.9\times10^{-11} despite being 99 units from the root. If ∣f′∣≥m>0|f^{\prime}|\ge m>0 throughout an interval joining an estimate to a root, the mean value theorem gives ∣x−r∣≤∣f(x)∣/m|x-r|\le |f(x)|/m. Without such information, a bracket is a clearer error certificate. A useful hybrid accepts Newton steps within a maintained bracket and otherwise bisects.

Newton's formula comes directly from solving a local linear equation. Write f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)f(x)\approx f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)(x-a); setting the right side to zero gives xnew=a−f(a)/f′(a)x_{\mathrm{new}}=a-f(a)/f^{\prime}(a). The approximation improves as the guess approaches a simple root because the neglected Taylor term is quadratic in x−ax-a. If f′(a)f^{\prime}(a) is tiny, however, the tangent is almost horizontal and its axis crossing can be far away. The update may also leave a domain where ff is defined, as happens for some starting points when solving equations involving log⁡x\log x or x\sqrt{x}. A formula alone is not a complete algorithm.

For f(x)=x2−2f(x)=x^2-2 and a positive estimate xx, Newton gives N(x)=(x+2/x)/2N(x)=(x+2/x)/2. Its error has the exact expression N(x)−2=(x−2)2/(2x)N(x)-\sqrt2=(x-\sqrt2)^2/(2x). Therefore every positive update is at least 2\sqrt2. Put ek=xk−2e_k=x_k-\sqrt2 after the first update. Since xk≥2x_k\ge\sqrt2 and 0≤ek<xk0\le e_k<x_k, the identity gives 0≤ek+1=ek2/(2xk)≤ek/20\le e_{k+1}=e_k^2/(2x_k)\le e_k/2. Induction yields 0≤ek≤e1/2k−1→00\le e_k\le e_1/2^{k-1}\to0, proving convergence directly from a geometric bound already taught in Chapter 3. The same identity shows quadratic improvement when the error is small. Starting at x=0x=0 is undefined; negative initial values track the negative root. These features depend on this equation and do not generalize to arbitrary smooth functions.

A repeated root behaves differently. If f(x)=(x−r)2f(x)=(x-r)^2, then f′(x)=2(x−r)f^{\prime}(x)=2(x-r), and for x≠rx\ne r Newton gives xnew=(x+r)/2x_{\mathrm{new}}=(x+r)/2. The error is halved rather than squared at each step. More generally f(x)=(x−r)mf(x)=(x-r)^m produces an error factor (m−1)/m(m-1)/m for integer m>1m>1. A small residual can therefore appear long before a small input error, because the residual is proportional to the mth power of the distance. Factoring an exact polynomial or using a multiplicity-aware update can help, but the ordinary local convergence theorem assumes a simple root.

A safeguarded solver combines the strengths of two methods: retain a sign-changing interval and propose a Newton step only when it is finite and lies safely inside that interval; otherwise bisect. After evaluating the proposal, update the bracket by signs. The bracket gives a location certificate even if Newton stops accelerating. A stopping report should distinguish an exact algebraic zero, a small residual, a small bracket, and iteration exhaustion. These are different outcomes. In the code laboratory, edit the start while keeping the bracket fixed and observe that the Newton sequence has no automatic knowledge of the certified interval.

The safeguarded recommendation above has two separate jobs: preserve a root and make its location interval small. An interior Newton proposal can preserve opposite endpoint signs without reducing the width by any fixed fraction. Even strict residual decrease does not imply progress of that width. Descending an objective instead of solving an equation

Elementary gradient descent in one variable updates xn+1=xn−αF′(xn)x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha F^{\prime}(x_n), where α>0\alpha>0 is a chosen step size. A positive derivative points toward increasing values, so moving in the opposite direction initially decreases the objective. The word initially matters: a large step can overshoot a valley and increase the objective. If xx and FF carry units, α\alpha must convert the derivative’s units into units of xx; rescaling variables changes the meaning of a numerical step size.

Chapter synthesis

  • A global optimum requires an objective, a feasible domain, and comparison of all relevant interior and boundary candidates.
  • Bisection trades speed for a maintained interval certificate; Newton uses tangent information and requires attention to starting points and failure.
  • Residual, root error, and objective error measure different things. Report the quantity that answers the practical question.
  • Gradient descent depends on step size and geometry; a converged stationary point is not automatically the best feasible design.

Common mistakes to check

  • “Every zero of the derivative is the best design.” A stationary point can be a minimum, maximum, or neither; check feasibility, compare all feasible interior and boundary candidates, and consider limits at excluded boundaries before claiming an optimum. Test with problem 1
  • “Newton always beats bisection.” Newton can cycle or divide by zero. For a continuous function with a sign-changing bracket, bisection halves the bracket at each step and bounds a root’s location. Test with problem 18
