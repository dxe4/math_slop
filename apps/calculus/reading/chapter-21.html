One approximation for all directions

A partial derivative answers a restricted question: what happens when just one coordinate changes? A physical measurement rarely follows that restriction. Moving a temperature sensor changes both its east–west and north–south coordinates; changing a cylinder’s dimensions changes both radius and height. We need a single approximation that accepts an arbitrary small displacement. The key requirement is that its error becomes negligible compared with the length of that displacement, regardless of the direction of approach.

Let ff be a real-valued function on an open neighborhood of a point a=(a1,a2)a=(a_1,a_2). We call ff differentiable at aa if there is a linear map LL with f(a+h)=f(a)+L(h)+r(h)f(a+h)=f(a)+L(h)+r(h), where r(h)/∥h∥→0r(h)/\|h\|\to0 as h→0h\to0. Here h=(h1,h2)h=(h_1,h_2), and ∥h∥\|h\| is its Euclidean length. The notation r(h)=o(∥h∥)r(h)=o(\|h\|) abbreviates this precise limiting statement. The input must remain in the domain. At a boundary, extensions or explicitly one-sided notions need separate treatment; our standard theorems concern interior points.

Every linear scalar-valued map on the plane has the form L(h)=Ah1+Bh2L(h)=Ah_1+Bh_2. Taking hh along each coordinate axis in the differentiability definition shows that A=fx(a)A=f_x(a) and B=fy(a)B=f_y(a). Thus existing partial derivatives identify the only possible linear approximation, but they do not establish that it works in all directions. Differentiability also implies continuity: the linear term tends to zero, and so does its remainder. This is the multivariable version of the familiar one-variable implication.

A useful sufficient condition is that the first partial derivatives exist throughout a neighborhood of aa and are continuous at aa. Then ff is differentiable there. Proof sketch: split a two-coordinate increment into a horizontal change and a vertical change, apply the one-variable mean value theorem to each, and use continuity of the partials to replace their nearby values by their values at aa with an error small compared with ∥h∥\|h\|. Smooth polynomial, exponential, and trigonometric combinations therefore support linear models wherever their defining expressions are valid.

The remainder condition is relative to the full displacement length. For h=(h1,h2)h=(h_1,h_2) and r=∥h∥r=\|h\|, an error of size h12+h22=r2h_1^2+h_2^2=r^2 is acceptable because r2/r=r→0r^2/r=r\to0. An error such as ∣h1∣|h_1| can be small in absolute size but is not negligible relative to rr along the horizontal axis. The distinction matters when judging numerical approximations: every continuous function has output changes that become small near a point, yet only differentiable functions have a single linear rule whose residual is smaller than distance.

Consider f(x,y)=∣x∣+y2f(x,y)=|x|+y^2 at the origin. The yy partial is zero, while the xx partial fails because the left and right slopes of ∣x∣|x| differ. No tangent plane exists. By contrast, f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2} has every nonzero-direction slice proportional to ∣t∣|t|, so directional derivatives fail as two-sided derivatives along every unit direction. These examples show two kinds of failure; neither should be confused with the subtler chapter-20 function whose coordinate partials exist but continuity fails.

Continuity of partials is a convenient sufficient condition, not the definition of differentiability. A partial may oscillate near a point while the function still admits a valid linear approximation there. To decide from first principles, subtract the candidate linear term and divide by rr. If the quotient tends to zero uniformly over nearby directions, differentiability holds. The theorem about continuous partials saves work for smooth formulas, but a proof using that theorem must check a neighborhood and the point actually lies in its interior.

For a vector-valued map, the derivative is a linear transformation between input and output spaces. Its columns are the responses to unit coordinate changes, while its action on an arbitrary displacement is the corresponding linear combination. The same remainder criterion uses the norm of the input displacement and the norm of the output error. Thinking of the Jacobian as an active map, rather than only a rectangular array of partials, prepares the chain rule and makes coordinate changes easier to track.

Near a smooth point, a function can have a good tangent plane without being nearly constant. For f(x,y)=100x+y2f(x,y)=100x+y^2 at the origin, the plane z=100xz=100x predicts a large change under a small horizontal move, but its error y2y^2 is still tiny relative to the displacement length. The size of the gradient measures first-order sensitivity; the differentiability condition measures the quality of that first-order rule. These are separate quantities, so an accurate plane can be steep and a flat plane can have noticeable curvature at finite scale.

A useful way to audit a claimed derivative is to test three levels of evidence. First, coordinate partials determine a candidate linear map. Second, substitutions along a few special directions can detect a contradiction, though agreement cannot prove the claim. Third, a bound on the full remainder establishes the result for every direction and every curved approach. For example, if ∣R(h,k)∣≤C(h2+k2)|R(h,k)|\le C(h^2+k^2), then ∣R∣/r≤Cr→0|R|/r\le Cr\to0. If only ∣R∣≤C∣h∣|R|\le C|h| is known, the ratio can remain as large as CC on horizontal approaches. The distinction prevents the common error of calling a formula differentiable because its partial derivatives look smooth at just one point. In a model with more inputs, the same logic applies: the error must be small compared with the norm of the entire input change, not only with each coordinate separately.

Tangent planes and measurable error

The graph z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y) is a surface in three-dimensional space. At a differentiability point (a,b)(a,b), its tangent plane is the graph of the affine function T(x,y)=f(a,b)+fx(a,b)(x−a)+fy(a,b)(y−b)T(x,y)=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b). Affine means a linear expression plus a constant. The graph passes through the original point and reproduces both coordinate slopes. Its deeper property is the vanishing relative error from the definition above. A picture that appears flat from one viewing direction does not provide that property.

The total differential is df=fx dx+fy dydf=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy: dx,dydx,dy are chosen input increments and dfdf is the predicted output change. The actual change is Δf=df+r(h)\Delta f=df+r(h). A quantitative error estimate can be obtained with one-variable calculus. Let a=(a1,a2)a=(a_1,a_2), h=(h1,h2)h=(h_1,h_2), and g(t)=f(a+th)g(t)=f(a+th) for 0≤t≤10\le t\le1. If the entire segment stays in the domain, ff is C2C^2 around it, and ∣g′′(t)∣≤M∥h∥2|g''(t)|\le M\|h\|^2 throughout, one-variable Taylor's theorem gives ∣f(a+h)−f(a)−∇f(a)⋅h∣=∣g(1)−g(0)−g′(0)∣≤M∥h∥2/2|f(a+h)-f(a)-\nabla f(a)\cdot h|=|g(1)-g(0)-g'(0)|\le M\|h\|^2/2. The bound is stronger than differentiability alone.

Units make the prediction meaningful. If xx and yy are distances in meters and ff is temperature in degrees Celsius, each partial has units degrees Celsius per meter, and each product in dfdf is a temperature change. If the inputs have different units, such as radius in meters and pressure in pascals, the total differential still adds compatible output changes. However, comparing coordinate lengths or asking for a unit direction requires a declared scaling; the Euclidean gradient geometry later in this chapter assumes compatible coordinates or specified dimensionless coordinates.

To test a local model, choose a displacement, compute the prediction, and compare it with the actual value. Then halve the displacement in the same direction. Smooth curvature usually makes the absolute error decrease by approximately a factor of four. That observed ratio is useful evidence about this experiment, not a universal consequence of differentiability: the definition only promises that error divided by displacement length tends to zero. Near a singularity, or outside the domain, a tangent-plane extrapolation may fail even when the starting derivatives are modest.

An affine plane is a local statement at a chosen base point. For f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2, the tangent plane at (a,b)(a,b) is z=a2+b2+2a(x−a)+2b(y−b)z=a^2+b^2+2a(x-a)+2b(y-b). Rearrangement gives z=2ax+2by−a2−b2z=2ax+2by-a^2-b^2. At another base point the coefficients change, so the entire curved bowl cannot be represented by one plane. The exact residual is (x−a)2+(y−b)2(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2; this nonnegative remainder means every tangent plane lies below the bowl, a global fact special to this convex example.

Measurement precision creates a practical floor. If each observed output carries uncertainty at most η\eta, comparing actual and predicted changes at very tiny hh can amplify relative uncertainty, because both outputs are nearly equal. A table of shrinking residuals may first show quadratic behavior and then flatten into noise. The mathematical remainder condition concerns exact function values; in data analysis, state the measurement scale and use steps large enough to exceed the noise while small enough for the local approximation to be credible.

Total differentials compose naturally with small simultaneous changes. Suppose the area of a rectangle is A=lwA=lw. At (l,w)=(3,2)(l,w)=(3,2), increments Δl=0.02\Delta l=0.02 and Δw=−0.01\Delta w=-0.01 give dA=2(0.02)+3(−0.01)=0.01dA=2(0.02)+3(-0.01)=0.01. The tangent plane of z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y) is often written fx(a,b)(x−a)+fy(a,b)(y−b)−(z−f(a,b))=0f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)-(z-f(a,b))=0. Its normal vector is (fx,fy,−1)(f_x,f_y,-1). For data-based approximations, the base point, displacement, and acceptable output error should all be stated. Suppose a temperature model has a Hessian norm bounded by 4 ∘C/m24\,^\circ\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{m}^2 in a 0.1 m0.1\,\mathrm m ball. The example joins mathematical approximation, physical units, and the limits of an experiment.

A tangent plane can predict a change of zero along a special displacement without the actual change being zero. For f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2 at (1,2)(1,2), choose h=(2s,−s)h=(2s,-s), perpendicular to the gradient (2,4)(2,4). The same quadratic residual explains why visual agreement near contact can be deceptive at coarse scale.

The chain rule and the Jacobian

Now suppose the input point moves along a differentiable curve r(t)=(x(t),y(t))r(t)=(x(t),y(t)). If ff is differentiable at r(t)r(t), substitution of the curve’s linear approximation into the linear approximation for ff gives the chain rule. The output rate is fx x′+fy y′f_x\,x^{\prime}+f_y\,y^{\prime}, with the partial derivatives evaluated at the moving point. Each coordinate contributes its sensitivity multiplied by its rate of change. Even if one contribution is positive, another can outweigh it, so a positive xx partial alone says little about the total rate along a path.

For several outputs, collect the linear coefficients into a matrix. A differentiable map FF from an open subset of Rn\mathbb R^n to Rm\mathbb R^m has derivative represented by its mm-by-nn Jacobian matrix JFJ_F. Entry (i,j)(i,j) is ∂Fi/∂xj\partial F_i/\partial x_j: rows correspond to outputs and columns to inputs. Multiplying JF(a)J_F(a) by an input displacement hh gives the first-order output displacement. A scalar-valued function has a one-row Jacobian; its gradient is conventionally written as a column vector, so the two are transposes in standard orthonormal coordinates.

If GG maps input coordinates into the domain of FF, and GG is differentiable at aa while FF is differentiable at G(a)G(a), the composition has Jacobian JF∘G(a)=JF(G(a))JG(a)J_{F\circ G}(a)=J_F(G(a))J_G(a). Matrix dimensions expose mistakes: an mm-by-nn matrix times an nn-by-kk matrix produces an mm-by-kk derivative. The order follows the actual transformation of increments: first JGJ_G acts, then JFJ_F. For x=x(s,t)x=x(s,t) and y=y(s,t)y=y(s,t), this yields ∂f/∂s=fx xs+fy ys\partial f/\partial s=f_x\,x_s+f_y\,y_s and the corresponding formula ∂f/∂t=fx xt+fy yt\partial f/\partial t=f_x\,x_t+f_y\,y_t.

The word Jacobian sometimes means the matrix and sometimes its determinant. The determinant is defined only for square matrices and measures signed local volume scaling; it is not a replacement for the full derivative map. For polar coordinates x=rcos⁡θx=r\cos\theta, y=rsin⁡θy=r\sin\theta, the matrix has rows (cos⁡θ,−rsin⁡θ)(\cos\theta,-r\sin\theta) and (sin⁡θ,rcos⁡θ)(\sin\theta,r\cos\theta), with determinant rr. At r=0r=0 the angular column vanishes, explaining why this parametrization loses a direction there. Chapter 24 will use the absolute determinant to transform area elements.

The chain rule is a statement about composition, not about treating dxdx and dydy as independent physical clocks. If x(t)=t2x(t)=t^2 and y(t)=sin⁡ty(t)=\sin t, then f(x(t),y(t))f(x(t),y(t)) changes because both inputs change. At a time when x′=0x^{\prime}=0, the xx contribution to the instantaneous rate vanishes even if fxf_x is large. At a time when fy=0f_y=0, the yy motion has no first-order contribution even if y′y^{\prime} is large. This separates motion through a field from spatial sensitivity of the field.

For f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2 and polar coordinates x=rcos⁡θx=r\cos\theta, y=rsin⁡θy=r\sin\theta, the composition is exactly r2r^2. The matrix chain rule predicts ∂f/∂r=2r\partial f/\partial r=2r and ∂f/∂θ=0\partial f/\partial\theta=0. Direct calculation using fx=2xf_x=2x and fy=2yf_y=2y gives 2xcos⁡θ+2ysin⁡θ=2r2x\cos\theta+2y\sin\theta=2r and −2xrsin⁡θ+2yrcos⁡θ=0-2xr\sin\theta+2yr\cos\theta=0. The cancellation in the angular derivative expresses rotational symmetry, and checking both routes is a useful guard against transposing a Jacobian.

Dimensions of a Jacobian matter in applications. If FF maps two controls to three sensor readings, JFJ_F has three rows and two columns. It cannot be inverted as an ordinary square matrix. A least-squares inverse problem may still estimate controls from readings, but that is an additional optimization problem, not the inverse function theorem. If GG maps a single time variable into the two controls, JF∘GJ_{F\circ G} is a three-by-one column of time rates. Matrix shapes encode the direction of causation.

At a coordinate singularity, a physical function can remain smooth while the coordinate map loses invertibility. The polar map has determinant rr, so r=0r=0 makes angular changes invisible in Cartesian position. A formula with 1/r1/r in its transformed derivatives may blow up there even when the underlying Cartesian function is regular. Do not infer a physical singularity solely from a bad coordinate chart. The origin is better analyzed in Cartesian coordinates or with limits that account for all angles.

A Jacobian determinant carries orientation as well as scale. For a plane map with determinant −2-2 at a point, a small oriented area is locally reversed and doubled in magnitude. The change-of-variables formula for ordinary area uses the absolute value 22. For a non-square derivative there is no ordinary determinant, though lengths, surface areas, and volumes can be obtained from related Gram determinants when the map has suitable rank. Chapter 24 develops the square-coordinate cases used in multiple integrals.

The matrix chain rule can be verified by a small-increment calculation. If G(a+h)=G(a)+JG(a)h+o(∥h∥)G(a+h)=G(a)+J_G(a)h+o(\|h\|) and F(b+k)=F(b)+JF(b)k+o(∥k∥)F(b+k)=F(b)+J_F(b)k+o(\|k\|) at b=G(a)b=G(a), substitute k=G(a+h)−G(a)k=G(a+h)-G(a). Differentiability of GG makes k=O(∥h∥)k=O(\|h\|), so the remainder from FF is also o(∥h∥)o(\|h\|). The linear part becomes JF(G(a))JG(a)hJ_F(G(a))J_G(a)h. This argument explains both the matrix order and why differentiability of both component maps is required. Merely possessing some partial derivatives of FF and GG at their points does not justify composing them as Jacobians.
