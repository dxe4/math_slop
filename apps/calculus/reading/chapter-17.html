A whole family of series

A numerical series gives one number, when it converges. A power series gives a series for each input xx: choose fixed coefficients c0,c1,…c_0,c_1,\ldots and a center aa, then consider ∑cn(x−a)n\sum c_n(x-a)^n. Its partial sums are polynomials. At x=ax=a every positive-power term vanishes, so the series always converges there to c0c_0. Away from the center, the factor (x−a)n(x-a)^n may amplify or suppress the coefficients. The first task is therefore to find the inputs for which a sum exists.

There is a radius RR, possibly zero or infinite, such that the series converges absolutely when ∣x−a∣<R|x-a|<R and diverges when ∣x−a∣>R|x-a|>R. When 0<R<∞0<R<\infty, each endpoint a−Ra-R and a+Ra+R needs its own test; either, both, or neither may belong to the interval of convergence. The radius is a distance, while the interval records actual permitted real inputs. A ratio test that produces a limit of one at an endpoint has left that case undecided.

For example, ∑n=1∞xn/n\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x^n/n has successive absolute-term ratio ∣x∣n/(n+1)|x|n/(n+1), tending to ∣x∣|x|. Thus R=1R=1. At x=1x=1 the harmonic series diverges. At x=−1x=-1 the alternating harmonic series converges conditionally. Its interval is [−1,1)[-1,1), even though the open interior is symmetric. Replacing nn by n2n^2 makes both endpoints converge; removing the denominator makes both diverge. Identical radii do not imply identical endpoint behavior.

A proof idea for the radius property comes from comparison with a geometric series. If the series converges at b≠ab\ne a, its terms cn(b−a)nc_n(b-a)^n are bounded in absolute value by some constant MM. At a closer point xx, the absolute terms are at most M(∣x−a∣/∣b−a∣)nM(|x-a|/|b-a|)^n. That geometric majorant converges. Thus convergence at one distance forces absolute convergence at every smaller distance. This argument explains why disconnected islands of convergence cannot occur. Define RR as the supremum of distances ∣x−a∣|x-a| at which the series converges, allowing R=0R=0 or R=∞R=\infty. Every distance below RR is below some convergent distance, so the comparison just proved gives absolute convergence there. No distance greater than RR can converge by the definition of supremum. At a finite boundary distance RR, examine the two endpoints separately.

For a power series, three questions must be answered separately: where does it converge, what function does it sum to there, and how accurately does a finite polynomial approximate that function? The radius addresses the first question in the open interval. An algebraic identity such as the geometric sum may answer the second. A remainder or tail bound answers the third. A ratio test alone gives no expression for the sum and often says nothing at endpoints. Organizing solutions in this order prevents a polished radius calculation from being mistaken for a complete power-series analysis.

The endpoint tests are ordinary numerical-series problems. For ∑n=1∞(x−2)n/(n3n)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(x-2)^n/(n3^n), the ratio test gives ∣x−2∣<3|x-2|<3. At x=5x=5 the terms become 1/n1/n and diverge; at x=−1x=-1 they become (−1)n/n(-1)^n/n and converge conditionally. The interval is [−1,5)[-1,5), with center 22 and radius 33. At the conditional endpoint, absolute convergence fails, so rearrangement properties differ from those inside the open interval. The left and right endpoints need not behave symmetrically because signs can change.

The radius may be infinite, as for ∑n=0∞xn/n!\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!, or zero, as for ∑n=0∞n!xn\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!x^n. In the first case the ratio of consecutive absolute terms tends to zero for every fixed xx. In the second, for any nonzero xx the ratio is (n+1)∣x∣→∞(n+1)|x|\to\infty, so the terms fail to approach zero. Both series converge at the center x=0x=0. Thus “a power series converges near its center” requires care: a zero-radius series has no nontrivial neighborhood of convergence. A polynomial, by contrast, has only finitely many terms and is defined everywhere.

A radius is a real distance in the input variable's units. If a power series is written in t−t0t-t_0 with tt measured in seconds, RR is measured in seconds; saying only “radius 22” hides the meaning of that interval. Rescaling time to milliseconds rescales the numerical radius, while the physical interval is the same. Coefficients carry compensating units so each term has the sum's units. This dimensional check is useful when a local model from measured data is transferred between unit systems.

The ratio and root tests can often find a radius from the coefficient growth, but an endpoint may still demand an alternating, comparison, or term test. For a series centered at aa, substitute x=a+Rx=a+R and x=a−Rx=a-R into the actual term formula before classifying either side. A common shortcut writes an interval with both endpoints included merely because the ratio is at most one there. At equality the ratio test is inconclusive. Recording an open interval first and adding tested endpoints one at a time is a safer workflow, especially when one endpoint is conditional and the other divergent. That distinction should remain visible in both the prose and every plotted approximation.

Matching derivatives: Taylor and Maclaurin polynomials

Linearization matched a function’s value and first derivative at a point. A Taylor polynomial of degree at most NN matches all derivatives through order NN. To see why its coefficients are forced, write P(x)=b0+b1(x−a)+⋯+bN(x−a)NP(x)=b_0+b_1(x-a)+\cdots+b_N(x-a)^N. Evaluating at aa leaves b0b_0. Differentiating once and evaluating leaves b1b_1; differentiating kk times leaves k!bkk!b_k. Hence bk=f(k)(a)/k!b_k=f^{(k)}(a)/k!. This construction requires derivatives at the center, while error estimates require information between the center and the evaluation point.

The polynomial TN(x)=∑k=0Nf(k)(a)(x−a)k/k!T_N(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k! is called a Maclaurin polynomial when a=0a=0. An infinite Taylor series uses the same coefficients for all kk, provided all derivatives exist. Familiar Maclaurin expansions include ex=∑n=0∞xn/n!e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!, sin⁡x=x−x3/3!+x5/5!−⋯\sin x=x-x^3/3!+x^5/5!-\cdots, and cos⁡x=1−x2/2!+x4/4!−⋯\cos x=1-x^2/2!+x^4/4!-\cdots. The trigonometric arguments must be in radians. These three identities hold for every real xx, as remainder estimates can establish.

Moving the center changes the useful local model. For exe^x at aa, every derivative equals eae^a, so TN(x)=ea∑k=0N(x−a)k/k!T_N(x)=e^a\sum_{k=0}^{N}(x-a)^k/k!. Near x=2x=2, centering at 22 makes the displacement small; centering at zero may require more terms. A finite Taylor polynomial is defined for every real xx even when the infinite series has a finite radius. Its existence outside that radius does not promise accuracy there.

Units also constrain the formula. If xx measures time in seconds and ff measures position in meters, f(k)(a)f^{(k)}(a) has units m/sk\mathrm{m}/\mathrm{s}^k. Multiplication by (x−a)k(x-a)^k restores meters in every term. An expression such as exe^x assumes xx is dimensionless; a physical exponential usually has a dimensionless argument such as t/τt/\tau, where τ\tau is a time scale.

A certificate, not just a close-looking graph

Suppose ff has N+1N+1 continuous derivatives on an interval containing the center aa and the evaluation point xx. Taylor’s theorem gives RN(x)=f(N+1)(ξ)(x−a)N+1/(N+1)!R_N(x)=f^{(N+1)}(\xi)(x-a)^{N+1}/(N+1)! for some ξ\xi between aa and xx. Usually ξ\xi is unknown. If the absolute value of that derivative is at most MM throughout the connecting interval, replacing it by MM produces an explicit upper bound. A graph may suggest that the approximation is good; this inequality specifies how good it must be.

For a certificate on an entire closed interval [L,U][L,U], with aa inside it, bound the derivative on [L,U][L,U] and set d=max⁡(∣L−a∣,∣U−a∣)d=\max(|L-a|,|U-a|). If the center lies outside the requested interval, the derivative bound must cover the larger interval joining the center to every requested input. Forgetting that extra region can invalidate an otherwise plausible calculation.

A proof sketch uses repeated integration of the fundamental theorem of calculus. The remainder has integral form RN(x)=(1/N!)∫axf(N+1)(t)(x−t)N dtR_N(x)=(1/N!)\int_a^x f^{(N+1)}(t)(x-t)^N\,dt. Bounding the absolute derivative and integrating the absolute kernel gives the factorial estimate, including when x<ax<a. The weighted mean value theorem for the integral then gives the Lagrange form above. This also explains why controlling a derivative between the points matters.

An upper bound need not be tight. A failure to meet a desired tolerance with this estimate does not prove the polynomial is inaccurate; a sharper derivative bound or an alternating-series estimate may succeed. Conversely, small sampled error cannot certify unsampled inputs. At high degrees, ordinary floating-point arithmetic can hide further mathematical improvement through rounding or cancellation, so the theorem bounds truncation error rather than every error in a computer implementation.

Smoothness does not guarantee a Taylor representation

A function is smooth if it has continuous derivatives of every order. It is analytic at aa if, on some neighborhood of aa, it equals its convergent Taylor series there. Analytic functions are smooth, but the reverse implication fails. Taylor’s theorem supplies a remainder for each finite degree; equality with the infinite series additionally requires that remainder to tend to zero. Increasing derivative order changes the derivative bound, sometimes too rapidly for the factorial denominator to win.

Let f(x)=e−1/x2f(x)=e^{-1/x^2} for x≠0x\ne0 and f(0)=0f(0)=0. Every derivative at zero vanishes, so every Taylor polynomial and the resulting convergent formal series are identically zero; yet f(x)>0f(x)>0 at every nonzero input. The next paragraph proves the derivative claim rather than inferring it from a flat-looking graph. At a fixed nonzero xx, the Taylor remainder is always f(x)f(x) and does not tend to zero with degree. Thus smoothness supplies coefficients, while analyticity requires equality with their series on a neighborhood.

For any fixed m≥0m\ge0, set u=1/x2→∞u=1/x^2\to\infty. Then ∣x∣−me−1/x2=um/2e−u→0|x|^{-m}e^{-1/x^2}=u^{m/2}e^{-u}\to0, since a fixed power grows more slowly than eue^u (for example, repeated l'Hôpital steps reduce its exponent). Away from zero, each derivative is a finite polynomial in 1/x1/x times e−1/x2e^{-1/x^2}. Inductively, the difference quotient at zero for each fixed derivative order has the same form with one extra inverse power and tends to zero; that derivative takes value zero and extends continuously. The argument works separately for each fixed order. A bound uniform across derivative orders can suffice for Taylor equality elsewhere, but is not necessary.

An analytic function is determined locally by all of its derivatives at a point, but a merely smooth function need not be. If two analytic functions have identical Taylor coefficients at a center, their series agree on a shared neighborhood and so do the functions there. The flat function and the zero function have identical derivatives at zero while differing at every nearby nonzero point, showing why the adjective analytic matters. This distinction appears in differential equations and approximation theory, where a formal power-series solution may require a separate convergence argument.

One should not generalize the flat example to imply that Taylor series are usually unreliable. The elementary exponential, sine, and cosine functions have controlled remainders that tend to zero for every fixed real x, so their Taylor series represent them globally. The logarithm near one and geometric rational functions have finite radii limited by nearby singularities. The correct question for each function is whether a proved remainder tends to zero on the requested region. A plot, a list of derivatives, or a symbolic series alone does not settle that question.

Convergence of the coefficient series and approximation of the given function are different claims even on a closed interval. Fix r>0r>0. For the flat function above, every Taylor polynomial TnT_n is zero, and the formal series converges uniformly to zero on [−r,r][-r,r]. Nevertheless, max⁡∣x∣≤r∣f(x)−Tn(x)∣=e−1/r2>0\max_{|x|\le r}|f(x)-T_n(x)|=e^{-1/r^2}>0 for every degree nn: the value grows with ∣x∣|x| and is attained at x=±rx=\pm r. Given a tolerance smaller than this fixed error, increasing the degree can never meet it on that interval. Making the interval smaller can reduce the error, but that changes the region rather than proving Taylor equality there. An extremely small positive value may disappear in a plotted scale or numerical display. Neither visual flatness nor convergence of the formal series establishes that it represents the original function.
