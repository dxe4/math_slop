A sequence is a function on the counting numbers

A sequence assigns a real number ana_n to each positive integer nn. Its domain is discrete: a1a_1, a2a_2, a3a_3, and so on. The index records position, not necessarily time. In a savings model it might count months; in a numerical algorithm it counts iterations. The values retain their physical units, while the index itself is a dimensionless count. Unless another starting index is stated, this chapter begins at n=1n=1.

To say ana_n converges to LL means that for every ε>0\varepsilon>0 there is a positive integer NN such that ∣an−L∣<ε|a_n-L|<\varepsilon for every n≥Nn\ge N. The word every matters. Some terms getting close to LL is insufficient if other arbitrarily late terms remain far away. For an=1/na_n=1/n, choose any integer N>1/εN>1/\varepsilon. Then n≥Nn\ge N implies 1/n≤1/N<ε1/n\le 1/N<\varepsilon. This is a proof of convergence to zero, not an inference from a long decimal table.

The limit laws from functions also apply to sequences when the relevant limits exist, with a nonzero denominator limit required for division. Thus (3n+2)/(n+5)=(3+2/n)/(1+5/n)(3n+2)/(n+5)=(3+2/n)/(1+5/n) tends to 33. A useful bridge is that if f(x)→Lf(x)\to L as x→∞x\to\infty, then an=f(n)→La_n=f(n)\to L. The converse need not hold: sin⁡(πn)=0\sin(\pi n)=0 at every integer, while sin⁡(πx)\sin(\pi x) has no limit as real x→∞x\to\infty.

A sequence may diverge by growing without bound, as n2n^2 does, or by oscillating, as (−1)n(-1)^n does. Being bounded does not alone guarantee convergence. Conversely, convergence guarantees boundedness: after some index the terms lie within one unit of the limit, and finitely many earlier terms have a finite maximum absolute value. Finite initial changes do not affect whether a sequence converges or its eventual limit.

A sequence limit is a claim about the entire tail after one index. For an=(−1)n/na_n=(-1)^n/n, the signs alternate, but ∣an∣=1/n|a_n|=1/n. Given ε>0\varepsilon>0, any N>1/εN>1/\varepsilon ensures ∣an−0∣<ε|a_n-0|<\varepsilon for all n≥Nn\ge N, so the sequence converges to zero. The oscillation becomes harmless because its amplitude shrinks. In contrast, (−1)n(-1)^n never settles: even and odd subsequences stay at 11 and −1-1. Checking these two subsequences proves divergence without needing to enumerate many terms. A graph of the first hundred points may suggest the result, but the all-later-terms condition proves it.

Finite prefixes can be ignored for convergence but not for an actual sum or an initial-condition model. Changing a1a_1 from 11 to a million leaves the limit of ana_n unchanged because the definition allows NN beyond the altered term. For a series, changing its first term leaves convergence status unchanged but changes the sum by 999999999999. This distinction will matter when formulas are indexed from zero or one. Always write the first few terms before applying a geometric-sum formula; a shifted starting index often changes the numerical answer while leaving the common ratio and convergence condition intact.

A limit law requires the pieces it combines to have limits. It is invalid to say an/bn→L/Ma_n/b_n\to L/M if bn→0b_n\to0, and even if bnb_n never equals zero the quotient may grow or oscillate. For example, (1/n)/(1/n2)=n(1/n)/(1/n^2)=n diverges. Algebraic normalization is more reliable: divide numerator and denominator of (3n+2)/(n+5)(3n+2)/(n+5) by nn, then apply limit laws to 1/n1/n. An expression that resembles 0/00/0 is a signal to simplify or compare orders, not a numerical value of the limit.

The epsilon definition can be translated into an explicit index budget. For an=5−2/na_n=5-2/n, requiring ∣an−5∣<0.01|a_n-5|<0.01 means 2/n<0.012/n<0.01, so any n>200n>200 works and N=201N=201 is sufficient. This turns an abstract existence claim into a concrete guarantee for all future terms. The chosen NN need not be smallest unless the problem asks for it; any valid bound proves convergence. In applications, state whether ε\varepsilon is an absolute tolerance in the units of ana_n or a relative tolerance scaled to a reference value.

A subsequence selects terms with indices n1<n2<⋯n_1<n_2<\cdots. If an→La_n\to L, then every subsequence also converges to LL because its indices eventually exceed any NN from the original epsilon definition. Therefore two subsequences with different limits prove that the full sequence diverges. This is the clean reason even and odd terms settle the fate of (−1)n(-1)^n. The converse is not true when only one subsequence is inspected: an apparently stable run of selected terms can hide other behavior. This cautions against sampling a sequence at convenient indices and inferring a full limit from those alone. The requirement concerns arbitrarily late terms, not merely a long finite plot or a handful of favorable indices.

Monotonicity turns a bound into a limit

A sequence is nondecreasing when an+1≥ana_{n+1}\ge a_n for all nn, and nonincreasing when the inequality reverses. Either property is called monotonicity. The monotone bounded theorem states that a nondecreasing real sequence bounded above converges; a nonincreasing real sequence bounded below also converges. These are the bounds that oppose its direction of travel. An increasing sequence already has a lower bound at its first term, but that lower bound says nothing about escape to infinity.

Here is the real-number step. An upper bound UU of a set is a number at least as large as every element; its supremum LL is the least such bound. Completeness of R\mathbb R says that every nonempty set of real numbers bounded above has a real supremum. Apply this axiom to the terms of a nondecreasing bounded sequence. For any ε>0\varepsilon>0, L−εL-\varepsilon cannot be an upper bound, so some aN>L−εa_N>L-\varepsilon. Monotonicity yields L−ε<aN≤an≤LL-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le L for every n≥Nn\ge N, which is the limit definition. The completeness axiom supplies LL; the inequality proves that the sequence approaches it.

For a concrete iteration, start x1=1x_1=1 and define xn+1=2+xnx_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}. Induction shows 1≤xn≤21\le x_n\le2: the interval maps into itself. Also x2>x1x_2>x_1, and the increasing square-root function carries xn≥xn−1x_n\ge x_{n-1} into xn+1≥xnx_{n+1}\ge x_n. Therefore the sequence has a limit L∈[1,2]L\in[1,2]. Continuity now allows L=2+LL=\sqrt{2+L}, giving L2−L−2=0L^2-L-2=0. Of the roots 22 and −1-1, only 22 lies in the permitted interval.

Solving a fixed-point equation was the final step, not the convergence argument. An iteration can have a fixed point while its actual terms fail to approach it. For example, xn+1=2−xnx_{n+1}=2-x_n has the fixed point 11 but alternates between 00 and 22 when started at 00. Bounds, starting values, and the update rule must all enter the reasoning.

Monotonicity alone does not guarantee a finite limit: an=na_n=n is increasing and unbounded. Boundedness alone also fails: an=(−1)na_n=(-1)^n is bounded and nonconvergent. The monotone bounded theorem needs both properties at once. Its proof through the supremum explains why the real-number system matters. If an iteration lives in a closed invariant interval and its update preserves order, this theorem can establish convergence before the eventual value is known. Solving the fixed-point equation afterward identifies which possible limit the sequence reaches.

For xn+1=2+xnx_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n} with x1=1x_1=1, the invariant interval and increasing-step argument above establish convergence before any fixed-point equation is used. Continuity then gives L=2+LL=\sqrt{2+L}; squaring gives candidates 22 and −1-1, but only 22 lies in [1,2][1,2] and satisfies the original equation. For an optional return after the existing Lesson 2 checkpoint, repeat this proof order for another continuous update: first find an invariant interval and a monotonicity argument, then identify the limit. The fixed-point equation alone cannot supply convergence.

A decreasing bounded example is an=1+1/na_n=1+1/n. Its terms decrease because 1/(n+1)<1/n1/(n+1)<1/n, and they stay above 11, so the monotone bounded theorem gives convergence. The explicit formula then identifies the limit 11. The theorem is more valuable when a closed formula is unavailable. For instance, in many iterative algorithms a carefully chosen invariant interval and a one-step inequality establish monotonicity and boundedness; the update's continuity then determines the limit. When the update is not monotone, a contraction estimate may be more appropriate than forcing a monotone argument.

A bounded recurrence can fail to converge through a cycle. For xn+1=2−xnx_{n+1}=2-x_n started at zero, the terms are 0,2,0,2,…0,2,0,2,\ldots. The interval [0,2][0,2] is invariant and the fixed point equation L=2−LL=2-L has the solution 11, but neither subsequence approaches 11. The missing hypothesis is not boundedness but actual convergence or a theorem that establishes it. This counterexample should be kept in mind whenever a solution begins by writing “let n→∞n\to\infty” in a recurrence: passing to a limit is legitimate only after convergence has been justified.

Completeness can be expressed through a Cauchy condition: a real sequence converges exactly when, for every ε>0\varepsilon>0, all sufficiently late terms are within ε\varepsilon of one another. For 1/n1/n this follows because both late terms are close to zero. For (−1)n(-1)^n, arbitrarily late adjacent terms differ by two, so the condition fails. This formulation is useful when the limit is not known in advance; it asks whether the tail settles internally. The monotone bounded theorem is one way to prove that settling property without writing an explicit limit formula. The Cauchy viewpoint will reappear when examining series tails.

Completeness is the hidden step in monotone convergence

Every bounded nondecreasing sequence of real numbers converges to its least upper bound. A bounded nonincreasing sequence converges to its greatest lower bound.

sn↑S=sup⁡{sn:n≥1}s_n\uparrow S=\sup\{s_n:n\ge1\}
Reasoning and proof

Let (sn)(s_n) be bounded and nondecreasing. The real-number completeness property guarantees that its set of values has a supremum SS. By definition, every sn≤Ss_n\le S. For any ε>0\varepsilon>0, S−εS-\varepsilon cannot be an upper bound, or SS would not be the least upper bound. Therefore some index NN satisfies sN>S−εs_N>S-\varepsilon. Monotonicity then gives S−ε<sN≤sn≤SS-\varepsilon<s_N\le s_n\le S for every n≥Nn\ge N, so ∣sn−S∣<ε|s_n-S|<\varepsilon. This is the full ε\varepsilon argument behind the compact phrase “increasing and bounded means convergent.”

For a nonincreasing bounded sequence, apply the same reasoning to −sn-s_n or use the infimum directly. The proof depends on completeness of the real numbers, not merely algebra. For example, rational approximations that increase toward 2\sqrt2 can be bounded in the rational numbers without having a rational limit; the missing least upper bound is irrational. Calculus takes place in a number system where those gaps have been filled.

When applying the theorem to a series with nonnegative terms, take sns_n to be its partial sums. They are nondecreasing automatically, so a finite upper bound on all partial sums proves convergence. The individual terms tending to zero gives no such upper bound: harmonic partial sums keep growing. Conversely, if positive partial sums are unbounded, their monotonicity forces divergence to +∞+\infty rather than oscillation.

Series are limits of accumulated totals

Given terms ana_n, define the NNth partial sum SN=a1+⋯+aNS_N=a_1+\cdots+a_N. The infinite series ∑an\sum a_n converges to SS exactly when the sequence SNS_N converges to a finite real number SS. Thus a series introduces a second sequence: the running totals. Terms can shrink while running totals grow without bound. For nonnegative terms, the partial sums are nondecreasing, so the monotone bounded theorem says that the series converges exactly when those partial sums are bounded above.

A necessary condition for convergence is an→0a_n\to0. Indeed, an=Sn−Sn−1a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}, and if both partial-sum sequences tend to SS their difference tends to zero. This gives the divergence test: if the terms do not tend to zero, the series diverges. If the terms do tend to zero, the test gives no conclusion. The harmonic series ∑n=1∞1/n\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n will soon supply the decisive counterexample.

For a geometric series indexed from zero, the terms are a,ar,ar2,…a,ar,ar^2,\ldots. Multiplying the finite sum by rr and subtracting gives ∑k=0N−1ark=a(1−rN)/(1−r)\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}ar^k=a(1-r^N)/(1-r), provided r≠1r\ne1. If ∣r∣<1|r|<1, then rN→0r^N\to0 and the sum is a/(1−r)a/(1-r). For nonzero aa and ∣r∣≥1|r|\ge1, the terms fail the necessary condition; this includes both endpoints r=1r=1 and r=−1r=-1. When a=0a=0 every term is zero regardless of rr.

A telescoping series instead exposes cancellation between neighboring terms. Since 1/[n(n+1)]=1/n−1/(n+1)1/[n(n+1)]=1/n-1/(n+1), its first NN terms sum to 1−1/(N+1)1-1/(N+1). Writing a finite partial sum before cancelling avoids ambiguous manipulation of infinite expressions. Taking the limit gives total 11. The remainder after NN terms is exactly 1/(N+1)1/(N+1), even though the next term is the much smaller 1/[(N+1)(N+2)]1/[(N+1)(N+2)].

For any convergent series define its tail or remainder RN=S−SNR_N=S-S_N. It is the sum of all omitted terms, not just the next term. In a model where terms measure energy in joules, both partial sums and remainders measure joules. A numerical stopping rule must control this entire tail in the required units.

A series is never literally formed by completing infinitely many additions one after another. It is defined by the limit of finite partial sums. This order of definition protects algebraic manipulations: first derive an identity for SNS_N, then take N→∞N\to\infty if that limit exists. For a geometric series with ∣r∣<1|r|<1, the finite identity SN=a(1−rN)/(1−r)S_N=a(1-r^N)/(1-r) shows both the sum a/(1−r)a/(1-r) and exact tail arN/(1−r)ar^N/(1-r). The tail formula turns convergence into a practical stopping rule when aa and rr are known.

The term test is one-way. If ∑an\sum a_n converges, then an=Sn−Sn−1→0a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\to0. The converse fails because small positive terms can accumulate forever. For the harmonic series, each term becomes tiny but the grouped blocks still contribute at least one half. A useful habit is to check an→0a_n\to0 first: failure proves divergence immediately; success merely permits further tests. Do not call it “the convergence test” without that qualification. The same subtlety appears in numerical integration, where a small rate over an infinite domain need not yield a finite total.

The geometric series has several boundary cases. If r=1r=1 and a≠0a\ne0, every term equals aa and the partial sums SN=NaS_N=Na diverge linearly. If r=−1r=-1 and a≠0a\ne0, partial sums alternate between two values. If ∣r∣>1|r|>1 and a≠0a\ne0, term magnitudes grow. If a=0a=0, the series is identically zero for every rr, so statements such as “converges exactly when ∣r∣<1|r|<1” need the nonzero-aa qualification. For negative r∈(−1,0)r\in(-1,0), the partial sums oscillate around their limit with shrinking amplitude. A plot makes that pattern visible, while the finite formula proves it.

The remainder is a whole tail, not the first omitted term. For ∑n=1∞1/[n(n+1)]\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/[n(n+1)], the (N+1)(N+1)st term is about 1/N21/N^2, but the entire remainder is 1/(N+1)1/(N+1), about 1/N1/N. For a geometric series with ratio 0.60.6, the omitted tail is the first omitted term divided by 1−0.61-0.6, or 2.52.5 times that term. Using only the next term as an error estimate is valid for some alternating-series conditions developed later, but not for arbitrary positive series. For a series, the Cauchy condition applies to partial sums: every sufficiently late block sum aN+1+⋯+aMa_{N+1}+\cdots+a_M must be small, uniformly for all M>NM>N. The necessary term condition tests only a block of length one, so it is weaker. In the harmonic series, individual late terms are small but sufficiently long late blocks have substantial total. For a positive series, bounding the entire tail is exactly the practical version of this condition. An exact geometric or telescoping remainder is therefore more than a computational convenience; it exhibits the required control of all future contributions.

A series is not merely a sequence with plus signs. Its object is the sequence of partial sums SN=∑n=1NanS_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_n, and convergence means that SNS_N approaches a finite number as NN grows. The necessary condition an→0a_n\to0 follows because aN=SN−SN−1a_N=S_N-S_{N-1} and both partial sums approach the same limit. The converse fails: for the harmonic series an=1/na_n=1/n tends to zero, but the partial sums grow without bound. A useful geometric explanation groups terms into blocks: from n=2k−1+1n=2^{k-1}+1 through 2k2^k, there are 2k−12^{k-1} terms each at least 1/2k1/2^k, so each block adds at least 1/21/2. Infinitely many such blocks force unbounded growth. This argument shows why small individual contributions need not have a small accumulated total.

Integral tests and the boundary at p = 1

Suppose ff is positive, continuous, and nonincreasing on [1,∞)[1,\infty), and an=f(n)a_n=f(n). The integral test states that ∑an\sum a_n and the improper integral ∫1∞f(x) dx\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx either both converge or both diverge. The same conclusion holds if these assumptions begin at some later integer: a finite prefix cannot change convergence. The test compares existence of a finite total, not equality of the integral and the series.

Here is the geometric reason. On each unit interval a decreasing graph lies between rectangles whose heights are its endpoint values. For a convergent positive series this gives ∫N+1∞f(x) dx≤RN≤∫N∞f(x) dx\int_{N+1}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx\le R_N\le\int_N^{\infty}f(x)\,dx. Summing the finite rectangle inequalities and then taking limits proves these bounds and the convergence equivalence. The required positivity prevents cancellation from invalidating this particular comparison.

Apply the test to f(x)=x−pf(x)=x^{-p} for p>0p>0. The antiderivative when p≠1p\ne1 is x1−p/(1−p)x^{1-p}/(1-p). Its improper integral converges precisely when p>1p>1. At p=1p=1 the antiderivative is ln⁡x\ln x, which grows without bound. Thus the p-series ∑n=1∞1/np\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^p converges exactly for p>1p>1; for p≤0p\le0 its terms do not even tend to zero. The endpoint p=1p=1 must be examined separately from the power antiderivative.

The harmonic terms 1/n1/n approach zero, yet their sum diverges. Another proof groups the terms with indices 2k−1+12^{k-1}+1 through 2k2^k. Each group has 2k−12^{k-1} terms, each at least 1/2k1/2^k, so each group contributes at least 1/21/2. Arbitrarily many such groups force the partial sums above every bound. A plot can look nearly flat because logarithmic growth is slow; its appearance cannot overturn this argument.

The integral test rests on a monotone comparison between rectangles and area. If ff is positive and decreasing, each term f(n)f(n) is the height of a left rectangle on [n,n+1][n,n+1], so it exceeds the area under ff there; f(n+1)f(n+1) is a right height and lies below the area. Summing these inequalities and then taking limits traps the tail RNR_N between improper integrals beginning at N+1N+1 and NN. The positivity makes all partial sums monotone; the decrease establishes the rectangle ordering. If ff oscillates sharply or changes sign, the quoted inequalities need not hold even if some related integral happens to converge.

The p-series threshold can be remembered through how quickly an infinite tail shrinks. For p=2p=2, RNR_N lies between 1/(N+1)1/(N+1) and 1/N1/N, so 10001000 terms give an upper bound of 0.0010.001 (at most), while 10011001 terms make this displayed bound strictly below 0.0010.001. The exact numerical sum of a p-series may be difficult, but convergence and a useful error certificate often follow from the integral comparison alone.

A finite prefix cannot change convergence. If ff is eventually decreasing but has a few irregular early values, start the integral test at an index beyond them. Prove the tail converges or diverges, then add the finite prefix. This is an instance of a general principle: convergence is a tail property, while the final sum depends on every term. The exact lower and upper remainder bounds use the index where monotonicity begins and must be shifted accordingly. Copying a bound indexed from one onto a series starting at zero can create an off-by-one numerical error.

The integral test does not say ∑n=1∞f(n)=∫1∞f(x) dx\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)=\int_1^{\infty}f(x)\,dx. The test compares convergence and bounds the tail; it does not replace discrete accumulation with continuous accumulation exactly. The difference between sum and integral may remain significant even when both converge. Use the correct object when answering a modeled count of discrete cycles.

Comparison transfers known behavior

The direct comparison test is an order argument for nonnegative terms. If 0≤an≤bn0\le a_n\le b_n eventually and ∑bn\sum b_n converges, then ∑an\sum a_n converges. Its partial sums are bounded by a finite initial contribution plus the comparison total. If instead 0≤bn≤an0\le b_n\le a_n eventually and ∑bn\sum b_n diverges, then ∑an\sum a_n diverges. Being below a divergent series or above a convergent one gives no conclusion.

For example, 0<1/(n2+3)≤1/n20<1/(n^2+3)\le1/n^2, so its series converges. Conversely, n/(n2+1)≥1/(2n)n/(n^2+1)\ge1/(2n) for n≥1n\ge1, since n2+1≤2n2n^2+1\le2n^2. Its series diverges by comparison with one half of the harmonic series. The inequality direction is the essential step; resemblance alone is not a proof.

Limit comparison can formalize that resemblance. For eventually positive ana_n and bnb_n, if an/bn→ca_n/b_n\to c for a finite c>0c>0, then their series have the same convergence behavior. Eventually c/2<an/bn<3c/2c/2<a_n/b_n<3c/2, so direct comparison applies in both directions. A ratio limit of zero or infinity does not give this two-way conclusion. For rational expressions, comparing their leading powers often provides an efficient positive reference series.

A practical investigation begins by naming the terms and their index range, checking the necessary zero limit, and looking for a finite-sum identity. If those do not settle it, positivity may permit comparison or an integral. Record which hypotheses were checked and what tail bound, if any, was obtained. Convergence alone does not say how many terms give a requested accuracy. The next chapter develops further tests for signs and faster-growing expressions.

For a rational term, leading powers often suggest the comparison but do not complete it. Consider an=(4n2+1)/(n3+7)a_n=(4n^2+1)/(n^3+7), which behaves like 4/n4/n. Compute an/(1/n)=n(4n2+1)/(n3+7)→4a_n/(1/n)=n(4n^2+1)/(n^3+7)\to4. Because the positive limit is finite and nonzero, limit comparison with the harmonic series proves divergence. Simply dropping the lower-order terms without a limit or inequality would be a heuristic. The ratio calculation shows precisely why the omitted terms do not change convergence.

Direct comparison is sometimes simpler than a limit. Since 0<1/(n2+4n)≤1/n20<1/(n^2+4n)\le1/n^2 for n≥1n\ge1, the series converges. But an upper comparison can also give a tail bound: RN≤∑n>N1/n2≤∫N∞x−2 dx=1/NR_N\le\sum_{n>N}1/n^2\le\int_N^{\infty}x^{-2}\,dx=1/N. Thus N=1000N=1000 suffices for an upper bound of 0.0010.001. Deeper derivation · A series is a limit of accumulated tails

The series ∑n=1∞an\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n converges when its partial sums SNS_N approach a finite number. The Cauchy criterion removes the unknown limit: for every ε>0\varepsilon>0 there is an NN such that ∣am+1+⋯+an∣<ε|a_{m+1}+\cdots+a_n|<\varepsilon whenever n>m≥Nn>m\ge N. In words, every sufficiently late finite tail must be small. Individual terms tending to zero is necessary, because an=Sn−Sn−1a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}, but it does not control the sum of many terms.

The harmonic series ∑n=1∞1/n\sum_{n=1}^\infty1/n shows the gap. Group terms after 11 as 1/21/2, then 1/3+1/41/3+1/4, then 1/5+⋯+1/81/5+\cdots+1/8, and so on. Chapter synthesis

  • A sequence converges when every sufficiently late term is close to one finite value. Monotonicity and an appropriate bound guarantee such a limit.
  • A series converges when its partial sums converge; vanishing terms alone do not suffice.
  • Geometric and telescoping identities can give exact sums and remainders. Positive-term integral and comparison tests prove convergence or divergence, and integral bounds can certify tails.

Common mistakes to check

  • “A bounded sequence must converge.” The sequence (−1)n(-1)^n stays in [−1,1][-1,1] but alternates forever. Monotonicity is an additional hypothesis in the monotone bounded theorem. Test with problem 8
  • “Once the next term is tiny, the total is accurate.” The remaining infinitely many terms can outweigh that term, and the series may even diverge. Control a proven tail bound. Test with problem 33
