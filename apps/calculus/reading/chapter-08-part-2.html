Concept checks

  1. What does the mean value theorem require?

    • Continuity on [a,b][a,b] and differentiability on (a,b)(a,b)
    • Only endpoint values
    • A positive derivative everywhere

    Answer. Continuity on [a,b][a,b] and differentiability on (a,b)(a,b). Continuity on the closed interval and differentiability in its interior suffice to invoke the theorem and guarantee a matching secant slope. A matching derivative can still exist when a hypothesis fails; the theorem then supplies no guarantee.

  2. If f′<0f^{\prime}<0 throughout an interval, then ff is…

    • increasing
    • decreasing
    • constant

    Answer. decreasing. The mean value theorem makes every later function value smaller.

  3. Can f′(a)=0f^{\prime}(a)=0 occur at a non-extremum?

    • No
    • Yes, at a stationary inflection
    • Only at endpoints

    Answer. Yes, at a stationary inflection. x3x^3 has derivative zero at zero while increasing through it.

  4. Where can an absolute extreme on [a,b][a,b] occur?

    • Only where f′=0f^{\prime}=0
    • At critical points or endpoints
    • Only at endpoints

    Answer. At critical points or endpoints. The closed-interval method checks all interior critical candidates and both endpoints.

  5. Does f′′(a)=0f^{\prime\prime}(a)=0 alone prove an inflection?

    • Yes
    • No; concavity must change
    • Only if f′(a)=0f^{\prime}(a)=0

    Answer. No; concavity must change. A zero second derivative is inconclusive unless the concavity actually changes.

  6. For F(x)=∣x∣−x2F(x)=|x|-x^2 on [−1,1][-1,1], which statement is correct?

    • The mean value theorem cannot be invoked, yet F′(−1/2)=F′(1/2)=0F^{\prime}(-1/2)=F^{\prime}(1/2)=0
    • The mean value theorem applies and guarantees both zero-slope points.
    • The mean value theorem cannot be invoked, so no interior zero derivative exists.

    Answer. The mean value theorem cannot be invoked, yet F′(−1/2)=F′(1/2)=0F^{\prime}(-1/2)=F^{\prime}(1/2)=0. The secant slope is zero. The corner at zero breaks interior differentiability, but solving on the two smooth sides gives exactly −1/2-1/2 and 1/21/2. A failed hypothesis removes a guarantee; it does not forbid the conclusion.

    Why the other choices fail.

    • The mean value theorem applies and guarantees both zero-slope points. — Continuity does not repair the missing derivative at the interior corner; the theorem cannot be invoked.
    • The mean value theorem cannot be invoked, so no interior zero derivative exists. — A theorem that cannot be invoked may still have a true conclusion. Check both derivative formulas.

Chapter synthesis

  • Existence theorems require their full domain and regularity hypotheses; critical-point equations only identify candidates.
  • The mean value theorem turns derivative signs and bounds into statements about change across intervals.
  • First derivative signs classify turning points; second derivative signs describe concavity, and a zero second derivative can be inconclusive.
  • L’Hôpital’s rule evaluates specified indeterminate quotients when the derivative quotient has an appropriate limit.
  • Numerical sign charts illustrate analytic conclusions and can miss small features.

Common mistakes to check

  • “Every zero of the derivative is a maximum or minimum.” For x3x^3 the derivative vanishes at zero while the function increases through it. Check signs. Test with problem 32
  • “A zero second derivative marks an inflection.” For x4x^4 the concavity stays up across zero. Check a change of concavity at a graph point. Test with problem 15
  • “A smooth-looking plot verifies the mean value theorem hypotheses.” A plot samples finitely many points and can hide a corner, a hole, or a narrow feature. Establish continuity and differentiability analytically. Test with problem 33
