1. A curve is a path with a parameter

A graph y=f(x)y=f(x) associates one height with each horizontal coordinate. A circle cannot be represented by one such graph: most horizontal positions have two heights. A parametrization instead gives both coordinates from a third variable: x=x(t)x=x(t), y=y(t)y=y(t), a≤t≤ba\le t\le b. As tt increases, the point follows a path with a specified direction. The interval belongs to the description; changing it can remove part of the path or cause the same part to be traversed several times.

For example, x(t)=Rcos⁡tx(t)=R\cos t and y(t)=Rsin⁡ty(t)=R\sin t, with R>0R>0 and 0≤t≤2π0\le t\le2\pi, trace a circle counterclockwise once. On 0≤t≤π0\le t\le\pi they trace only its upper semicircle. On 0≤t≤4π0\le t\le4\pi they travel around twice. Eliminating tt gives x2+y2=R2x^2+y^2=R^2 in every case, but this equation loses direction, starting point, and number of traversals. It also cannot tell whether the parameter interval covers the entire circle.

If tt represents time in seconds and xx and yy are measured in meters, derivatives x′(t)x^{\prime}(t) and y′(t)y^{\prime}(t) are velocity components in meters per second. If tt is an angle or an abstract label, the derivatives describe change per unit of that parameter. This distinction matters: the same geometric circle can be followed slowly, quickly, or with varying speed. Geometry alone does not specify motion.

A point where (x′,y′)≠(0,0)(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\ne(0,0) is called regular. Regularity guarantees a nonzero instantaneous direction, although the tangent may be vertical. A zero velocity requires separate examination; it might represent a cusp, an ordinary smooth point described by a poor parameter, or a pause. For x=t3x=t^3, y=0y=0, both derivatives vanish at t=0t=0, yet the image is simply a horizontal line. Do not infer a geometric singularity from zero derivatives alone.

A parameterization is a map from an interval into the plane. Its image is a set of points, while the map itself remembers order, direction, and repetitions. The maps (cos⁡t,sin⁡t)(\cos t,\sin t) and (cos⁡2t,sin⁡2t)(\cos 2t,\sin 2t) on 0≤t≤2π0\le t\le2\pi have the same image, the unit circle, but the second travels twice. The maps (cos⁡t,sin⁡t)(\cos t,\sin t) and (cos⁡t,−sin⁡t)(\cos t,-\sin t) traverse the same circle in opposite directions. These differences matter for velocity, oriented line integrals, and traveled length. Always give the interval with the coordinate formulas.

Eliminating a parameter can help identify geometry but can introduce extra points or lose restrictions. For x=t2x=t^2, y=ty=t with 0≤t≤10\le t\le1, elimination gives x=y2x=y^2, yet that equation includes the branch with negative yy unless the original restriction 0≤y≤10\le y\le1 is retained. Conversely, a closed curve may require several yy-values for one xx, making an explicit y=f(x)y=f(x) representation awkward. Parameterization is not merely an alternative notation; it is often the more complete description of a moving point or a multi-branch curve.

Regularity depends on the chosen parameter. The path (t3,0)(t^3,0) has zero derivative at t=0t=0 yet its image is a straight line; reparameterizing by s=t3s=t^3 gives (s,0)(s,0) with nonzero derivative. At a genuine cusp such as (t2,t3)(t^2,t^3), the image has two branches meeting at the origin and cannot be repaired into one smooth regular arc through that point by a simple monotone parameter change. Looking at the image, limiting secants, and nearby tangent directions is safer than declaring every zero-velocity point a cusp.

A change of parameter must also transform the interval. If t=2st=2s, then x=cos⁡tx=\cos t, y=sin⁡ty=\sin t over 0≤t≤2π0\le t\le2\pi becomes x=cos⁡(2s)x=\cos(2s), y=sin⁡(2s)y=\sin(2s) over 0≤s≤π0\le s\le\pi for one full lap. Keeping 0≤s≤2π0\le s\le2\pi instead doubles the traversal. If the change reverses direction, endpoint order changes accordingly. The chain rule scales derivative components, but physical speed only has meaning when the parameter actually represents time. These checks prevent a geometric reparameterization from being mistaken for a new physical motion.

A path can be retraced without changing its image. Suppose a parameter first runs around a circle counterclockwise and then returns clockwise. The set of occupied points is still one circle, but traveled length counts both trips and net displacement is zero. An oriented line integral may cancel between the two trips, while an ordinary length integral adds their positive speeds. The correct quantity therefore depends on whether the problem asks about geometry, motion, or orientation. Stating the parameter interval and direction at the start helps decide which mathematical operation matches the question. This remains true even when the plotted image looks exactly the same.

2. Slopes and second derivatives

Suppose xx and yy are continuously differentiable near t0t_0 and x′(t0)≠0x^{\prime}(t_0)\ne0. Locally, xx can serve as a coordinate along the curve. The chain rule y′(t)=(dy/dx)x′(t)y^{\prime}(t)=(dy/dx)x^{\prime}(t) then gives dy/dx=y′(t)/x′(t)dy/dx=y^{\prime}(t)/x^{\prime}(t). This slope compares two simultaneous changes. It is not the speed of the point: a horizontal path has zero slope even when a particle travels along it rapidly.

If xx and yy have continuous second derivatives and x′≠0x^{\prime}\ne0, differentiate the slope with respect to tt and divide once more by x′x^{\prime}. Thus d2ydx2=x′y′′−y′x′′(x′)3\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}=\dfrac{x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}}{(x^{\prime})^3}. The extra division converts change per unit parameter into change per unit horizontal coordinate. Merely computing y′′/x′′y^{\prime\prime}/x^{\prime\prime} does not make this conversion and generally gives the wrong result. These derivatives describe a local graph branch, which matters when several branches pass through the same coordinate.

When x′=0x^{\prime}=0 but y′≠0y^{\prime}\ne0, the tangent is vertical. One can describe xx locally as a function of yy and use dx/dy=x′/y′dx/dy=x^{\prime}/y^{\prime} instead. When y′=0y^{\prime}=0 but x′≠0x^{\prime}\ne0, the tangent is horizontal. When both vanish, the quotient is inconclusive: inspect the first nonzero changes in xx and yy or examine limiting secant directions. At interval endpoints, only the direction supported by the available one-sided parameter values is relevant.

For x=t2x=t^2 and y=t3y=t^3, the ordinary slope away from zero is 3t/23t/2. Its limit is zero from both sides, but the curve has a cusp at the origin: xx is nonnegative and the two branches meet there. A horizontal limiting tangent does not imply that the whole curve near that point is the graph of one differentiable function of xx. The upper and lower branches separately satisfy y=±x3/2y=\pm x^{3/2}.

Derive the parametric slope from a tangent vector. At a regular point, a small parameter change produces displacement approximately (x′(t)Δt,y′(t)Δt)(x^{\prime}(t)\Delta t,y^{\prime}(t)\Delta t). When x′≠0x^{\prime}\ne0, the ratio of vertical to horizontal displacement approaches y′/x′y^{\prime}/x^{\prime}. For x=tx=t, y=t2y=t^2, the slope is 2t2t, matching ordinary differentiation after eliminating tt. For x=t2x=t^2, y=ty=t, the slope is 1/(2t)1/(2t) away from zero and grows without bound in magnitude as t→0t\to0, indicating a vertical tangent. The same image relation x=y2x=y^2 confirms that local orientation.

Second derivatives require differentiating the slope with respect to xx, not with respect to tt alone. Write Dx=(1/x′)DtD_x=(1/x^{\prime})D_t where x′≠0x^{\prime}\ne0. Then Dx(y′/x′)=(x′y′′−y′x′′)/(x′)3D_x(y^{\prime}/x^{\prime})=(x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime})/(x^{\prime})^3. For x=tx=t, y=t3y=t^3, this gives 6t6t, the ordinary second derivative of x3x^3. For x=t2x=t^2, y=t3y=t^3 at t≠0t\ne0, the first slope is 3t/23t/2 and the second derivative is (3/2)/(2t)=3/(4t)(3/2)/(2t)=3/(4t), which differs on the two branches and becomes unbounded near the cusp. State where x′x^{\prime} is nonzero before using either formula.

Horizontal and vertical tangents can be checked from derivative components at regular points. If y′=0y^{\prime}=0 and x′≠0x^{\prime}\ne0, the tangent is horizontal; if x′=0x^{\prime}=0 and y′≠0y^{\prime}\ne0, it is vertical. On the circle (Rcos⁡t,Rsin⁡t)(R\cos t,R\sin t), at t=0t=0 the tangent is vertical because x′=0x^{\prime}=0, y′=Ry^{\prime}=R; at t=π/2t=\pi/2 it is horizontal because x′=−Rx^{\prime}=-R, y′=0y^{\prime}=0. A graph may show this, but the derivative test supplies exact locations. When both derivatives vanish, neither conclusion follows from the component test alone.

A parametric path may cross itself, so a coordinate point may have several tangent directions corresponding to different parameter values. If (x(t1),y(t1))=(x(t2),y(t2))(x(t_1),y(t_1))=(x(t_2),y(t_2)) with t1≠t2t_1\ne t_2, evaluate derivatives at both times. There is no single dy/dxdy/dx for the entire image at that crossing unless the branch tangents happen to agree. This is another reason to retain tt in derivative formulas rather than eliminating it and pretending the image is one graph. The local branch, not merely the coordinate pair, determines the slope being requested.

The derivative ratio y′/x′y^{\prime}/x^{\prime} is unchanged by a smooth monotone reparameterization where the new parameter speed is nonzero: both numerator and denominator gain the same chain factor. Thus slope is a geometric property of the regular branch, while x′x^{\prime} and y′y^{\prime} separately depend on timing. At a zero of the reparameterization derivative, the new coordinate derivatives may both vanish even though the branch has a clear tangent, so the quotient becomes 0/00/0 there. Return to the original regular parameter or use limiting secants. This explains how a poor clock can create an apparent derivative failure without changing the curve.

3. Speed and arc length

Over a short parameter increment Δt\Delta t, the displacement is approximately (x′Δt,y′Δt)(x^{\prime}\Delta t,y^{\prime}\Delta t). The Pythagorean theorem gives its magnitude as (x′)2+(y′)2∣Δt∣\sqrt{(x^{\prime})^2+(y^{\prime})^2}|\Delta t|. When tt is time, the factor (x′)2+(y′)2\sqrt{(x^{\prime})^2+(y^{\prime})^2} is speed. It is always nonnegative; reversing direction changes velocity but does not produce negative distance.

For a continuously differentiable path on a closed finite interval, its traveled length is L=∫abx′(t)2+y′(t)2 dtL=\int_a^b\sqrt{x^{\prime}(t)^2+y^{\prime}(t)^2}\,dt. The same formula applies to a piecewise continuously differentiable path by adding its pieces. Proof sketch: inscribed polygonal paths approximate the curve, each short chord approximates the derivative magnitude times its parameter width, and continuity controls the accumulated error as the partition is refined. This explains why the integral measures length; a numerical polygon by itself is not a proof. For every partition, the vector integral triangle inequality bounds each chord by the integral of ∣r′∣|\mathbf r^{\prime}| over that piece. Its chord sum is therefore at most ∫ab∣r′(t)∣ dt\int_a^b|\mathbf r^{\prime}(t)|\,dt. The fine-mesh chord sums approach that integral, so the supremum of all polygonal chord sums equals it.

Length counts every traversal. For the radius-RR circle over 0≤t≤4π0\le t\le4\pi, speed is RR and length is 4πR4\pi R, although the geometric boundary has circumference 2πR2\pi R. If an application asks for wire needed to form the boundary once, repeated tracing must be removed. If it asks for distance traveled by a particle, the repetitions belong in the answer. A smooth monotone change of parameter preserves traveled length through substitution, provided the new bounds describe the same traversal.

Displacement is different again: a complete circuit has zero displacement and positive length. For a time interval of duration TT, average speed is L/TL/T, whereas average velocity is the displacement vector divided by TT. If coordinates have length units and the parameter has time units, integrating speed restores length units. In numerical work, a polygonal chord sum underestimates the length of a circular arc; refining the parameter grid improves that estimate without turning it into an exact result.

Length is invariant under a smooth monotone reparameterization that describes the same traversal. If t=g(s)t=g(s) increases from aa to bb, then dx/ds=x′(g(s))g′(s)dx/ds=x^{\prime}(g(s))g^{\prime}(s) and similarly for yy. The speed in ss is x′(g(s))2+y′(g(s))2 ∣g′(s)∣\sqrt{x^{\prime}(g(s))^2+y^{\prime}(g(s))^2}\,|g^{\prime}(s)|; substitution converts its integral back to the tt-length. The absolute value matters if gg decreases, because traveled distance cannot become negative when direction reverses. This invariance belongs to geometric path length, while velocity components and speed per unit parameter do change when the timing changes.

For x=3cos⁡tx=3\cos t, y=3sin⁡ty=3\sin t on 0≤t≤π0\le t\le\pi, speed is 9sin⁡2t+9cos⁡2t=3\sqrt{9\sin^2t+9\cos^2t}=3, so length is 3π3\pi, half the circumference. Extending to 3π3\pi gives 9π9\pi of travel, one and a half laps, while displacement from t=0t=0 to t=3πt=3\pi has magnitude 66. These quantities answer different questions. A closed or nearly closed image does not imply small travel. Label the parameter interval and distinguish the vector from start to end from the integral of speed.

For a graph y=f(x)y=f(x), choose parameter t=xt=x to recover the Cartesian formula: x′=1x^{\prime}=1 and y′=f′(x)y^{\prime}=f^{\prime}(x), so L=∫1+f′(x)2 dxL=\int\sqrt{1+f^{\prime}(x)^2}\,dx. Thus the parametric formula generalizes the earlier arc-length result rather than replacing it. If a graph has a vertical tangent, the xx-based derivative may become unbounded while a smooth parameterization can still have finite speed; this can make the parametric integral easier to analyze. A different parameter does not change the geometric length when it traces the same path once.

A chord sum underestimates the length of a circular arc because each straight chord is shorter than the corresponding curved portion. Refinement improves the estimate, but showing convergence to the speed integral needs regularity of the path. For a polygonal path, the speed integral over each straight piece reproduces its Euclidean segment length exactly. These two benchmark cases check a numerical arc-length routine. If the routine reports less than the endpoint distance for any path, it has violated a basic geometric lower bound.

For a piecewise smooth path, length adds across the breakpoints even when the tangent direction jumps. Split the parameter interval at each corner and integrate speed on the smooth pieces. The path need not be the graph of any one Cartesian function. A triangle traced along three straight sides is the simplest case: each speed integral equals a side length, and their sum is its perimeter. If a path retraces one side, that side contributes again to distance traveled. This additivity is the path analogue of splitting an ordinary definite integral at a change in formula.

4. Polar curves and swept area

A polar description r=r(θ)r=r(\theta) becomes a parametrization through x=r(θ)cos⁡θx=r(\theta)\cos\theta and y=r(θ)sin⁡θy=r(\theta)\sin\theta. Angles are in radians. A negative radius places the point in the direction opposite θ\theta: (r,θ)(r,\theta) and (−r,θ+π)(-r,\theta+\pi) represent the same point. At r=0r=0 every angle represents the origin. These identifications make polar notation convenient, but they make intersection and tracing questions subtler than simply comparing two radii.

A thin circular sector of radius rr and angular width Δθ\Delta\theta has area approximately r2Δθ/2r^2\Delta\theta/2. Summing sectors suggests A=12∫r(θ)2 dθA=\frac12\int r(\theta)^2\,d\theta. For an ordinary radial region with continuous r≥0r\ge0, increasing angular bounds spanning at most one revolution, and nonoverlapping sector interiors, this integral gives its geometric area. The requirement that the sectors describe the desired region once is essential; the integral otherwise counts swept contributions with multiplicity.

Negative radii do not make the squared integrand negative, but their rays must be interpreted correctly. Split at zeros when necessary and determine which loop each interval traces. For a simple closed polar loop traversed once with increasing θ\theta, the same area formula follows from 12∫(xy′−yx′) dθ\frac12\int(xy^{\prime}-yx^{\prime})\,d\theta, since xy′−yx′=r2xy^{\prime}-yx^{\prime}=r^2. Reversing the bounds reverses this signed integral; geometric area remains positive. With overlapping or repeated loops, the signed boundary integral is not automatically the area of the union.

For a region between two curves on the same rays, with 0≤rinner(θ)≤router(θ)0\le r_{\mathrm{inner}}(\theta)\le r_{\mathrm{outer}}(\theta), subtract sector areas: A=12∫(router(θ)2−rinner(θ)2) dθA=\frac12\int\bigl(r_{\mathrm{outer}}(\theta)^2-r_{\mathrm{inner}}(\theta)^2\bigr)\,d\theta. Solve for angular intersections and determine the ordering on every subinterval before integrating. Subtracting whole enclosed areas is valid only when the smaller region is actually contained in the larger. A quick sketch, a few point evaluations, and a statement of the bounds are part of the calculation.

The polar area factor one half follows from a sector. A full disk of radius rr has area πr2\pi r^2 and angle 2π2\pi, so a sector of angle Δθ\Delta\theta has area about (Δθ/2π)πr2=r2Δθ/2(\Delta\theta/2\pi)\pi r^2=r^2\Delta\theta/2. For a varying nonnegative radius, thin sectors use the local r(θ)r(\theta), leading to 12∫r(θ)2 dθ\frac12\int r(\theta)^2\,d\theta. Squaring the radius is required by area units: r dθr\,d\theta would have length units. The derivation assumes that the angular sectors cover the intended region without repeated overlap. A formula can be algebraically correct and geometrically misapplied if the tracing interval is wrong.

For the cardioid r=1+cos⁡θr=1+\cos\theta, the radius is nonnegative on 0≤θ≤2π0\le\theta\le2\pi and vanishes only at θ=π\theta=\pi. Its total area is 12∫02π(1+cos⁡θ)2 dθ\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}(1+\cos\theta)^2\,d\theta. Expand the square and use ∫02πcos⁡θ dθ=0\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta=0 and ∫02πcos⁡2θ dθ=π\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\pi over a full turn to get 12(2π+π)=3π/2\frac12(2\pi+\pi)=3\pi/2. The curve touches the origin at a cusp but the radial sectors still form the intended region once. This example links algebraic integration with a visible swept shape.

A signed polar radius can trace a point on a ray different from the displayed θ\theta. The pair (−2,0)(-2,0) represents Cartesian point (−2,0)(-2,0), the same as (2,π)(2,\pi). A curve that crosses r=0r=0 may change which angular sector its parameter labels. To compute geometric area, identify each loop and choose bounds tracing it once; then use nonnegative radial distances or a justified oriented boundary formula. Integrating r2r^2 over an arbitrary 00-to-2π2\pi interval can double-count a loop even though every integrand value is nonnegative.

For an annular sector with inner radius 11, outer radius 22, and angle π/2\pi/2, area is 12∫0π/2(4−1) dθ=3π/4\frac12\int_0^{\pi/2}(4-1)\,d\theta=3\pi/4. This agrees with one quarter of the difference between full disk areas 4π−π=3π4\pi-\pi=3\pi. The two routes check the factor one half and the angular bounds. If outer and inner polar curves cross, split where their radii exchange order; squaring and subtracting without checking that order can produce a negative integrand for a positive geometric area.

The sector formula has a signed boundary counterpart. For a smooth closed parametric curve traversed counterclockwise once without self-intersection, A=12∫(xy′−yx′) dtA=\frac12\int(xy^{\prime}-yx^{\prime})\,dt gives positive enclosed area. Clockwise traversal reverses the sign. Substituting x=rcos⁡θx=r\cos\theta and y=rsin⁡θy=r\sin\theta yields xy′−yx′=r2xy^{\prime}-yx^{\prime}=r^2, so the polar area formula follows for the standard increasing-angle traversal. With self-intersections, the boundary integral counts oriented regions with multiplicity rather than automatically returning the geometric area of the union. This is why tracing and orientation checks remain necessary even after deriving the compact formula.

A negative polar radius is a direction reversal, not an impossible distance. The pair (r,θ)(r,\theta) and (−r,θ+π)(-r,\theta+\pi) name the same Cartesian point because both give the same x=rcos⁡θx=r\cos\theta and y=rsin⁡θy=r\sin\theta. Consequently, a polar curve can revisit a point under different parameter values, and a symmetric-looking interval may trace a petal or loop more than once. Before applying A=12∫r2 dθA=\tfrac12\int r^2\,d\theta, identify a parameter interval that sweeps the target region exactly once. Squaring rr makes each local area contribution nonnegative, but it does not remove double counting from repeated tracing. Test a few boundary angles and zeros of rr to decide where a loop begins and ends.

5. Polar length, intersections, and tracing checks

Differentiate the Cartesian coordinates: x′=r′cos⁡θ−rsin⁡θx^{\prime}=r^{\prime}\cos\theta-r\sin\theta and y′=r′sin⁡θ+rcos⁡θy^{\prime}=r^{\prime}\sin\theta+r\cos\theta. Squaring and adding cancels the cross terms and uses sin⁡2θ+cos⁡2θ=1\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1. Consequently (x′)2+(y′)2=(r′)2+r2(x^{\prime})^2+(y^{\prime})^2=(r^{\prime})^2+r^2. For a continuously differentiable polar radius on [α,β][\alpha,\beta], or piecewise such a radius, the traveled length is therefore ∫αβr2+(r′)2 dθ\int_\alpha^\beta\sqrt{r^2+(r^{\prime})^2}\,d\theta. The formula allows negative radii; it also counts repeated traversal just as any parametric length formula does.

To find intersections of two polar curves, solving r1(θ)=r2(θ)r_1(\theta)=r_2(\theta) finds points represented by the same angle and signed radius. It may miss an intersection where one representation differs by π\pi and has the opposite radius. Also test whether each curve passes through the origin, possibly at different angles. More generally, seek parameters θ\theta and ϕ\phi for which both Cartesian coordinates agree.   parametric.push([a * (1 + Math.cos(t)), a * Math.sin(t)]);
