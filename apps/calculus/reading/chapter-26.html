1. Green’s theorem: circulation from a planar interior

A line integral measures what happens along a curve. A double integral measures what happens throughout a region. Green’s theorem connects them when the curve is the entire boundary of that region. Let DD be a bounded planar region with a piecewise smooth boundary, and let PP and QQ have continuous first partial derivatives on an open set containing DD and its boundary. With positive boundary orientation, the circulation integral of P dx+Q dyP\,dx+Q\,dy equals the integral of Qx−PyQ_x-P_y over DD. The integrand measures local circulation per unit area.

Positive orientation means that the region stays on your left as you travel along its boundary. For a disk this means counterclockwise travel. For a region with a hole, the outer boundary is counterclockwise and each inner boundary is clockwise. Every boundary component matters. Reversing the entire boundary reverses the line integral; the double integral acquires a corresponding minus sign if we deliberately use negative orientation.

A proof sketch for a vertically simple region explains the sign. Integrating −Py-P_y first in yy gives PP on the lower boundary minus PP on the upper boundary. These are exactly the contributions from traveling right along the bottom and left along the top. Integrating QxQ_x first in xx produces the other pair of edges. For more general regions, subdivide into suitable pieces: shared edges are traversed in opposite directions and cancel. This cancellation is the mechanism behind all three theorems in this chapter.

The flux version is equally useful: the outward planar flux of F=(P,Q)\mathbf F=(P,Q) is ∮∂D(P dy−Q dx)\oint_{\partial D}(P\,dy-Q\,dx), which equals ∬D(Px+Qy) dA\iint_D(P_x+Q_y)\,dA. Along a positively oriented curve, the outward normal element is n ds=(dy,−dx)\mathbf n\,ds=(dy,-dx). Thus circulation uses the tangential component, whereas flux uses the normal component. Do not interchange these expressions merely because they integrate along the same boundary.

Green's circulation formula can be checked on a rectangle before trusting a complicated curve. For F=(−y,x)\mathbf F=(-y,x), the scalar curl Qx−PyQ_x-P_y is 1−(−1)=21-(-1)=2. On [0,2]×[0,1][0,2]\times[0,1], the area is two, so positive circulation is four. Direct integration around its four edges gives the same answer. If the traversal is clockwise, the sign is −4-4. This example separates three ingredients: the field derivative, the enclosed area, and boundary orientation. A wrong sign in any one of them changes the result.

A region with a hole has a complete boundary containing more than one loop. On an annulus a≤r≤ba\le r\le b, positive orientation means outer circle counterclockwise and inner circle clockwise. For F=(−y/2,x/2)\mathbf F=(-y/2,x/2), the outer circulation is πb2\pi b^2 and the inner contribution is −πa2-\pi a^2, so the total is π(b2−a2)\pi(b^2-a^2), exactly the integral of scalar curl 11 over the annular area. Omitting the inner boundary would count a disk that is not in the region. The theorem is about the entire oriented boundary.

Green's flux form follows from the circulation form by rotating the field. If F=(P,Q)\mathbf F=(P,Q), the outward normal line element for a positively oriented planar boundary is n ds=(dy,−dx)\mathbf n\,ds=(dy,-dx). Thus F⋅n ds=P dy−Q dx\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=P\,dy-Q\,dx. Set the rotated field to (−Q,P)(-Q,P); its circulation integral is exactly this flux, and its scalar curl is Px+QyP_x+Q_y. This derivation shows why divergence pairs with outward flux while curl pairs with tangential circulation. Both are local derivatives converted to boundary measurements.

For a simple closed curve that is not positively oriented, one may reverse the given parameterization or insert a minus sign after applying Green's theorem to the positive orientation. A diagram should mark arrows, especially when a curve is described as “clockwise as seen from above.” For a planar hole, the inner clockwise direction is already part of positive orientation of the whole region. It is easy to reverse it twice by mistake if orientation is treated as a decorative note rather than part of the integral.

The field must be continuously differentiable on an open neighborhood of the region and its boundary in the elementary theorem. The angular field from chapter 25 is undefined at the origin. Applying Green's theorem to a full disk around that point would be invalid, even though its curl is zero at every point where it is defined. Applying it to an annulus that excludes the origin is valid and includes two boundary circles whose circulations cancel. The domain check comes before the derivative calculation.

Green’s theorem on a rectangle is cancellation at work

For a continuously differentiable planar field F=(P,Q)\mathbf F=(P,Q) on a neighborhood of a rectangle RR, counterclockwise circulation around ∂R\partial R equals the integral of scalar curl Qx−PyQ_x-P_y over RR.

∮∂R(P dx+Q dy)=∬R(Qx−Py) dA\oint_{\partial R}(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint_R(Q_x-P_y)\,dA
Reasoning and proof

Travel right along the bottom edge and left along the top. Their PP contributions are ∫abP(x,c) dx−∫abP(x,d) dx\int_a^bP(x,c)\,dx-\int_a^bP(x,d)\,dx. By the one-variable fundamental theorem in yy, P(x,d)−P(x,c)P(x,d)-P(x,c) is the integral of Py(x,y)P_y(x,y) from cc to dd. Thus the combined horizontal contribution is the negative integral of PyP_y over RR. The sign comes from the reversed top-edge orientation.

Travel upward on the right edge and downward on the left. Their QQ contributions are the integral of Q(b,y)Q(b,y) from cc to dd minus the integral of Q(a,y)Q(a,y) over that interval. The fundamental theorem in xx turns their difference into the integral of QxQ_x over RR. Add vertical and horizontal contributions; both are integrals over the same rectangle, yielding ∬R(Qx−Py) dA\iint_R(Q_x-P_y)\,dA.

A general simple region can be decomposed into small pieces. Every internal edge is traversed in opposite directions by adjacent pieces, so its line integrals cancel. Only the external boundary remains. Turning that picture into a theorem requires regularity of the boundary and field, as well as a suitable limiting argument. A hole creates an additional boundary component with its own orientation. The rectangle proof exposes the mechanism without pretending that a diagram alone proves every version of Green’s theorem.

2. Parametrized surfaces and their area elements

A surface patch is described by r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v))\mathbf r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)) for (u,v)(u,v) in a planar parameter region RR. The vectors ru\mathbf r_u and rv\mathbf r_v are tangent to its coordinate curves. A tiny parameter rectangle becomes approximately a parallelogram whose area is ∣ru×rv∣ du dv\lvert\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\rvert\,du\,dv. Regularity means this cross product is nonzero in the interior of a regular patch. A whole surface may require several patches; seams of area zero can be handled separately.

The scalar surface integral ∬Sg dS\iint_S g\,dS therefore becomes ∬Rg(r(u,v))∣ru×rv∣ du dv\iint_R g(\mathbf r(u,v))\lvert\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\rvert\,du\,dv. If gg is surface mass density in kilograms per square metre, this integral is mass in kilograms. A parametrization that covers a patch twice counts its contribution twice unless multiplicity is explicitly corrected. Parameter bounds and coverage are mathematical data, not merely convenient plotting choices.

Flux requires an orientation as well as area. Choose a continuous unit normal n\mathbf n on the surface. Then ∬SF⋅n dS\iint_S\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS becomes ∬RF(r(u,v))⋅(ru×rv) du dv\iint_R\mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv when the cross product points in the chosen direction. Reverse the cross product when necessary. The norm belongs in a scalar area integral; the signed vector belongs in a flux integral. If F\mathbf F is a fluid velocity in metres per second, its flux is a signed volume rate in cubic metres per second.

For a graph z=g(x,y)z=g(x,y), use r(x,y)=(x,y,g(x,y))\mathbf r(x,y)=(x,y,g(x,y)). The upward vector area element is n dS=(−gx,−gy,1) dx dy\mathbf n\,dS=(-g_x,-g_y,1)\,dx\,dy. This formula avoids separately normalizing the normal and then multiplying by the area factor. A sphere cannot be covered by a single globally regular longitude–latitude patch at its poles, but the usual spherical formula still integrates correctly because the exceptional parameter edges have area zero. A nonorientable surface, such as a Möbius strip, has no globally consistent choice of normal, so ordinary globally oriented flux is not defined on it.

For a graph z=g(x,y)z=g(x,y), the tangent vectors are rx=(1,0,gx)\mathbf r_x=(1,0,g_x) and ry=(0,1,gy)\mathbf r_y=(0,1,g_y). Their cross product is rx×ry=(−gx,−gy,1)\mathbf r_x\times\mathbf r_y=(-g_x,-g_y,1), which points upward. Its magnitude 1+gx2+gy2\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2} converts planar parameter area into actual surface area. For g=x+2yg=x+2y over the unit square, the surface-area factor is 6\sqrt6, so the slanted patch has area 6\sqrt6. If F=(0,0,3)\mathbf F=(0,0,3), upward flux is only 33 because F⋅(−1,−2,1)=3\mathbf F\cdot(-1,-2,1)=3. Flux and scalar area differ.

An oriented flux integral can be negative. It records flow through a surface in the chosen normal direction; a negative value means net flow in the opposite direction. For F=(0,0,3)\mathbf F=(0,0,3) through the preceding graph over the unit square, upward flux is 33. Reversing the normal makes it −3-3, while scalar surface area and any nonnegative surface mass stay positive. A computation that takes an absolute value of F⋅n\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n everywhere would answer a different question, summing unsigned crossing rather than net flux.

A sphere cannot be covered by one regular graph over a single plane because its top and bottom lie above the same base points. Use two graph patches, or spherical parameters away from coordinate singularities. For a closed-surface flux, each patch's normal must point outward; the upper graph uses an upward normal while the lower graph uses a downward normal. If both patches are assigned upward normals, their contributions may cancel incorrectly. The divergence theorem often avoids this patch bookkeeping when its hypotheses hold.

A surface parameterization can cover the same physical patch more than once. The scalar integral with ∣ru×rv∣\lvert\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\rvert then counts area with multiplicity, while oriented flux can count signed multiplicity depending on orientation. Before using a formula, identify the parameter domain and whether its interior maps injectively to the surface. Angular seams and poles in standard sphere coordinates are lower-dimensional exceptions that do not double the ordinary surface integral. A full extra turn in an angle would double it.

A useful physical test is a constant vertical field through a tilted planar patch. The flux equals field strength times the area of the patch’s horizontal projection, with a sign set by orientation. For the graph z=x+2yz=x+2y over the unit square, that projection has area one, so vertical field F=(0,0,3)\mathbf F=(0,0,3) gives flux three regardless of the patch’s larger slanted area 6\sqrt6. The dot product with the normal accounts for the tilt. This is why “flux equals magnitude times surface area” is usually false.

3. Curl, divergence, and Stokes’ theorem

For F=(P,Q,R)F=(P,Q,R), divergence is the scalar Px+Qy+RzP_x+Q_y+R_z. Positive divergence describes a local excess of outward flux over inward flux. Curl is the vector (Ry−Qz,Pz−Rx,Qx−Py)(R_y-Q_z,P_z-R_x,Q_x-P_y). Its component along a unit normal describes local circulation density in the corresponding plane. Curl is not a velocity, and divergence is not the magnitude of the field. Both involve spatial changes of components. For a velocity field, both have units of inverse seconds.

Stokes’ theorem says that the circulation of F\mathbf F around the boundary of an oriented surface equals the flux of ∇×F\nabla\times\mathbf F through that surface. Assume SS is a compact, orientable, piecewise smooth surface with piecewise smooth boundary and F\mathbf F has continuous first derivatives on an open neighborhood of SS. The boundary must have the orientation induced by the chosen normal. On an upward disk the boundary is counterclockwise when viewed from above: curling the fingers of the right hand along the boundary makes the thumb point upward.

The surface need not be flat. A bowl and a flat disk with the same boundary give the same curl flux when their orientations induce the same boundary direction and the field is smooth near both surfaces. This can replace a difficult integral over a curved surface by a simple integral over a disk. It does not say that the flux of F\mathbf F itself is independent of the spanning surface. The field being integrated over the surface in Stokes’ theorem is ∇×F\nabla\times\mathbf F.

A proof sketch partitions the surface into small oriented patches and applies the planar circulation idea in local coordinates. Tangential contributions on interior edges cancel, leaving only the actual boundary. Green’s circulation theorem is the special case of a planar surface in z=0z=0 with upward normal. For a closed oriented surface there is no boundary, so the total flux of ∇×F\nabla\times\mathbf F is zero under the stated smoothness assumptions; no volume extension is required for that conclusion.

The curl vector has components determined by circulation in the three coordinate planes. Its zz component Qx−PyQ_x-P_y is the planar scalar curl used in Green's theorem. For F=(−y/2,x/2,0)\mathbf F=(-y/2,x/2,0), ∇×F=(0,0,1)\nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,0,1). A horizontal disk with upward normal therefore has curl flux equal to its area. Its boundary direction, viewed from above, is counterclockwise by the right-hand rule. Reversing the disk normal reverses both flux and induced boundary direction, keeping Stokes' equality intact.

The right-hand rule is an orientation convention, not a force law. Point the right thumb along the chosen surface normal; curled fingers show positive boundary traversal. On a flat disk this is easy to visualize. On a curved surface, use the same local convention consistently along its edge. If a space curve is prescribed clockwise from above, an upward-oriented spanning surface has the wrong induced direction; choose downward orientation or negate a result calculated upward.

Stokes' theorem allows a convenient spanning surface when the field is smooth near the relevant surfaces. A tilted or curved cap may have a complicated normal, while a flat disk with the same boundary makes curl flux simple. The two oriented surfaces must induce the same traversal of the common boundary. This does not justify choosing a spanning disk through a singularity of the field. The domain condition survives the surface swap.

The identity ∇⋅(∇×F)=0\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0 holds for fields with sufficiently continuous second derivatives, because mixed partial terms cancel in pairs. It is a local identity and helps check calculations. It also implies that the total flux of curl through a closed surface is zero under the divergence theorem's hypotheses. A closed surface has no boundary, so Stokes' theorem likewise predicts zero circulation around its empty boundary when appropriately interpreted. These consistency relations connect the two boundary theorems.

Green's theorem is the planar case of Stokes' theorem. Embed a planar region in z=0z=0 with normal +k+\mathbf k, extend F=(P,Q,0)\mathbf F=(P,Q,0), and note that (∇×F)⋅k=Qx−Py(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf k=Q_x-P_y. Its boundary circulation is ∮∂D(P dx+Q dy)\oint_{\partial D}(P\,dx+Q\,dy). The general surface theorem adds arbitrary orientable surfaces in three dimensions while preserving the same circulation-versus-curl structure. Recognizing this relationship helps choose a theorem without memorizing unrelated formulas.

4. The divergence theorem and conservation

Let VV be a bounded solid with a closed piecewise smooth boundary and let FF have continuous first derivatives on an open set containing VV and its boundary. The divergence theorem equates outward flux across the entire boundary with the triple integral of div⁡F\operatorname{div}F inside. A lidless cylinder is not a closed boundary. Either integrate directly or add the missing lid, apply the theorem to the resulting closed surface, and subtract its outward flux.

Outward means away from the material of VV. For a solid with an internal cavity, the cavity boundary normal points into the empty cavity. This agrees with the same cancellation principle: partition the volume into small boxes, use the one-variable fundamental theorem on opposite faces, and cancel flux across all internal faces. Only the external boundary and cavity boundaries remain. This argument is a proof sketch; extending it to curved boundaries requires approximation and regularity control.

The hypotheses prevent a serious trap. The field F=(x,y,z)/(x2+y2+z2)3/2\mathbf F=(x,y,z)/(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2} has divergence zero away from the origin, yet its outward flux across a sphere centered at the origin is 4π4\pi. There is no contradiction: F\mathbf F is undefined at the origin. On a spherical shell the theorem does apply. The outer sphere contributes 4π4\pi and the inner boundary, oriented toward the origin, contributes −4π-4\pi, so the total is zero.

For a mass flux field J\mathbf J measured in kilograms per square metre per second, ∇⋅J\nabla\cdot\mathbf J is a net outflow rate per unit volume. If density ρ\rho changes with time and mass is neither created nor destroyed, the integral balance is ddt∭Vρ dV=−∬∂VJ⋅n dS\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_V\rho\,dV=-\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf n\,dS for a fixed volume VV. Under sufficient differentiability, the divergence theorem leads to ρt+∇⋅J=0\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0. A negative sign is necessary: net outward transport decreases the mass inside.

For F=(x,y,z)\mathbf F=(x,y,z) on a radius-RR ball centered at the origin, ∇⋅F=3\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3. The volume integral is 3(4πR3/3)=4πR33(4\pi R^3/3)=4\pi R^3. Directly on the sphere, the outward unit normal is n=(x,y,z)/R\mathbf n=(x,y,z)/R, so F⋅n=R\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=R, and multiplying by sphere area 4πR24\pi R^2 gives the same result. The direct calculation is a useful check on orientation and the divergence theorem. If the normal points inward, the flux becomes −4πR3-4\pi R^3 and no longer matches the outward convention.

For F=(x2,y,z)\mathbf F=(x^2,y,z) on the unit cube, divergence is 2x+22x+2. Integrating over the cube gives ∫01(2x+2) dx=3\int_0^1(2x+2)\,dx=3. A face-by-face calculation agrees: the x=1x=1 face contributes 11, x=0x=0 contributes 00, y=1y=1 contributes 11, y=0y=0 contributes 00, z=1z=1 contributes 11, and z=0z=0 contributes 00. Listing all six faces can reveal an omitted side or a wrong normal. The divergence route is shorter because it sums local sources throughout the volume.

For a shell a≤r≤ba\le r\le b, 0<a<b0<a<b, retain both surfaces in ∂V\partial V. With F=(x,y,z)\mathbf F=(x,y,z), the outward flux is 4πb3−4πa34\pi b^3-4\pi a^3, equal to divergence 33 times the shell volume. The negative inner contribution follows because the shell’s outward normal points into the cavity. This repeats the ball arithmetic only to test a new boundary component; it does not allow the singular radial field to be used on a full ball.

For the singular field F=r/∣r∣3\mathbf F=\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3, divergence is zero only for r>0r>0. On a shell ε≤r≤R\varepsilon\le r\le R, the outer flux 4π4\pi and inward-pointing inner-boundary flux −4π-4\pi cancel. Letting ε→0\varepsilon\to0 does not restore the field value at the origin, so the ordinary theorem still cannot be applied to the full ball. A distributional point source is a separate extension.

The fixed-volume mass balance above becomes local when it holds for every sufficiently small volume and ρ,J\rho,\mathbf J are smooth enough to pass the time derivative inside: ∭V(ρt+∇⋅J) dV=0\iiint_V(\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J)\,dV=0. Continuity of the integrand then forces ρt+∇⋅J=0\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0 pointwise. If sources exist, their density belongs on the right side. The negative sign in the original outward-flux balance encodes loss of mass inside.

5. Choosing the boundary principle

First identify the quantity. A planar circulation integral suggests Green; a space-curve circulation suggests Stokes; outward flux through a closed surface suggests divergence. Next identify the region, its complete boundary, and any singularities of the field. Finally choose the easier side to calculate. A theorem is useful even when direct integration is possible because it supplies an independent check and explains which geometric features determine the answer.

These results share the fundamental theorem’s structure: integrating a derivative over an interior gives oriented data on its boundary. In one dimension, the endpoints carry opposite signs. In two dimensions, neighboring edges cancel. In three dimensions, neighboring faces cancel. The derivative and the boundary measurement must match: curl with circulation, divergence with flux. Numerical agreement illustrates this structure but cannot establish smoothness or rule out an unsampled singularity.

Choose Green's theorem when the requested boundary is a closed planar curve and the field is regular on its enclosed region. Choose Stokes when a space curve bounds an orientable surface and circulation is requested. Choose divergence when the requested quantity is outward flux through a closed surface around a solid. A scalar line integral against dsds is none of these boundary data; it measures length-weighted accumulation and is not converted by curl or divergence. Identifying the differential element first prevents applying the wrong theorem to a familiar-looking curve.

After choosing a theorem, write its full interior and boundary hypotheses. A circle in space might bound many surfaces, but a chosen spanning surface must lie where the field is smooth. A closed surface may surround a cavity or singularity that changes the admissible volume. A planar region with holes has multiple boundary loops. These geometric details determine whether a derivative integral represents the requested boundary integral or omits a component. A quick sketch of the domain and arrows is often more valuable than immediately differentiating the field.

Orientation has a different concrete meaning in each setting. Green's positive planar boundary keeps the region on the left. Stokes uses a surface normal and its induced edge direction by the right-hand rule. Divergence uses outward normals on every closed-surface component. Reversing any one orientation negates the corresponding signed integral. Scalar area, volume, and mass integrals do not acquire a negative sign merely from reversing a parameter, because their measure factors use magnitudes.

Not every transformed integral is simpler. For a polynomial field on a box, direct face fluxes might be as easy as a triple integral; for a radial field on a sphere, direct dot products may beat divergence calculations. A theorem offers an equality, not a command to use one side. Compute derivative fields and inspect region geometry before deciding. When both sides are tractable, calculating both independently is a strong check of sign, factor, and orientation.

A useful diagnostic table has four entries: boundary quantity, interior derivative, dimension of the interior, and orientation. Planar circulation pairs with scalar curl over area; planar outward flux pairs with planar divergence over area; space-curve circulation pairs with curl flux over a surface; closed-surface outward flux pairs with divergence over volume. The matching dimensions are a clue: a boundary integral in each case becomes an integral over one higher-dimensional interior. This organizing idea generalizes the one-variable fundamental theorem without making the hypotheses disappear.

A theorem checklist should distinguish a field singularity from a coordinate singularity. Spherical coordinates are awkward at a pole, but a smooth Cartesian field such as F=(x,y,z)\mathbf F=(x,y,z) remains defined there, so the divergence theorem on a ball is unaffected. By contrast, F=r/∣r∣3\mathbf F=\mathbf r/\lvert\mathbf r\rvert^3 is genuinely undefined at the origin, and a ball containing it fails the field hypothesis. Choosing a different coordinate chart cannot repair that missing field value. This distinction helps prevent rejecting valid calculations merely because a parameterization has a seam, and prevents accepting invalid calculations merely because a convenient parameter formula hides a physical singularity.

Two boundary theorems checked from both sides

Use the exact ball result from Lesson 4 as the reference for this diagnostic section: for F=(x,y,z)\mathbf F=(x,y,z) on the radius-RR ball centered at the origin, ∇⋅F=3\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3 and the complete outward sphere flux is 4πR34\pi R^3. The direct dot product F⋅n=R\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=R agrees with the volume calculation because sphere area is 4πR24\pi R^2. With this identity already established, the new task is to diagnose a numerical result that disagrees: check whether its panels cover the whole boundary, use outward normals, and carry the area factor. Equality at one sampled radius would not establish the theorem or those implementation conditions.

Orientation is not optional. If the sphere normal points inward, F⋅n\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n becomes −R-R and the surface integral is −4πR3-4\pi R^3. The standard divergence theorem uses the outward normal. A code routine that sums surface panels can silently reverse normals while still drawing a plausible sphere, so an analytic field such as this is a valuable orientation test. The ball and the field satisfy the smoothness assumptions everywhere, including the center.

The disk calculation from Lesson 3 is the orientation reference: G=(−y/2,x/2,0)\mathbf G=(-y/2,x/2,0) has curl k\mathbf k, so an upward radius-RR disk and its counterclockwise edge both give πR2\pi R^2. These two examples use different local quantities. Divergence measures net source strength and pairs with flux through a closed surface. Curl measures local rotation and pairs with circulation along a surface boundary. Green’s theorem is the planar circulation case of this curl principle. A surface can be curved, but the orientation relation remains: its chosen normal determines the positive direction around its boundary by the right-hand rule.

When applying any boundary theorem, write down the region or surface, its boundary, the field’s domain and differentiability, and the orientation before integrating. A singularity inside the region may invalidate a direct application even when the field is smooth along the boundary itself. Checking both sides on a simple case can reveal sign errors, but it does not replace a theorem’s hypotheses.

The sphere example can be scaled without repeating the integral. On radius RR, divergence remains three, ball volume scales as R3R^3, and flux is 4πR34\pi R^3. On the surface, F⋅n=R\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=R and area scales as R2R^2, giving the same cubic scaling. If a numerical surface discretization reports a quadratic dependence on RR, it likely used area but forgot the field’s changing magnitude. Scaling is therefore a practical check as well as a conceptual link between the two sides of the theorem.

Reverse the known disk normal and boundary arrow together: both exact sides become −πR2-\pi R^2. Reversing only the normal while leaving the edge counterclockwise produces opposite signs and is an incompatible Stokes setup. For a code check, record the two orientation choices independently and display whether they match; agreement of unsigned magnitudes hides this error.

Suppose a code lab approximates sphere flux with midpoint panels. Its error can arise from approximating each curved patch by a planar panel, evaluating F\mathbf F at a midpoint, and estimating a normal. Refining the grid may reduce error, but equal-looking values at two resolutions are not a proof of exact equality. Compare against 4πR34\pi R^3, report the radius and panel count, and verify that all panels point outward. A single reversed band can introduce a large error that refinement alone will not repair.

For a horizontal disk, a numerical boundary sum can approximate circulation using short chords. The field G\mathbf G is linear, so the midpoint rule on each straight chord computes the work along that chord exactly. The polygon encloses less area than the true circle; Green’s theorem then says the polygon’s circulation equals its area, not the disk’s πR2\pi R^2. As more sides are added, polygon area approaches circle area. This explains a typical finite-grid discrepancy geometrically rather than treating it as arbitrary floating-point noise.

The two test fields emphasize complementary measurements. F=(x,y,z)\mathbf F=(x,y,z) points outward on a sphere and has positive divergence, so it is natural for closed-surface flux. G=(−y/2,x/2,0)\mathbf G=(-y/2,x/2,0) circulates around the zz-axis and has curl k\mathbf k, so it is natural for a disk boundary. Trying to infer one field’s behavior from the other theorem without matching the quantity would mix source strength with rotation. A clear solution names the field, the region, the requested integral, and the derivative theorem used before presenting a number.

A strong numerical check uses several independent invariants: sign under orientation reversal, expected scaling with radius, and agreement with a simple exact case. For the disk example, doubling radius should multiply both Stokes sides by four, while changing the normal to −k-\mathbf k should negate both. For the sphere example, doubling radius should multiply flux by eight. If a discretization passes a single numerical comparison but fails these transformations, it likely has a structural error such as wrong panel normals, a missing Jacobian, or an incomplete boundary. These checks do not replace theorem hypotheses, but they make implementation mistakes easier to isolate.

Deeper derivation · Boundary theorems convert one integral into another

Green’s theorem relates circulation around a positively oriented, piecewise smooth boundary CC of a suitable planar region DD to the curl inside: ∮C(P dx+Q dy)=∬D(∂Q/∂x−∂P/∂y) dA\oint_C(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint_D(\partial Q/\partial x-\partial P/\partial y)\,dA. For P=−y/2P=-y/2 and Q=x/2Q=x/2, the curl is 11, so counterclockwise circulation around a disk equals its area. The visual investigation changes the radius and shows the quadratic scaling on both sides.

Orientation carries information. Reversing CC reverses the line integral, while the region integral remains positive when curl is 11, so the standard statement must specify counterclockwise outer orientation. If DD has a hole, the inner boundary is oriented clockwise so that the region remains on the left while walking along the total boundary. A field singularity inside a circle prevents direct application of Green’s theorem to the full disk; excluding a small inner disk changes the boundary.

Stokes’s theorem extends the circulation–curl relationship to surfaces in space. The divergence theorem instead relates outward flux through a closed surface to total divergence inside. They are not interchangeable: curl concerns local rotation, divergence local source or sink strength. Before applying one, identify what is being integrated, whether the boundary is closed, the chosen normal or traversal orientation, and where the field is differentiable.

  22. Problem 22. Interpretation Why does the outward normal on an internal cavity wall point into the cavity? Where does it point for a spherical cavity?

    Hint. Define outward relative to the solid material; then consider a spherical cavity separately.

    Solution. At a cavity wall, leaving the solid means entering the empty cavity, so the solid’s outward normal points into the cavity. If the cavity is spherical, this normal points toward that sphere’s center. A general cavity wall need not have normals directed toward one center.

  23. Problem 23. Interpretation Which theorem converts closed-surface outward flux to a volume integral?

    Hint. Match closed-surface outward flux with a volume integral.

    Solution. The divergence theorem states ∬∂VF⋅n dS=∭V∇⋅F dV\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV, with outward orientation and sufficient smoothness on a neighborhood of the solid.

  24. Problem 24. Interpretation Which theorem converts circulation around a space curve to a surface integral?

    Hint. Match boundary circulation with curl flux through a surface.

    Solution. Stokes' theorem states ∮∂SF⋅dr=∬S(∇×F)⋅n dS\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS. The curve orientation follows the surface normal by the right-hand rule, and the field must be smooth near the surface.

  25. Problem 25. Interpretation Does a scalar line integral ∫g ds\int g\,ds become a curl integral by Stokes?

    Hint. Distinguish arc-length measure from oriented displacement.

    Solution. No. Stokes' theorem concerns oriented circulation ∮CF⋅dr\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r. The scalar integral ∫Cg ds\int_Cg\,ds uses nonnegative arc length and is unchanged by reversal, so it has different orientation behavior.

    Hint. Keep the region to your left.

    Solution. Green’s theorem uses counterclockwise orientation on a simple outer boundary, so the enclosed region stays on the left during traversal.

    Hint. Check differentiability throughout the enclosed region.

    Solution. The theorem’s regularity hypotheses do not hold at the singularity. One may need to remove that point and account for an inner boundary.

Concept checks

  1. Which local quantity does Green’s circulation form integrate over area?

    • curl
    • divergence only
    • speed

    Answer. curl. Its integrand is ∂Q/∂x−∂P/∂y\partial Q/\partial x-\partial P/\partial y, the planar curl.

  2. Which theorem relates outward closed-surface flux to volume divergence?

    • Stokes
    • Divergence theorem
    • Mean value theorem

    Answer. Divergence theorem. The divergence theorem integrates ∇⋅F\nabla\cdot\mathbf F over the enclosed volume.

  3. What is positive orientation for a simple planar outer boundary?

    • clockwise
    • counterclockwise
    • either without sign change

    Answer. counterclockwise. Walking counterclockwise keeps the enclosed region on the left.

  4. Can Green’s theorem be applied through a field singularity in the enclosed region?

    • Yes without checks
    • No, its smoothness hypotheses fail
    • Only clockwise

    Answer. No, its smoothness hypotheses fail. A field must satisfy the theorem’s differentiability conditions on a suitable containing region.

  5. Stokes’s theorem relates boundary circulation to…

    • surface curl flux
    • enclosed volume
    • arc length

    Answer. surface curl flux. The surface integral of curl with compatible normal orientation equals boundary circulation.

  6. For F=r/∣r∣3\mathbf F=\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3 on the shell 1≤∣r∣≤21\le|\mathbf r|\le2, what does the complete outward-boundary audit give?

Chapter synthesis

  • Green connects planar circulation to scalar curl; Stokes connects boundary circulation to curl flux through an oriented surface.
  • The divergence theorem connects total outward flux on a closed boundary to integrated divergence in its volume.
  • Surface parametrizations determine both area factors and orientation; theorem hypotheses include the relevant interior, not just the boundary.

Common mistakes to check

  • “Zero divergence means zero flux through every surface.” It guarantees zero total outward flux for a closed surface bounding a region on which the field is sufficiently smooth. An open patch can have nonzero flux. Test with problem 33
  • “The normal can be chosen after choosing the boundary direction.” Stokes’ theorem couples the normal and boundary orientation. Reversing only one orientation changes the sign of its integral; when the common value is nonzero, the two sides no longer agree. Test with problem 27
  • “A field smooth on the boundary is enough.” Green and divergence require smoothness throughout the enclosed region as well; a hidden singularity can invalidate the calculation. Test with problem 17
