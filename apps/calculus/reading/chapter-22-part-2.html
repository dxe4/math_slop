Multivariable optimization · part 2

Written practice and solutions

  1. Problem 1. Fluency Find all stationary points of f=x2+y2−2x−4yf=x^2+y^2-2x-4y.

    Hint. Set both first partial derivatives to zero.

    Solution. The gradient is ∇f=⟨2x−2,2y−4⟩\nabla f=\langle 2x-2,2y-4\rangle. Setting both components to zero gives the unique stationary point (1,2)(1,2).

  2. Problem 2. Interpretation Why does ∇f=0\nabla f=0 miss the minimum of ∣x∣+y2|x|+y^2?

    Hint. Check whether the partial derivative with respect to xx exists at the origin.

    Solution. The function is nonnegative and equals zero at (0,0)(0,0). There ∣x∣|x| has no xx derivative, so the minimum is not a stationary point with ∇f=0\nabla f=0.

  3. Problem 3. Proof Show x2+y2x^2+y^2 has no absolute maximum on R2\mathbb R^2.

    Hint. Evaluate the function along (t,0)(t,0) as ∣t∣|t| grows.

    Solution. On the xx-axis, f(t,0)=t2f(t,0)=t^2. Given any upper bound M>0M>0, choose ∣t∣>M|t|>\sqrt M; then f(t,0)>Mf(t,0)>M. Thus there is no absolute maximum on R2\mathbb R^2.

  4. Problem 4. Proof Minimize x2+y2x^2+y^2 subject to x+y≥1x+y\ge1.

    Hint. Use Cauchy–Schwarz and examine its equality condition.

    Solution. Cauchy–Schwarz gives (x+y)2≤2(x2+y2)(x+y)^2\le 2(x^2+y^2). Feasibility gives x+y≥1x+y\ge1, so x2+y2≥(x+y)2/2≥1/2x^2+y^2\ge(x+y)^2/2\ge1/2. Equality requires x=yx=y and x+y=1x+y=1, hence the unique minimum is 1/21/2 at (1/2,1/2)(1/2,1/2).

  5. Problem 5. Fluency Classify the critical point of f=3x2+2xy+2y2f=3x^2+2xy+2y^2.

    Hint. Solve the gradient equations, then use the Hessian determinant.

    Solution. The equations 6x+2y=06x+2y=0 and 2x+4y=02x+4y=0 give only (0,0)(0,0). Here D=6⋅4−22=20>0D=6\cdot4-2^2=20>0 and fxx=6>0f_{xx}=6>0, so it is a strict local minimum. Since f=3(x+y/3)2+(5/3)y2≥0f=3(x+y/3)^2+(5/3)y^2\ge0, it is also the unique absolute minimum.

  6. Problem 6. Proof Classify the origin for f=x4−y4f=x^4-y^4 and h=x4+y4h=x^4+y^4 despite their identical Hessians there.

    Hint. Compare values on the axes, then use nonnegativity.

    Solution. Both gradients and Hessians vanish at the origin. For ff, values f(t,0)=t4>0f(t,0)=t^4>0 and f(0,t)=−t4<0f(0,t)=-t^4<0 show a saddle. For hh, h=x4+y4≥0h=x^4+y^4\ge0, with equality only at the origin, so the origin is a strict absolute minimum.

  7. Problem 7. Fluency Classify the origin for f(x,y)=x2+2xy+2y2f(x,y)=x^2+2xy+2y^2 using the Hessian test.

    Hint. Compute A=fxxA=f_{xx}, B=fxyB=f_{xy}, C=fyyC=f_{yy}, and D=AC−B2D=AC-B^2.

    Solution. The origin is stationary. Here A=2A=2, B=2B=2, C=4C=4, so D=2⋅4−22=4>0D=2\cdot4-2^2=4>0 and A>0A>0. The Hessian test gives a strict local minimum; f=(x+y)2+y2≥0f=(x+y)^2+y^2\ge0 proves it is the unique absolute minimum.

  8. Problem 8. Fluency Classify the origin for f=2x2+2xy+3y2f=2x^2+2xy+3y^2.

    Hint. Compute AA, BB, CC, then the sign of AC−B2AC-B^2.

    Solution. The origin is stationary and A=4A=4, B=2B=2, C=6C=6, giving D=4⋅6−22=20>0D=4\cdot6-2^2=20>0. Since A>0A>0, the Hessian test gives a strict local minimum.

  9. Problem 9. Interpretation Does positive-definite Hessian at a nonstationary point prove a local minimum?

    Hint. Check the gradient of f=x+x2+y2f=x+x^2+y^2 at the origin.

    Solution. No. This Hessian is positive definite, but ∇f(0,0)=⟨1,0⟩\nabla f(0,0)=\langle1,0\rangle. For small negative xx, f(x,0)=x+x2<0=f(0,0)f(x,0)=x+x^2<0=f(0,0), so the origin is not a local minimum.

  10. Problem 10. Proof Classify zero for x2−y4x^2-y^4 although the Hessian determinant is zero.

    Hint. Compare values on the coordinate axes.

    Solution. At (t,0)(t,0) the value is t2>0t^2>0, while at (0,t)(0,t) it is −t4<0-t^4<0 for t≠0t\ne0. Both occur arbitrarily near zero, so the origin is a saddle despite the inconclusive Hessian determinant.

  11. Problem 11. Proof Classify zero for x2+y4x^2+y^4.

    Hint. Use the fact that both terms are nonnegative.

    Solution. For every (x,y)(x,y), x2+y4≥0=f(0,0)x^2+y^4\ge0=f(0,0). Equality requires x=y=0x=y=0, so the origin is the unique strict global minimum even though its Hessian test is inconclusive.

  12. Problem 12. Fluency Find the absolute extrema of f=x+2yf=x+2y on the closed disk x2+y2≤1x^2+y^2\le1.

    Hint. Use Cauchy–Schwarz to bound the linear objective on the disk.

    Solution. There is no interior stationary point because ∇f=⟨1,2⟩\nabla f=\langle1,2\rangle. Cauchy–Schwarz gives ∣x+2y∣≤5x2+y2≤5|x+2y|\le\sqrt5\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\le\sqrt5. Equality occurs at (1/5,2/5)(1/\sqrt5,2/\sqrt5) for the maximum 5\sqrt5 and at its negative for the minimum −5-\sqrt5.

  13. Problem 13. Fluency Find extrema of x+yx+y on [0,1]2[0,1]^2.

    Hint. Check the allowable ranges of both coordinates.

    Solution. On [0,1]2[0,1]^2, each coordinate lies between zero and one. Hence 0≤x+y≤20\le x+y\le2, with minimum 00 at (0,0)(0,0) and maximum 22 at (1,1)(1,1).

  14. Problem 14. Interpretation Does noncompactness always prevent a global minimum?

    Hint. Consider a nonnegative sum of squares on R2\mathbb R^2.

    Solution. No. A continuous real-valued function on a nonempty compact set attains both absolute extrema, but compactness is not necessary for a minimum. On noncompact R2\mathbb R^2, f=x2+y2≥0f=x^2+y^2\ge0 attains its minimum 00 at the origin.

  15. Problem 15. Fluency Find extrema of x2+y2x^2+y^2 on the closed unit disk.

    Hint. Use 0≤x2+y2≤10\le x^2+y^2\le1 throughout the disk.

    Solution. The objective x2+y2x^2+y^2 is nonnegative, with minimum 00 only at the origin. Its maximum is 11 at every boundary point satisfying x2+y2=1x^2+y^2=1.

  16. Problem 16. Interpretation Why is a corner checked separately from smooth-edge multiplier points?

    Hint. Think about the tangent to a square boundary at a corner.

    Solution. At a square corner the boundary has two incident edge directions and no single smooth tangent, so a regular single-edge multiplier equation need not capture the corner. Check each smooth boundary piece, any singular boundary points, and the corners before completing the boundary search.

  17. Problem 17. Interpretation Why do the ordinary multiplier equations fail for minimizing f=yf=y subject to x2+y2=0x^2+y^2=0, and what are the extrema?

    Hint. Identify the feasible set before applying a multiplier equation.

    Solution. The equation x2+y2=0x^2+y^2=0 allows only (0,0)(0,0), where f=0f=0 is both constrained minimum and maximum. But ∇g(0,0)=⟨0,0⟩\nabla g(0,0)=\langle0,0\rangle and ∇f(0,0)=⟨0,1⟩\nabla f(0,0)=\langle0,1\rangle, so ∇f=λ∇g\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g cannot hold. The regularity hypothesis fails.

  18. Problem 18. Fluency Maximize x+yx+y on the ellipse x2+4y2=4x^2+4y^2=4.

    Hint. Scale the ellipse to a unit circle, then apply Cauchy–Schwarz.

    Solution. Let u=x/2u=x/2 and v=yv=y, so u2+v2=1u^2+v^2=1 and x+y=2u+v≤5x+y=2u+v\le\sqrt5. Equality holds at (u,v)=(2/5,1/5)(u,v)=(2/\sqrt5,1/\sqrt5), or (x,y)=(4/5,1/5)(x,y)=(4/\sqrt5,1/\sqrt5). Thus the maximum is 5\sqrt5; the opposite point gives minimum −5-\sqrt5.

  19. Problem 19. Fluency Solve the multiplier candidates for x+yx+y on x2+4y2=4x^2+4y^2=4.

    Hint. Set ⟨1,1⟩=λ⟨2x,8y⟩\langle1,1\rangle=\lambda\langle2x,8y\rangle and use the constraint.

    Solution. The equations give x=1/(2λ)x=1/(2\lambda) and y=1/(8λ)y=1/(8\lambda). Substitution into x2+4y2=4x^2+4y^2=4 yields candidates ±(4/5,1/5)\pm(4/\sqrt5,1/\sqrt5), with objective values ±5\pm\sqrt5.

  20. Problem 20. Proof Why can f=xf=x constrained by x2+y2=0x^2+y^2=0 have an extremum without a multiplier?

    Hint. Inspect the feasible set and the constraint gradient.

    Solution. The constraint g=x2+y2=0g=x^2+y^2=0 leaves only (0,0)(0,0), so f=xf=x has both constrained extrema there. At that point ∇f=(1,0)\nabla f=(1,0) while ∇g=(0,0)\nabla g=(0,0), so ∇f=λ∇g\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g has no solution. The multiplier theorem does not apply because its regularity hypothesis fails.

  21. Problem 21. Interpretation If a constraint equation g=cg=c is replaced by 2g=2c2g=2c, what happens to λ\lambda?

    Hint. Compare the old and new constraint gradients.

    Solution. Since ∇(2g)=2∇g\nabla(2g)=2\nabla g, an equation ∇f=λ∇g\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g becomes ∇f=(λ/2)∇(2g)\nabla f=(\lambda/2)\nabla(2g). The multiplier halves; the feasible set and optimizer do not change.

  22. Problem 22. Interpretation Does every solution of multiplier equations give a constrained maximum?

    Hint. Recall whether multiplier equations are necessary or sufficient.

    Solution. No. At a regular constrained local extremum, the multiplier equations are necessary, not sufficient. A solution may be a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither along the feasible set. A feasible local test can classify local behavior; an absolute maximum requires a global bound or comparison of all relevant candidates after establishing that a maximum is attained.

  23. Problem 23. Fluency For f=(x2+10y2)/2f=(x^2+10y^2)/2 starting at (2,1)(2,1), analyze gradient descent with α=0.2\alpha=0.2. Give ff after kk steps.

    Hint. Apply the coordinate update factors 1−α1-\alpha and 1−10α1-10\alpha.

    Solution. The recurrence gives xk=2(0.8)kx_k=2(0.8)^k and yk=(−1)ky_k=(-1)^k. Thus f(xk,yk)=2(0.64)k+5→5f(x_k,y_k)=2(0.64)^k+5\to5, although the minimum is zero. The yy coordinate oscillates because its update factor is −1-1; the chosen step is the stability endpoint.

  24. Problem 24. Fluency For f=(x2+10y2)/2f=(x^2+10y^2)/2, what happens with α=0.2\alpha=0.2 and y0≠0y_0\ne0?

    Hint. Compute the update factor for yy.

    Solution. Gradient descent gives yk+1=(1−10α)yk=−yky_{k+1}=(1-10\alpha)y_k=-y_k at α=0.2\alpha=0.2. Thus yk=(−1)ky0y_k=(-1)^k y_0 oscillates without shrinking when y0≠0y_0\ne0.

  25. Problem 25. Fluency Find the interval of positive fixed step sizes for which gradient descent on (x2+4y2)/2(x^2+4y^2)/2 converges to its minimum from every starting point.

    Hint. Require both coordinate update factors to have magnitude below one.

    Solution. The updates are xk+1=(1−α)xkx_{k+1}=(1-\alpha)x_k and yk+1=(1−4α)yky_{k+1}=(1-4\alpha)y_k. Convergence from every starting point requires ∣1−α∣<1|1-\alpha|<1 and ∣1−4α∣<1|1-4\alpha|<1. Their intersection is 0<α<1/20<\alpha<1/2; at α=1/2\alpha=1/2, a nonzero yy-component alternates without shrinking.

  26. Problem 26. Interpretation For f(x,y)=x2−y2f(x,y)=x^2-y^2, calculate the gradient at the origin. Does its zero value certify a local or absolute minimum? Check nearby points on both coordinate axes.

    Hint. Consider a stationary saddle.

    Solution. The gradient is ∇f=(2x,−2y)\nabla f=(2x,-2y), so ∇f(0,0)=(0,0)\nabla f(0,0)=(0,0). Yet f(t,0)=t2>0f(t,0)=t^2>0 and f(0,t)=−t2<0f(0,t)=-t^2<0 for every nonzero tt. The origin is a saddle, neither a local nor an absolute minimum. A zero or tiny gradient alone cannot certify optimality.

  27. Problem 27. Proof Show that f(x,y)=x+yf(x,y)=x+y has no maximum on the open unit disk, although it has supremum 2\sqrt2.

    Hint. Approach the maximizing boundary point from inside.

    Solution. For n≥2n\ge2, let (xn,yn)=((1−1/n)/2,(1−1/n)/2)(x_n,y_n)=((1-1/n)/\sqrt2,(1-1/n)/\sqrt2). Its radius is 1−1/n<11-1/n<1, while xn+yn=2(1−1/n)→2x_n+y_n=\sqrt2(1-1/n)\to\sqrt2. Cauchy–Schwarz gives x+y<2x+y<\sqrt2 everywhere inside, so no maximum is attained.

  28. Problem 28. Proof Prove x+y≤2x+y\le\sqrt2 on the closed unit disk and find equality.

    Hint. Apply Cauchy–Schwarz and check equality conditions.

    Solution. Write x+y=⟨x,y⟩⋅⟨1,1⟩≤x2+y22≤2x+y=\langle x,y\rangle\cdot\langle1,1\rangle\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt2\le\sqrt2. Equality requires the unit boundary point (1/2,1/2)(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2), where the maximum is attained.

  29. Problem 29. Interpretation For f(x,y)=x+yf(x,y)=x+y on the open unit disk x2+y2<1x^2+y^2<1, explain the difference between its maximum and supremum.

    Hint. Approach the missing boundary point in the direction (1,1)(1,1).

    Solution. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds x+y<2x+y<\sqrt2 in the open disk, but interior points can approach (1/2,1/2)(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2) and make x+y→2x+y\to\sqrt2. Thus 2\sqrt2 is the supremum, with no maximum.

  30. Problem 30. Fluency For the closed disk x2+y2≤R2x^2+y^2\le R^2, where R≥0R\ge0, find the extrema of x+yx+y.

    Hint. Scale the unit-disk bound by RR.

    Solution. On the radius-RR disk, Cauchy–Schwarz gives ∣x+y∣≤2R|x+y|\le\sqrt2R. The maximum is 2R\sqrt2R at (R/2,R/2)(R/\sqrt2,R/\sqrt2) and the minimum is −2R-\sqrt2R at the opposite point, assuming R≥0R\ge0.

  31. Problem 31. Interpretation Does a zero Hessian at a critical point imply no local extremum?

    Hint. Consider x4+y4x^4+y^4.

    Solution. No. x4+y4x^4+y^4 has a strict local minimum at the origin, where its Hessian is zero.

  32. Problem 32. Interpretation Why must the boundary be examined on a compact region?

    Hint. Could a global maximum occur there?

    Solution. Yes. Interior stationarity only covers interior candidates; a continuous function can attain its global extreme on the boundary.

  33. Problem 33. Challenge On D={0≤x≤1, y2≤x3}D=\{0\le x\le1,\ y^2\le x^3\}, find absolute extrema of f=x+yf=x+y, explicitly checking interior, curved edges, vertical edge, corners, and cusp.

    Hint. On curved edges use y=±x3/2y=\pm x^{3/2}; the cusp is singular for the boundary constraint.

    Solution. There is no interior stationary point because ∇f=(1,1)\nabla f=(1,1). Upper edge x+x3/2x+x^{3/2} rises to 22 at (1,1)(1,1). Lower edge x−x3/2x-x^{3/2} has derivative 1−3x/21-3\sqrt x/2, with stationary value 4/274/27 at x=4/9x=4/9; its endpoints are zero. On the vertical edge x=1x=1, f=1+yf=1+y ranges from 00 to 22. The cusp (0,0)(0,0) also gives zero, despite singular constraint gradient there. Thus minimum 00 occurs at (0,0)(0,0) and (1,−1)(1,-1), and maximum 22 at (1,1)(1,1).

  34. Problem 34. Challenge For F(x,y)=(x2+4y2)/2F(x,y)=(x^2+4y^2)/2, start fixed-step gradient descent at (1,1)(1,1). Derive the convergent step-size interval and compute two states for α=1/4,1/2,3/4\alpha=1/4,1/2,3/4.

    Hint. Each update multiplies x by 1−α1-\alpha and y by 1−4α1-4\alpha.

    Solution. The recurrence is (xk+1,yk+1)=((1−α)xk,(1−4α)yk)(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})=((1-\alpha)x_k,(1-4\alpha)y_k). Both multipliers must have magnitude below one, yielding 0<α<1/20<\alpha<1/2. At 1/41/4, states are (3/4,0),(9/16,0)(3/4,0),(9/16,0); at 1/21/2, (1/2,−1),(1/4,1)(1/2,-1),(1/4,1); at 3/43/4, (1/4,−2),(1/16,4)(1/4,-2),(1/16,4). The latter two fail to converge because the y multiplier has magnitude at least one.

Concept checks

  1. At a critical point, a Hessian with one positive and one negative eigenvalue indicates…

    • local minimum
    • saddle
    • local maximum

    Answer. saddle. Second-order changes have opposite signs in different directions.

  2. Does a zero Hessian determinant settle the classification?

    • Yes
    • No
    • Only for quadratics

    Answer. No. It is inconclusive; higher-order terms or direct inequalities may decide.

  3. Where may a compact-domain maximum occur?

    • Only inside
    • Inside or on the boundary
    • Nowhere

    Answer. Inside or on the boundary. Continuity on a compact region guarantees attainment, but the boundary remains a candidate.

  4. Does a Lagrange multiplier equation guarantee a maximum?

    • Yes
    • No, it finds candidates under hypotheses
    • Only for circles

    Answer. No, it finds candidates under hypotheses. Candidates must be checked and regularity of the constraint matters.

  5. What happens for descent on x2+4y2x^2+4y^2 with Euler-like step h>1/4h>1/4?

    • The yy component can grow
    • Convergence is guaranteed
    • The gradient vanishes

    Answer. The yy component can grow. The yy multiplier 1−8h1-8h has magnitude greater than 11 when h>1/4h>1/4.

Chapter synthesis

  • Interior extrema require critical-point checks, and second derivatives classify only when their quadratic information is decisive.
  • Compact-domain optimization combines interior analysis with every boundary piece, endpoint, corner, and singular point.
  • Lagrange multipliers express vanishing tangential variation at regular constrained extrema and supply candidates rather than automatic classifications.
  • Gradient descent needs controlled step sizes; exact quadratic recurrences explain slow convergence, oscillation, and divergence.

Common mistakes to check

  • “Zero gradient proves a minimum.” The origin is stationary for x2−y2x^2-y^2, but values have both signs arbitrarily close to it. Test with problem 26
  • “A zero Hessian determinant means a saddle.” The function x4+y4x^4+y^4 has determinant zero at its strict minimum; the test is inconclusive. Test with problem 6
  • “Multipliers find every constrained extremum.” Their usual theorem requires regular constraints; singular feasible points and endpoints need separate checks. Test with problem 17
