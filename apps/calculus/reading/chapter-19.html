Vectors describe displacement

This chapter opens the L2–L3 Multivariable and Vector Calculus book. Its route keeps chapters 19–26 in their original order, then adds differential forms in chapter 29 and implicit functions in chapter 30. Study the linked single-variable preparation before beginning; the new dimensions reuse those limit and accumulation ideas.

A point records a location; a vector records a displacement. If P=(1,2,3)P=(1,2,3) and Q=(4,0,5)Q=(4,0,5), the displacement from PP to QQ is Q−P=⟨3,−2,2⟩Q-P=\langle3,-2,2\rangle. The same vector can be drawn starting at any point. This freedom matters in mechanics: moving an arrow on a sketch does not change its length or direction. Position vectors depend on a chosen origin, while the displacement between two fixed points does not.

Vectors in two dimensions have two components and vectors in three dimensions have three. Add vectors component by component, corresponding to following one displacement with another. Multiplication by a positive scalar changes length, multiplication by a negative scalar also reverses direction, and multiplication by zero produces the zero vector. Standard basis vectors i\mathbf i, j\mathbf j, k\mathbf k point along the positive coordinate axes, so ⟨a,b,c⟩=ai+bj+ck\langle a,b,c\rangle=a\mathbf i+b\mathbf j+c\mathbf k.

The Euclidean norm ∣u∣=u12+u22+u32|\mathbf u|=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2} is a length, justified by two applications of the Pythagorean theorem. For nonzero u\mathbf u, the unit vector u/∣u∣\mathbf u/|\mathbf u| preserves direction and has length one. The zero vector has no direction and cannot be normalized. A displacement whose components are measured in metres has a norm in metres; its associated unit vector is dimensionless. These formulas presume perpendicular axes using the same length unit.

A vector-valued function r(t)=⟨x(t),y(t),z(t)⟩\mathbf r(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle assigns a vector to each parameter value in a specified interval. When it describes position, its three components must exist at the same parameter value: its domain is their common domain. Its trace is a geometric curve, but the function also records order and timing. Two functions can trace the same curve at different speeds or in opposite directions. This extends the parametric viewpoint of chapter 18 without changing the underlying idea.

Vector addition encodes a path-independent net displacement. If a traveler moves 3 m3\,\mathrm m east and then 4 m4\,\mathrm m north, the resulting displacement vector is ⟨3,4⟩ m\langle3,4\rangle\,\mathrm m with length 5 m5\,\mathrm m, while total distance walked is 7 m7\,\mathrm m. Reversing the two straight moves changes the route but not the displacement. In three dimensions the same componentwise rule applies. This distinction returns in vector-valued motion: r(b)−r(a)\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a) depends only on endpoints, while ∫ab∣r′(t)∣ dt\int_a^b|\mathbf r^{\prime}(t)|\,dt measures the actual route and can be much larger.

The triangle inequality ∣u+v∣≤∣u∣+∣v∣|\mathbf u+\mathbf v|\le|\mathbf u|+|\mathbf v| expresses that a direct displacement is no longer than following two segments. It can be proved by squaring and using u⋅v≤∣u∣∣v∣\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\le|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|, a result justified by the dot-product angle formula or Cauchy–Schwarz. Equality holds when nonzero u\mathbf u and v\mathbf v point in the same direction. This inequality gives an immediate check on a computed resultant vector. It also explains why polygonal approximations to a curved path can underestimate its length: a chord is no longer than the broken route it replaces. The inequality is proved in the next lesson; this use is an optional forward link if reading in order.

A coordinate system should use compatible units on its axes. If horizontal coordinates are metres and vertical coordinates are feet, the raw norm x2+y2\sqrt{x^2+y^2} mixes numbers with different scales and is not a physical distance until units are converted. Likewise a velocity vector can mix components only when they refer to the same time scale. The algebraic formulas assume an orthonormal basis; in oblique or curved coordinates the metric changes. For this introductory chapter, use perpendicular Cartesian axes and state units before interpreting a norm physically.

The zero vector is a special case in many geometric formulas. Its norm is zero, but it cannot define a direction, unit vector, angle, projection axis, or unique normal. An object at rest has zero velocity vector; it may nevertheless have nonzero acceleration and begin moving immediately. Software that normalizes a vector should check the norm before division. Mathematically, one can often handle a zero vector by returning to component equations rather than forcing a direction that is not defined.

Vector equations can be checked componentwise, but their geometric meaning can simplify reasoning. If u+v=w\mathbf u+\mathbf v=\mathbf w, then v=w−u\mathbf v=\mathbf w-\mathbf u is the displacement needed after u\mathbf u to reach w\mathbf w. If u\mathbf u and v\mathbf v are perpendicular, ∣u+v∣2=∣u∣2+∣v∣2|\mathbf u+\mathbf v|^2=|\mathbf u|^2+|\mathbf v|^2; if they point in opposite directions, their lengths can cancel. The component calculation and the geometric picture should agree. In a physical trajectory, position vectors depend on the chosen origin, yet velocity and acceleration under a fixed translated origin do not, because differentiating removes that constant translation. This is another reason to distinguish locations from displacements.

Dot and cross products measure different geometry

The dot product u⋅v=u1v1+u2v2+u3v3u\cdot v=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3 produces a scalar. For nonzero vectors it also equals ∣u∣∣v∣cos⁡θ|u||v|\cos\theta, where 0≤θ≤π0\le\theta\le\pi is their angle. One justification expands ∣u−v∣2|u-v|^2 in coordinates and compares it with the cosine rule for the triangle formed by the vectors. Thus a positive dot product indicates an acute angle, a negative one an obtuse angle, and zero indicates perpendicular directions. The zero vector has zero dot product with every vector, but no defined angle.

Projection turns this angle information into a displacement. For v≠0\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0, the projection of u\mathbf u onto the line directed by v\mathbf v is proj⁡vu=u⋅vv⋅vv\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\mathbf v. Subtracting this projection from u\mathbf u gives a remainder perpendicular to v\mathbf v; taking its dot product with v\mathbf v proves the claim directly. The signed scalar component along v\mathbf v is (u⋅v)/∣v∣(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)/|\mathbf v|, which can be negative. Distinguish this signed quantity from the nonnegative length of the vector projection.

In three dimensions, the cross product produces a vector perpendicular to both inputs. Its coordinate formula is u×v=⟨u2v3−u3v2,u3v1−u1v3,u1v2−u2v1⟩\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\langle u_2v_3-u_3v_2,u_3v_1-u_1v_3,u_1v_2-u_2v_1\rangle. Its direction follows the right-hand rule in a right-handed coordinate system. Interchanging the inputs reverses it: v×u=−u×v\mathbf v\times\mathbf u=-\mathbf u\times\mathbf v. Its magnitude ∣u∣∣v∣sin⁡θ|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\sin\theta equals the area of their parallelogram, so the triangle they span has half this area.

The cross product vanishes when the vectors are parallel or one is zero. In that case it cannot supply a normal direction. For two planar vectors, embedding them as ⟨u1,u2,0⟩\langle u_1,u_2,0\rangle and ⟨v1,v2,0⟩\langle v_1,v_2,0\rangle gives cross product ⟨0,0,u1v2−u2v1⟩\langle0,0,u_1v_2-u_2v_1\rangle; the last component is signed area. A dot product of force in newtons with displacement in metres has units of joules. A cross product of two length vectors has units of square metres, even though its direction lives in space.

The dot product measures alignment. For u=⟨3,4,0⟩\mathbf u=\langle3,4,0\rangle and v=⟨1,0,1⟩\mathbf v=\langle1,0,1\rangle, u⋅v=3\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=3, ∣v∣2=2|\mathbf v|^2=2, so proj⁡vu=32⟨1,0,1⟩=⟨32,0,32⟩\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u=\frac32\langle1,0,1\rangle=\langle\frac32,0,\frac32\rangle. The remainder is ⟨32,4,−32⟩\langle\frac32,4,-\frac32\rangle, whose dot product with v\mathbf v is zero. This check verifies the projection algebra without a picture. If v\mathbf v were zero, division by ∣v∣2|\mathbf v|^2 would fail because there is no line direction onto which to project.

The cross product's magnitude is the area of a parallelogram, so the triangle with sides u\mathbf u and v\mathbf v from a common point has area ∣u×v∣/2|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|/2. For u=⟨1,2,0⟩\mathbf u=\langle1,2,0\rangle and v=⟨0,1,2⟩\mathbf v=\langle0,1,2\rangle, u×v=⟨4,−2,1⟩\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\langle4,-2,1\rangle and its norm is 21\sqrt{21}, giving triangle area 21/2\sqrt{21}/2. Swapping u\mathbf u and v\mathbf v reverses the normal but leaves area unchanged. This distinction between oriented normal and nonnegative area will matter for surface integrals in chapter 26.

The dot and cross products answer different questions about the same pair of vectors. For unit vectors meeting at angle θ\theta, the dot product is cos⁡θ\cos\theta and cross-product magnitude is sin⁡θ\sin\theta. At θ=0\theta=0 the dot product is one and cross magnitude zero; at θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2 they are zero and one respectively. The first measures the parallel component, the second the perpendicular span. Neither product is a generic substitute for the other. In physics, work uses force dotted with displacement, while torque uses a position vector crossed with force.

Cauchy–Schwarz ∣u⋅v∣≤∣u∣∣v∣|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\le|\mathbf u||\mathbf v| can be proved by considering ∣u−tv∣2≥0|\mathbf u-t\mathbf v|^2\ge0 for all real tt. When v≠0\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0, choose t=(u⋅v)/∣v∣2t=(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)/|\mathbf v|^2 to minimize the quadratic; the remaining nonnegative value yields the inequality. For v≠0\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0, equality occurs exactly when u\mathbf u is a scalar multiple of v\mathbf v; when v=0\mathbf v=\mathbf0, equality holds for every u\mathbf u. This theorem ensures the angle formula has a cosine value between −1-1 and 11 for nonzero vectors, and it underlies the triangle inequality. The proof illustrates how projection and norms fit together algebraically.

The right-hand rule depends on orientation of the coordinate system. In a standard right-handed Cartesian frame, i×j=k\mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k and j×i=−k\mathbf j\times\mathbf i=-\mathbf k. A reflected coordinate frame would reverse the conventional cross-product direction, though the magnitude of spanned area remains the same. When an application uses torque or oriented flux, state the chosen axes and orientation. For an ordinary nonnegative triangle area, only ∣u×v∣|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v| matters. This distinction prepares the reader for oriented surfaces, where a normal direction is a deliberate choice rather than an automatic property of a patch.

Lines, planes, and intersection checks

A line through PP with nonzero direction dd consists of r(λ)=P+λdr(\lambda)=P+\lambda d for all real λ\lambda. Restricting λ\lambda to [0,1][0,1] with d=Q−Pd=Q-P describes the segment from PP to QQ, including its endpoints. Parametric equations remain valid when a component of dd is zero, whereas dividing to form symmetric equations then fails. A zero direction describes a point, not a line.

A plane through PP with nonzero normal n\mathbf n consists of points r\mathbf r satisfying n⋅(r−P)=0\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-P)=0. Every displacement within the plane must be perpendicular to the normal. Equivalently, if nonparallel vectors u\mathbf u and v\mathbf v lie in the plane, write r=P+su+tv\mathbf r=P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v and choose n=u×v\mathbf n=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v. Three noncollinear points determine such a plane: take two differences from one point. Three collinear points do not determine a unique plane because their cross product is zero.

To intersect a line P+λdP+\lambda\mathbf d with a plane n⋅r=c\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r=c, substitute and solve λ(n⋅d)=c−n⋅P\lambda(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d)=c-\mathbf n\cdot P. If n⋅d≠0\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d\ne0, there is exactly one intersection on the full line. If n⋅d=0\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d=0, the line is parallel to the plane: it is entirely contained when n⋅P=c\mathbf n\cdot P=c and otherwise has no intersection. For a segment or ray, a computed parameter must also satisfy the parameter bounds.

The distance from QQ to the plane through PP is ∣n⋅(Q−P)∣/∣n∣|\mathbf n\cdot(Q-P)|/|\mathbf n|. This is the absolute value of the scalar projection onto the normal, so it measures perpendicular separation. In three dimensions, two nonparallel lines need not meet: they can be skew. Solving all three coordinate equations with two independent parameters checks an intersection; matching only the first two coordinates can create a false crossing in a planar picture.

Three noncollinear points determine a plane because two independent displacement vectors span it. For P=(1,0,0)P=(1,0,0), Q=(0,1,0)Q=(0,1,0), R=(0,0,1)R=(0,0,1), take u=Q−P=⟨−1,1,0⟩\mathbf u=Q-P=\langle-1,1,0\rangle and v=R−P=⟨−1,0,1⟩\mathbf v=R-P=\langle-1,0,1\rangle. Their cross product is ⟨1,1,1⟩\langle1,1,1\rangle, giving plane x+y+z=1x+y+z=1. Multiplying the normal and equation by any nonzero scalar gives the same plane. If u×v=0\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf0, the points are collinear or repeated, and this construction cannot pick a unique plane.

Intersecting a line with a plane is one scalar equation in the line parameter. For r(λ)=P+λd\mathbf r(\lambda)=P+\lambda\mathbf d and n⋅r=c\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r=c, solve λ=(c−n⋅P)/(n⋅d)\lambda=(c-\mathbf n\cdot P)/(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d) when n⋅d≠0\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d\ne0. With plane x+y+z=1x+y+z=1 and line λ⟨1,2,3⟩\lambda\langle1,2,3\rangle, λ=1/6\lambda=1/6 and intersection is (1/6,1/3,1/2)(1/6,1/3,1/2). Checking the coordinates gives sum one. If the line is restricted to a segment with λ∈[0,1]\lambda\in[0,1], the same solution is valid; a solution outside those bounds would lie only on its extension.

When n⋅d=0\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d=0, do not divide. The line direction lies parallel to the plane, and the remaining condition n⋅P=c\mathbf n\cdot P=c decides whether every line point is in the plane or none is. For plane x+y=1x+y=1, a line through (0,0)(0,0) with direction (1,−1)(1,-1) misses it, while a line through (1,0)(1,0) with the same direction lies in it. These are different geometric outcomes of the same zero denominator. The visual investigation makes the distinction visible in a two-dimensional cross-section.

Skew lines are a specifically spatial possibility. The xx-axis line (s,0,0)(s,0,0) and the parallel-to-yy line (0,t,1)(0,t,1) appear to cross at the origin in an xx–yy projection, but their zz-coordinates differ by one. Solving (s,0,0)=(0,t,1)(s,0,0)=(0,t,1) gives an impossible equation 0=10=1. Thus they are neither parallel nor intersecting. When checking two 3D paths for collision, all three coordinate equations must hold at the same time; a planar plot or two-coordinate match is insufficient.

The plane distance formula is a projection theorem. Let n\mathbf n be a nonzero normal and QQ any point. The signed scalar component of Q−PQ-P along the unit normal n/∣n∣\mathbf n/|\mathbf n| is n⋅(Q−P)/∣n∣\mathbf n\cdot(Q-P)/|\mathbf n|; its absolute value is the shortest distance to the plane through PP. Moving QQ within a direction parallel to the plane leaves that value unchanged. Multiplying the plane equation and normal by −1-1 reverses the signed component but not the distance. If a computed distance is negative, the absolute value or interpretation has been mishandled.

In three dimensions, two lines can be parallel, intersect, or skew. To test intersection of P+suP+s\mathbf u and Q+tvQ+t\mathbf v, solve three scalar coordinate equations simultaneously for the two unknown parameters ss and tt. An overdetermined system may be inconsistent even if two coordinate equations agree. If both lines are restricted to segments, each successful parameter must additionally lie in its segment interval. A common computational mistake solves for ss and tt from the xx and yy equations and forgets to substitute into zz. The resulting plotted crossing can be an artifact of projection, as the skew-line example shows.

Velocity and acceleration are vector derivatives

A vector limit exists exactly when each component limit exists. Accordingly, differentiate a vector function component by component: v(t)=r′(t)v(t)=r^{\prime}(t) and a(t)=r′′(t)a(t)=r^{\prime\prime}(t). When v(t)≠0v(t)\ne\mathbf 0, its direction is the instantaneous direction of travel; velocity also gives the rate of displacement. At zero velocity it supplies no direction, although acceleration can exist. Speed is the scalar ∣v(t)∣|v(t)|. If position is measured in metres and time in seconds, velocity components and speed have units m/s, while acceleration components have units m/s².

The distinction between acceleration and increasing speed is essential. On a circle, velocity can rotate while its magnitude stays fixed, producing nonzero acceleration. Conversely, a negative acceleration component along one coordinate axis need not mean that the object slows down. The relevant comparison is between acceleration and velocity, not between acceleration and an arbitrary positive coordinate direction.

For continuously differentiable r\mathbf r on [a,b][a,b], the distance traveled is ∫ab∣r′(t)∣ dt\int_a^b|\mathbf r^{\prime}(t)|\,dt, while displacement is r(b)−r(a)\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a). Componentwise integration gives that displacement as the integral of velocity. Endpoint derivatives can be interpreted one-sided when the motion is only defined on a closed interval. Corners or jumps in the prescribed path may prevent a velocity from existing; a plot joining samples does not repair that defect.

Where speed is nonzero, differentiate v⋅v=∣v∣2\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf v|^2 to obtain ddt∣v∣=v⋅a∣v∣\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf v|=\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a}{|\mathbf v|}. Thus v⋅a>0\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a>0 means locally increasing speed, while v⋅a<0\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a<0 means decreasing speed. At v=0\mathbf v=\mathbf0 the quotient is undefined, even if the vector acceleration exists. For r(t)=⟨t2,0,0⟩\mathbf r(t)=\langle t^2,0,0\rangle, speed is 2∣t∣2|t| and has a corner at t=0t=0 despite the smooth position function.

The vector derivative is a limit of displacement vectors divided by elapsed time. Componentwise differentiation is valid because a vector limit exists exactly when every component limit exists. For r(t)=⟨t,t2,0⟩\mathbf r(t)=\langle t,t^2,0\rangle, velocity is ⟨1,2t,0⟩\langle1,2t,0\rangle and speed is 1+4t2\sqrt{1+4t^2}. At t=0t=0 the velocity points along the positive xx-axis with speed one, while acceleration is ⟨0,2,0⟩\langle0,2,0\rangle and is perpendicular to velocity. The object is turning without instantaneously changing speed at that moment.

For ω≠0\omega\ne0, a complete lap of r(t)=⟨Rcos⁡ωt,Rsin⁡ωt,0⟩\mathbf r(t)=\langle R\cos\omega t,R\sin\omega t,0\rangle returns to its starting point after time 2π/∣ω∣2\pi/|\omega|, so displacement is zero. Velocity magnitude is R∣ω∣R|\omega|, and traveled distance is 2πR2\pi R. Acceleration is −ω2r(t)-\omega^2\mathbf r(t), pointing inward with magnitude Rω2R\omega^2. These results separate endpoint, speed, and curvature information. They also provide a direct benchmark for a numerical lab: a method that reports zero travel because the endpoints agree has confused displacement with distance.

The sign of v⋅a\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a determines the instantaneous change of speed only when v≠0\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0. If v⋅a=0\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a=0, speed has zero derivative at that instant but need not be globally constant. For r(t)=⟨t,t2,0⟩\mathbf r(t)=\langle t,t^2,0\rangle, v⋅a=4t\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a=4t, so at zero the speed derivative is zero while the speed increases for t>0t>0. If v=0\mathbf v=\mathbf0, the formula divides by zero; speed can have a corner even for a smooth position path, as r(t)=⟨t2,0,0⟩\mathbf r(t)=\langle t^2,0,0\rangle shows. Examine one-sided behavior there instead.

The displacement integral ∫abv(t) dt\int_a^b\mathbf v(t)\,dt is vector-valued and integrates each component. By the fundamental theorem, it equals r(b)−r(a)\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a) when r\mathbf r is differentiable with suitable continuity. In contrast, integrating the scalar speed ∣v∣|\mathbf v| produces path length. The triangle inequality for integrals gives ∣∫abv(t) dt∣≤∫ab∣v(t)∣ dt|\int_a^b\mathbf v(t)\,dt|\le\int_a^b|\mathbf v(t)|\,dt, so displacement magnitude cannot exceed traveled distance. This inequality is a strong check on modeled motion, regardless of how complicated the path looks.

The velocity–acceleration relationship can be checked by differentiating kinetic energy per unit mass, ∣v∣2/2|\mathbf v|^2/2. Its derivative is v⋅a\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a. When v⋅a>0\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a>0, the speed is increasing at that instant if v≠0\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0; when v⋅a<0\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a<0, it is decreasing. A perpendicular acceleration has v⋅a=0\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a=0 and changes direction without first-order change in speed. This dot-product viewpoint works in any number of Cartesian dimensions and avoids relying on the sign of one coordinate component. It also clarifies why a vehicle turning at constant speed still requires acceleration.

Average velocity over [a,b][a,b] is [r(b)−r(a)]/(b−a)[\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a)]/(b-a), a vector. Average speed is [∫ab∣r′(t)∣ dt]/(b−a)\bigl[\int_a^b|\mathbf r^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\bigr]/(b-a), a nonnegative scalar. They can differ even in magnitude, because a route can bend or reverse. On a complete constant-speed circular lap, average velocity is zero while average speed equals the constant speed. Reporting one as the other would erase the path taken. Both quantities divide by elapsed time, but their numerators answer different geometric questions.

Curvature separates bending from timing

Suppose rr is twice continuously differentiable and regular, meaning r′(t)≠0r^{\prime}(t)\ne0 throughout the interval under discussion. Its unit tangent is T=r′/∣r′∣T=r^{\prime}/|r^{\prime}|. Arc length ss increases at rate ds/dt=∣r′∣ds/dt=|r^{\prime}|. Curvature κ=∣dT/ds∣\kappa=|dT/ds| measures how rapidly the tangent changes per unit distance traveled. It has units of inverse length. This measures the bending of the curve rather than how quickly a particular traveler follows it.

Applying the chain rule gives κ=∣T′∣/∣r′∣\kappa=|\mathbf T^{\prime}|/|\mathbf r^{\prime}|. An equivalent computational formula is κ=∣r′×r′′∣/∣r′∣3\kappa=|\mathbf r^{\prime}\times\mathbf r^{\prime\prime}|/|\mathbf r^{\prime}|^3. A derivation sketch differentiates T=v/∣v∣\mathbf T=\mathbf v/|\mathbf v|, removes the component of acceleration parallel to v\mathbf v, and uses ∣v×a∣2=∣v∣2∣a∣2−(v⋅a)2|\mathbf v\times\mathbf a|^2=|\mathbf v|^2|\mathbf a|^2-(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a)^2. The hypotheses matter: neither quotient defines curvature directly at a stationary parameter value. A different regular parameter may sometimes describe the same geometric curve there.

When κ>0\kappa>0, the principal unit normal is N=T′/∣T′∣\mathbf N=\mathbf T^{\prime}/|\mathbf T^{\prime}|. Differentiating v=∣v∣T\mathbf v=|\mathbf v|\mathbf T yields a=aTT+aNN\mathbf a=a_T\mathbf T+a_N\mathbf N, where aT=ddt∣v∣a_T=\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf v| is signed and aN=κ∣v∣2a_N=\kappa|\mathbf v|^2 is nonnegative. For a regular path, curvature can be interpreted through the osculating circle: where κ>0\kappa>0, its radius is 1/κ1/\kappa. A straight line has κ=0\kappa=0 and formally infinite radius of curvature, but no unique finite center of curvature. This interpretation connects the derivative definition with a geometric object.
