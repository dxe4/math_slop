1. Forms measure oriented directions

A line integral of P dx+Q dyP\,dx+Q\,dy assigns a number to a point and a direction of travel. The expression is not an ordinary scalar function: at a point, it takes a tangent vector (vx,vy)(v_x,v_y) to Pvx+QvyPv_x+Qv_y. Definition 29.1: a covector is a linear map from vectors to real numbers. The coordinate covector dxidx_i returns the iith component. A one-form on an open set assigns a covector at each point, with coefficient functions of the declared regularity. A zero-form is an ordinary function.

Definition 29.2: a kk-form assigns an alternating kk-linear map to each point. Alternating means swapping two arguments changes the sign, so equal arguments give zero. In coordinates on Rn\mathbb R^n, it is a sum ω=∑i1<⋯<ikai1…ik dxi1∧⋯∧dxik\omega=\sum_{i_1<\cdots<i_k}a_{i_1\ldots i_k}\,dx_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{i_k}. The basis wedge evaluated on vectors v1,…,vkv_1,\ldots,v_k is the determinant of the matrix whose row pp, column qq entry is (vq)ip(v_q)_{i_p}. This determinant convention fixes the normalization and every orientation sign.

The wedge product is bilinear and concatenates basis covectors, using dxi∧dxj=−dxj∧dxidx_i\wedge dx_j=-dx_j\wedge dx_i and dxi∧dxi=0dx_i\wedge dx_i=0. Order the remaining indices and include the sign of that permutation. If α\alpha has degree pp and β\beta degree qq, then α∧β=(−1)pqβ∧α\alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{pq}\beta\wedge\alpha: moving each of the pp covectors past all qq requires pqpq swaps. This proof applies term by term; repeated-index terms vanish on both sides. Two two-forms therefore commute, while two one-forms anticommute.

A kk-form vanishes when k>nk>n, because every coordinate wedge then repeats an index. A one-form wedges with itself to zero, but an even-degree form need not. For example, (dx1∧dx2+dx3∧dx4)2(dx_1\wedge dx_2+dx_3\wedge dx_4)^2 is 2 dx1∧dx2∧dx3∧dx42\,dx_1\wedge dx_2\wedge dx_3\wedge dx_4, where the square means wedge product. Treating every wedge as ordinary multiplication or claiming every form squares to zero would destroy this distinction.

A form carries orientation information without requiring lengths or angles. In Euclidean coordinates a one-form can be represented by a vector using a dot product, but that identification uses the metric. Under a coordinate change its covector components follow a pullback rule, not an arbitrary vector-component mnemonic. The ordinary area measure dAdA is nonnegative; the area form dx∧dydx\wedge dy changes sign under an orientation reversal. Their numerical values agree only after an orientation convention is fixed.

2. Pullback is the chain rule for integrands

Definition 29.3: for a continuously differentiable map ϕ:V⊂Rm→U⊂Rn\phi:V\subset\mathbb R^m\to U\subset\mathbb R^n, define the pullback by (ϕ∗ω)u(v1,…,vk)=ωϕ(u)(Dϕ(u)v1,…,Dϕ(u)vk)(\phi^*\omega)_u(v_1,\ldots,v_k)=\omega_{\phi(u)}(D\phi(u)v_1,\ldots,D\phi(u)v_k). For a function, ϕ∗f=f∘ϕ\phi^*f=f\circ\phi. The map may lower the available dimension; if k>mk>m, the pulled-back form is zero. Injectivity is unnecessary for defining a pullback. It becomes important when the map is used to parametrize an integral without counting a region repeatedly.

The coordinate rule is ϕ∗(dxi)=dϕi\phi^*(dx_i)=d\phi_i. Indeed both sides evaluate a vector vv to the iith component of Dϕ vD\phi\,v, which is the directional derivative of ϕi\phi_i. Thus substitute the coordinate functions into each coefficient, replace every coordinate differential by the corresponding differential, and expand the wedges. The determinant definition also proves ϕ∗(α∧β)=ϕ∗α∧ϕ∗β\phi^*(\alpha\wedge\beta)=\phi^*\alpha\wedge\phi^*\beta, first for coordinate wedges and then by bilinearity.

Pullback respects composition: (ϕ∘ψ)∗ω=ψ∗(ϕ∗ω)(\phi\circ\psi)^*\omega=\psi^*(\phi^*\omega). At a point, the chain rule says D(ϕ∘ψ)=Dϕ DψD(\phi\circ\psi)=D\phi\,D\psi. Substituting this identity into every vector argument of the definition produces the same alternating map on both sides. The reversal in the written order is meaningful: an integrand travels backward through maps, whereas points travel forward. No inverse of either map is required.

For a curve r(t)r(t), the one-form P dx+Q dy+R dzP\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz pulls back to [P(r)x′+Q(r)y′+R(r)z′] dt[P(r)x'+Q(r)y'+R(r)z']\,dt. This is the usual work integral. For a surface r(u,v)r(u,v), the two-form P dy∧dz+Q dz∧dx+R dx∧dyP\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy pulls back to F(r)⋅(ru×rv) du∧dv\mathbf F(r)\cdot(r_u\times r_v)\,du\wedge dv. Expand each two-by-two minor to verify the cross-product formula. The order of u,vu,v fixes its sign.

For a map between equal dimensions, the top-degree form pulls back by the signed Jacobian determinant. In the plane, ϕ∗(f dx∧dy)=f(ϕ)det⁡Dϕ du∧dv\phi^*(f\,dx\wedge dy)=f(\phi)\det D\phi\,du\wedge dv. Ordinary change of variables for a nonnegative area measure uses the absolute determinant instead. The two rules answer different orientation questions. Replacing the signed determinant by an absolute value in every forms calculation changes an oriented integral into a different quantity.

3. Exterior differentiation and its identities

Definition 29.4: for a form with continuously differentiable coefficients, set dω=∑IdaI∧dxId\omega=\sum_I da_I\wedge dx_I, where daI=∑j(∂jaI)dxjda_I=\sum_j(\partial_j a_I)dx_j. This raises degree by one. In particular, df=fxdx+fydy+fzdzdf=f_xdx+f_ydy+f_zdz. For α=Pdx+Qdy+Rdz\alpha=Pdx+Qdy+Rdz, collecting increasing coordinate orders gives dα=(Qx−Py)dx∧dy+(Rx−Pz)dx∧dz+(Ry−Qz)dy∧dzd\alpha=(Q_x-P_y)dx\wedge dy+(R_x-P_z)dx\wedge dz+(R_y-Q_z)dy\wedge dz. Every sign follows from reordering a wedge.

For a flux form β=P dy∧dz+Q dz∧dx+R dx∧dy\beta=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy, the derivative is dβ=(Px+Qy+Rz)dx∧dy∧dzd\beta=(P_x+Q_y+R_z)dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. The three cyclic orders have positive sign in three dimensions. This is divergence in form notation. The preceding one-form derivative encodes curl, but a vector representation of that two-form requires the chosen Euclidean metric and orientation. The form itself is already a well-defined integrand.

Proposition 29.5: for a C2C^2 form, d(dω)=0d(d\omega)=0. It suffices to examine one term a dxIa\,dx_I. Its second derivative is ∑j,ℓ∂ℓ∂ja dxℓ∧dxj∧dxI\sum_{j,\ell}\partial_\ell\partial_j a\,dx_\ell\wedge dx_j\wedge dx_I. Terms with repeated indices vanish. The terms indexed by distinct j,ℓj,\ell cancel in pairs because their mixed partial derivatives agree and their first two wedges have opposite signs. Summing over II proves the assertion. Continuous second partial derivatives are an explicit sufficient regularity hypothesis.

The graded product rule is d(α∧β)=dα∧β+(−1)pα∧dβd(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^p\alpha\wedge d\beta when α\alpha has degree pp. For coordinate monomials α=a dxI\alpha=a\,dx_I, β=b dxJ\beta=b\,dx_J, apply d(ab)=b da+a dbd(ab)=b\,da+a\,db. The second term initially places dbdb before dxIdx_I; moving this one-form past pp coordinate factors introduces (−1)p(-1)^p. Bilinearity proves the formula for sums. A missing sign in this rule can turn a true cancellation into a false nonzero derivative.

For a C2C^2 map and a C1C^1 form, d(ϕ∗ω)=ϕ∗(dω)d(\phi^*\omega)=\phi^*(d\omega). On a dxIa\,dx_I, the pulled-back expression is (a∘ϕ)dϕi1∧⋯∧dϕik(a\circ\phi)d\phi_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\phi_{i_k}. The product rule and d(dϕi)=0d(d\phi_i)=0 leave only d(a∘ϕ)d(a\circ\phi) wedged with these factors. The ordinary chain rule identifies this differential with ϕ∗(da)\phi^*(da). Summing proves naturality. The calculation explains both the smoothness and the compatibility of differentiation with parametrization.

Exterior differentiation commutes with a smooth pullback

If ϕ:V→U\phi:V\to U is C2C^2 and ω\omega has C1C^1 coefficients, then d(ϕ∗ω)=ϕ∗(dω)d(\phi^*\omega)=\phi^*(d\omega) wherever the maps are defined.

d(ϕ∗ω)=ϕ∗(dω).d(\phi^*\omega)=\phi^*(d\omega).
Reasoning and proof

For each coordinate covector, ϕ∗dxi=dϕi\phi^*dx_i=d\phi_i. Pullback preserves wedge products by the alternating determinant definition.

For one coefficient term, use the graded product rule on (a∘ϕ)dϕi1∧⋯∧dϕik(a\circ\phi)d\phi_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\phi_{i_k}. All terms differentiating a factor dϕid\phi_i vanish by equality of its continuous mixed partials.

The surviving coefficient derivative is d(a∘ϕ)=ϕ∗dad(a\circ\phi)=\phi^*da by the chain rule. This gives the desired equality for that term; linearity gives the full result.

4. Integration includes dimension, coverage, and orientation

Definition 29.6: if an oriented kk-dimensional patch is parametrized by r:D⊂Rk→Rnr:D\subset\mathbb R^k\to\mathbb R^n, integrate a kk-form by ∫Dr∗ω\int_D r^*\omega. After pullback, the integrand is one coefficient times du1∧⋯∧dukdu_1\wedge\cdots\wedge du_k, so this is an ordinary multiple integral of that signed coefficient. Use a regular parametrization, meaning rank Dr=kDr=k in the interior, which covers the patch once there. Seams and exceptional boundary sets must be accounted for rather than counted as extra copies.

An orientation-compatible change of parameters preserves the integral: the chain rule gives pullback composition, and ordinary change of variables cancels the positive Jacobian factor. An orientation-reversing change negates it. Covering a circle twice is not an innocent reparametrization of a once-covered curve; its integral counts the traversal twice. Orientation describes sign, while coverage describes multiplicity, and both must be stated.

For a compact oriented manifold with boundary, the induced boundary orientation uses the outward-first convention. An outward transverse vector followed by a positively oriented boundary basis must give a positive basis of the manifold. On a planar region with orientation dx∧dydx\wedge dy, this makes the outer boundary counterclockwise and a hole boundary clockwise. On an upward surface, it is the familiar right-hand rule. On a closed surface there is no boundary curve.

On a positively oriented nn-box, the face xj=bjx_j=b_j has orientation (−1)j−1(-1)^{j-1} times the coordinate order with dxjdx_j omitted; the face xj=ajx_j=a_j has the opposite orientation. To check the upper sign, move the outward vector eje_j from the first position into its jjth coordinate position, making j−1j-1 swaps. Replacing eje_j by −ej-e_j at the lower face changes the sign once more.

The boundary of a boundary cancels as an oriented chain. A corner belongs to two adjacent faces with opposite induced signs, so their contributions cancel. It is imprecise to claim the set of corners does not exist. The algebraic cancellation is what connects the boundary operation to d2=0d^2=0. When a surface or solid is split into compatible pieces, shared faces likewise occur twice with opposite orientations, leaving the complete external boundary.

∫r(D)ω=∫Dr∗ωfor a one-to-one, regular, orientation-compatible patch.\int_{r(D)}\omega=\int_D r^*\omega\qquad\text{for a one-to-one, regular, orientation-compatible patch}.

5. Stokes on boxes and the general theorem

Theorem 29.7, coordinate-box Stokes: let B=∏j=1n[aj,bj]B=\prod_{j=1}^n[a_j,b_j] with aj<bja_j<b_j, carrying the standard orientation, and let ω\omega be a C1C^1 (n−1)(n-1)-form on an open neighborhood of BB. Then ∫Bdω=∫∂Bω\int_B d\omega=\int_{\partial B}\omega, with the complete induced oriented boundary. A box has corners; this statement is proved directly and does not quietly identify it with a smooth-boundary manifold.

Write ω=∑j(−1)j−1Aj dx1∧⋯∧dxj^∧⋯∧dxn\omega=\sum_j(-1)^{j-1}A_j\,dx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx_j}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_n, where the hat means omit that coordinate. Exterior differentiation gives dω=(∑j∂jAj)dx1∧⋯∧dxnd\omega=(\sum_j\partial_jA_j)dx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_n: all other coefficient derivatives repeat an existing coordinate. On a face with fixed xjx_j, all terms except the jjth pull back to zero.

The upper jjth face contributes the integral of AjA_j with xj=bjx_j=b_j; the lower contributes its negative with xj=ajx_j=a_j. These signs follow from the outward-first calculation and the factor (−1)j−1(-1)^{j-1} in ω\omega. Their difference is the iterated integral of ∂jAj\partial_jA_j by the one-variable fundamental theorem. Adding over jj yields exactly the integral of dωd\omega. This proves the theorem, using finite sums and the ordinary multiple-integral theorem for continuous functions on a box.

For a C2C^2 regular parametrized curvilinear box, apply this proof to the pulled-back form. Naturality gives d(r∗ω)=r∗(dω)d(r^*\omega)=r^*(d\omega), and the mapped face orientations must agree with the chosen orientation. For a finite collection of such cells with matching common faces, sum the identities: opposite internal faces cancel. This proves Stokes for that specified finite cell representation. The existence of a compatible cell representation for an arbitrary manifold is a separate question, not something a cancellation picture proves.

Theorem 29.8, general smooth Stokes: let MM be a compact oriented smooth kk-manifold with smooth boundary, k≥1k\ge1, and let ω\omega be a C1C^1 (k−1)(k-1)-form on a neighborhood of MM (or a form on MM with C1C^1 coefficients up to its boundary). Then ∫Mdω=∫∂Mω\int_Md\omega=\int_{\partial M}\omega, where the boundary has the induced orientation. Empty boundary gives zero on the right. Compactness means every open cover has a finite subcover, so finitely many suitable coordinate neighborhoods suffice. Smooth charts make these local pieces look like open subsets of Euclidean space or a half-space.

The general theorem is an explicitly cited result here. Its full manifold proof uses a partition of unity to localize to interior and boundary charts; that construction and the coordinate independence of general manifold integration are developed in the primary references. The complete proofs supplied above cover boxes, their regular parametrized images, and compatible finite cell sums. The applications below use those cases or the classical surface theorems already studied. This distinction keeps the genuinely global theorem separate from the argument actually established in this chapter.

6. The classical theorems are degree-specific cases

On an interval, ω=f\omega=f is a zero-form and dω=f′(t)dtd\omega=f'(t)dt. The induced boundary is the terminal endpoint minus the initial endpoint, so Stokes reads ∫abf′(t)dt=f(b)−f(a)\int_a^bf'(t)dt=f(b)-f(a). For a curve in space and a scalar function ff, pullback and the chain rule give ∫Cdf=f(end)−f(start)\int_Cdf=f(\text{end})-f(\text{start}). This is the fundamental theorem for line integrals, with no arc-length factor.

On a planar region, take ω=Pdx+Qdy\omega=Pdx+Qdy. Its exterior derivative is (Qx−Py)dx∧dy(Q_x-P_y)dx\wedge dy. General Stokes becomes Green's circulation formula with the complete positively oriented boundary. The flux version follows by using the one-form Pdy−QdxPdy-Qdx, whose derivative is (Px+Qy)dx∧dy(P_x+Q_y)dx\wedge dy. The same boundary supports two different integrands, and matching the requested quantity remains essential.

For a surface in R3\mathbb R^3, let α=F1dx+F2dy+F3dz\alpha=F_1dx+F_2dy+F_3dz. Its derivative pulls back to (∇×F)⋅(ru×rv)du∧dv(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot(r_u\times r_v)du\wedge dv. Thus ∫Sdα=∫∂Sα\int_Sd\alpha=\int_{\partial S}\alpha is the classical curl Stokes theorem. Reversing the surface orientation reverses the induced edge direction as well. Reversing only one of them produces an incompatible setup.

For a three-dimensional solid, use β=F1dy∧dz+F2dz∧dx+F3dx∧dy\beta=F_1dy\wedge dz+F_2dz\wedge dx+F_3dx\wedge dy. Its derivative is (∇⋅F)dx∧dy∧dz(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)dx\wedge dy\wedge dz, while its boundary integral is outward flux. Hence Stokes gives the divergence theorem. The distinction between a work one-form and a flux two-form is part of the dimension bookkeeping; it cannot be removed by naming both simply “the field”.

Smoothness must hold where the theorem integrates a derivative, including the whole chosen interior or spanning surface. A form can be smooth along a boundary yet undefined inside it. A hole does not itself prevent Stokes: it creates another boundary component. A singularity lying in the region does prevent an invocation requiring a smooth neighborhood. Degree, domain, complete boundary, and orientation should be written before evaluating either side.

7. Closed forms, exact forms, and a hole

Definition 29.9: a differentiable form is closed if dω=0d\omega=0; it is exact if ω=dη\omega=d\eta for an appropriately differentiable form of one lower degree. With a C2C^2 primitive, exact implies closed by d2=0d^2=0. The converse is a global question about the domain. All closed one-forms are locally exact on sufficiently small balls by the construction below; a single global primitive can still fail around a hole.

Proposition 29.10, star-shaped one-form lemma: let UU be open and star-shaped about zero, meaning tx∈Utx\in U for x∈Ux\in U and 0≤t≤10\le t\le1. If α=∑iPi(x)dxi\alpha=\sum_iP_i(x)dx_i has C1C^1 coefficients and is closed, then Φ(x)=∫01∑iPi(tx)xi dt\Phi(x)=\int_0^1\sum_iP_i(tx)x_i\,dt satisfies dΦ=αd\Phi=\alpha. A translated formula applies when the star centre is a different point.

Proof: closedness means ∂jPi=∂iPj\partial_jP_i=\partial_iP_j. Differentiate the proposed integral with respect to xjx_j to obtain ∂jΦ=∫01[Pj(tx)+t∑ixi∂jPi(tx)]dt\partial_j\Phi=\int_0^1[P_j(tx)+t\sum_ix_i\partial_jP_i(tx)]dt. Replace the cross partial by ∂iPj\partial_iP_j. The bracket is the derivative in tt of tPj(tx)tP_j(tx), so the fundamental theorem gives ∂jΦ=Pj(x)\partial_j\Phi=P_j(x). Differentiating inside the finite integral is valid because continuous first partials are uniformly continuous on a compact neighborhood of each radial segment; difference quotients then converge uniformly there. Thus the integral construction gives a C2C^2 potential.

On the punctured plane, α=(−y dx+x dy)/(x2+y2)\alpha=(-y\,dx+x\,dy)/(x^2+y^2) is smooth and closed. Its pullback by the unit circle is dtdt, so its integral over one counterclockwise circuit is 2π2\pi. If it were dΦd\Phi for a single-valued potential throughout the punctured plane, the line-integral theorem would make that closed-loop integral zero. It is therefore not exact there. The field is undefined at the origin, so applying Green on the full disk would violate its hypotheses.

On the right half-plane, the same form has potential arctan⁡(y/x)\arctan(y/x). Differentiation gives the two displayed coefficients, and that domain admits one continuous angle branch. The local potential does not extend to a single angle around the whole punctured plane. This example separates the local differential condition from the global antiderivative. Higher-degree versions lead toward de Rham theory, which is a continuation rather than an unstated prerequisite.

A finite numerical agreement cannot decide closedness, exactness, or the general theorem. Closedness asks for a derivative identity at every point, and exactness asks for a globally defined primitive. The laboratory below uses an analytic error formula to explain a finite polygon calculation. Its success supplies a check on that calculation, while the theorem's domain and orientation hypotheses remain mathematical obligations.

Closed one-forms on a star-shaped domain have a potential

For an open set star-shaped about 00 and a closed C1C^1 one-form α=∑iPidxi\alpha=\sum_iP_i dx_i, the function Φ(x)=∫01∑iPi(tx)xi dt\Phi(x)=\int_0^1\sum_iP_i(tx)x_i\,dt satisfies dΦ=αd\Phi=\alpha.

Φ(x)=∫01∑iPi(tx)xi dt,dΦ=α.\Phi(x)=\int_0^1\sum_iP_i(tx)x_i\,dt,\qquad d\Phi=\alpha.
Reasoning and proof

The coefficient functions and their first derivatives are continuous on compact neighborhoods of the radial segments, so the finite integral may be differentiated by a uniform difference-quotient bound.

For each coordinate jj, differentiate and use ∂jPi=∂iPj\partial_jP_i=\partial_iP_j to write the integrand as Pj(tx)+t∑ixi∂iPj(tx)=ddt[tPj(tx)]P_j(tx)+t\sum_i x_i\partial_iP_j(tx)=\frac{d}{dt}[tP_j(tx)].

The fundamental theorem evaluates that integral as Pj(x)−0P_j(x)-0. Hence every partial derivative of Φ\Phi is the required coefficient, proving the claimed potential.

  20. Problem 20. Reasoning A proposed formula integrates a one-form α\alpha over the two-dimensional boundary of a solid and dαd\alpha over the three-dimensional interior. Explain the degree mismatch and identify the appropriate form degree for a flux theorem.

    Hint. First: A form's degree must equal the integration domain's dimension. Next: A solid needs a three-form in its interior and a two-form on its boundary.

    Solution. A one-form is naturally integrated over a curve, not an entire surface, and its derivative is a two-form, not a volume form.   25. Problem 25. Reasoning Suppose a proposed surface has no globally consistent orientation. Can the usual oriented form version of Stokes be applied by choosing normals independently on its patches? Explain what remains meaningful.

More general theories can integrate other objects on nonorientable spaces, but that is not the theorem stated here.

Chapter synthesis

  • A differential form measures oriented k-dimensional directions; its degree must match the domain being integrated.
  • Pullback implements the chain rule for integrands, including signed Jacobian determinants and multiplicity.
  • Exterior differentiation raises degree, satisfies a graded product rule, and has d²=0 under explicit regularity.
  • Stokes equates a derivative integral with the complete induced boundary integral; the coordinate-box proof is a repeated fundamental theorem calculation.
  • Closedness is local differential information, while a global potential can be obstructed by the domain.

Common mistakes to check

  • “A wedge is ordinary multiplication.” Its order records orientation, and repeated coordinate covectors make a term zero. The graded sign depends on both degrees.
  • “A signed Jacobian can always be replaced by its absolute value.” Absolute values belong to unsigned area or volume measure; a form retains orientation.
  • “A hole prevents Stokes.” A smooth form can satisfy Stokes on a region with holes, provided every boundary component is included with its induced direction.
  • “Closed means globally exact.” The punctured-plane vortex is closed but has a nonzero closed-loop integral and no global single-valued potential.
  • “The coordinate-box proof proves the entire manifold theorem without further work.” General chart integration and the localization step require additional results, explicitly cited here.
  • “The boundary of the boundary has no points.” Faces can meet at corners; it is their oriented chain contributions that cancel.

Choose one mistake that tempted you. Give a counterexample or identify the missing condition.
