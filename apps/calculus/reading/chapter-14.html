1. Replace the curve with pieces we can integrate

An integral can exist even when its antiderivative has no convenient formula. Measurements may give only sampled values, and a physical model may be easy to evaluate but difficult to integrate symbolically. Numerical quadrature replaces the integrand by a simpler function whose integral is known. Unlike a symbolic answer, a decimal approximation needs an account of its accuracy. Begin by recording the interval, the assumptions about the function, and the units of the answer.

Partition [a,b][a,b] into nn equal subintervals of width h=(b−a)/nh=(b-a)/n, with xi=a+ihx_i=a+ih and fi=f(xi)f_i=f(x_i). The composite trapezoidal rule joins adjacent samples by straight lines. Each strip contributes h(fi+fi+1)/2h(f_i+f_{i+1})/2. Summing counts the interior samples twice and the two endpoint samples once. For a convex function, every chord lies above the graph, so the approximation overestimates the integral; a concave function reverses the inequality. This geometric statement assumes the function remains convex or concave throughout each strip.

Simpson’s rule groups subintervals in pairs and integrates the quadratic interpolant through each group’s three equally spaced samples. Consequently nn must be a positive even integer. Its weights alternate 44 and 22 between endpoint weights 11. Although the interpolants are quadratic, symmetry makes the rule exact for every polynomial of degree at most three. Higher algebraic accuracy does not mean the rule is always a better choice: rough data, singularities, and limited sample locations may violate the conditions that make its accuracy predictable.

The Simpson weights come from one symmetric pair. Center three nodes at −h,0,h-h,0,h and integrate their three quadratic Lagrange basis polynomials over [−h,h][-h,h]; their integrals are h/3,4h/3,h/3h/3,4h/3,h/3. Therefore the pair contributes h[f(−h)+4f(0)+f(h)]/3h[f(-h)+4f(0)+f(h)]/3. A cubic minus its quadratic interpolant is a multiple of t(t2−h2)t(t^2-h^2), which is odd, so its integral over this pair is zero too. Joining pairs shares their endpoints and produces the composite 1,4,2,4,…,2,4,11,4,2,4,\ldots,2,4,1 weights; the number of subintervals must be even.

If xx is time in seconds and f(x)f(x) is a flow rate in litres per second, every weighted height is still a rate, while multiplication by hh produces litres. Numerical integration does not remove unit checks. With measured data, quadrature error also differs from measurement error: repeatedly refining a formula cannot recover detail the measurements never recorded.

The trapezoidal rule is exact for every affine function because the graph and its chord coincide on each panel. Composite formulas require correct endpoint weights. For even nn, Simpson's rule is Sn=h3[f0+fn+4∑j=1n/2f2j−1+2∑j=1n/2−1f2j]S_n=\frac h3[f_0+f_n+4\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}f_{2j-1}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n/2-1}f_{2j}]. The odd-indexed interior nodes carry weight four, even-indexed interior nodes weight two, and endpoints weight one. A common implementation mistake gives both endpoints weight four or uses odd nn, destroying the rule's derivation. A measurement table changes the question. If samples are only available at fixed times, decreasing hh in a quadrature program would require inventing new data by interpolation. That may estimate the integral under an interpolation model, but it does not reduce measurement uncertainty. Conversely, if ff is a known formula that can be evaluated at new points, refinement is meaningful. Report whether the input is a continuous model, sampled data with interpolation, or noisy measurements. The same weighted-sum formula can have very different reliability under those three circumstances.

When building a quadrature rule from samples, distinguish interpolation from integration. The trapezoidal rule first replaces ff by a piecewise linear interpolant passing through measured nodes; then it integrates that interpolant exactly. Simpson replaces ff on each pair of panels by a quadratic interpolant. The numerical answer is therefore exact for the surrogate curve but approximate for the original one. Error analysis asks how far the surrogate's accumulated area can be from the true area. This viewpoint makes clear why a missed narrow spike matters: the interpolant never sees it. It also explains why smoother functions often permit better accuracy from the same sample count.

Adaptive refinement spends more samples where the function needs them. On one interval compute a Simpson value SS, then split at its midpoint and compute the sum Sleft+SrightS_{\mathrm{left}}+S_{\mathrm{right}}. Compare ∣Sleft+Sright−S∣/15|S_{\mathrm{left}}+S_{\mathrm{right}}-S|/15 with a local error budget. Accept the fine result if the estimate is small; otherwise subdivide again. Split the error budget between children so their budgets sum to the parent budget. A practical algorithm also limits depth and evaluations and reports failure if its budget cannot be met. Quietly accepting a maximum-depth result would hide uncertainty.

The estimator works best when the function is sufficiently smooth and sampled structure represents its true variation. A narrow peak between all initial nodes may be missed entirely. Known breakpoints should therefore be supplied by splitting the domain before integration. At very fine spacing, subtraction and summation roundoff compete with discretization error. More samples are not a substitute for examining the function, a convergence study, and an independent check when one is available.

An error budget is a promise about the final reported number. If a tolerance is 10−410^{-4} and an improper tail is bounded by 4⋅10−54\cdot10^{-5}, only 6⋅10−56\cdot10^{-5} remains for quadrature and roundoff. In adaptive recursion, each child's allowed contribution should sum to the parent's, and termination caused by iteration limits should be reported as incomplete. Distinguish estimated error, proven bound, and floating-point display precision in the final result.

A derivative-based certificate can be conservative by design. For exe^x on [0,1][0,1], the fourth derivative bound ee is attained only at the right endpoint, yet Simpson's formula uses that worst value to cover the whole interval. A smaller observed error is welcome but does not invalidate the bound. To claim a tighter rigorous number, one needs a sharper bound on the relevant derivative or a different theorem, not merely more displayed digits. If measurements include uncertainty, add a separate bound for uncertainty in the sampled heights. A quadrature theorem for an exact ff does not automatically cover noisy ff-values.

A practical convergence study reports several resolutions and their differences rather than one rounded output. For a smooth model, compute TnT_n, T2nT_{2n}, and T4nT_{4n}; if successive differences shrink by roughly a factor of four, the expected second-order regime may have begun. Keep raw values long enough to see whether apparent stability is merely rounding to the same printed decimal.

5. Cancellation and the principal value distinction

If ff is singular at an interior point cc, split the integral there. The ordinary improper integral exists only if both one-sided integrals converge separately to finite values. For 1/x1/x on [−1,1][-1,1], the left integral tends to −∞-\infty and the right integral to +∞+\infty. Adding these symbols is not an allowed operation that produces zero. The ordinary improper integral diverges.

The Cauchy principal value deliberately imposes symmetric cutoffs. For this example, ∫−1−εdx/x+∫ε1dx/x=0\int_{-1}^{-\varepsilon}dx/x+\int_\varepsilon^1 dx/x=0 for every ε>0\varepsilon>0, so the principal value is zero. Choosing the left cutoff ε\varepsilon and the right cutoff 2ε2\varepsilon instead produces −log⁡2-\log 2. Different coupling gives a different outcome, revealing why symmetry must not be silently built into the definition of an ordinary integral.

A responsible strategy therefore starts with domain analysis. Locate singularities, decide convergence analytically when possible, transform or split troublesome regions, bound excluded tails, and only then apply a numerical rule. State whether the result is an ordinary integral, a principal value, or a finite truncation. The lab below contrasts a smooth convergence study with endpoint truncation so that numerical stability and mathematical existence remain separate questions.

Principal value can be a useful mathematical object, but it answers a different limiting question. For 1/x1/x on [−1,1][-1,1], symmetric holes [−ε,ε][-\varepsilon,\varepsilon] force exact cancellation. If the right hole is twice as wide, the combined truncated value is −ln⁡2-\ln2 for every ε>0\varepsilon>0, so the limiting number depends on the imposed coupling. The ordinary improper integral avoids this ambiguity by requiring each one-sided integral to converge independently. In a numerical program, a symmetric grid may accidentally compute a principal value while labeling the output an integral. The label must match the definition used.

Even a finite principal value is not guaranteed by visual oddness if the truncation is not symmetric or the interval is not balanced around the singularity. On [−1,2][-1,2], the symmetric-hole principal value of 1/x1/x is ln⁡2\ln2, while the ordinary improper integral still diverges. This value comes from cancellation near zero plus a remaining positive interval from 11 to 22. It may be appropriate in some transform theories, but it must be explicitly requested. When a physical density or rate is nonnegative, principal-value cancellation is usually not a meaningful substitute for missing mass or flow.

The evaluation theorem from chapter 11 cannot be applied across a singular point because its continuity hypothesis fails. Writing F(b)−F(a)F(b)-F(a) after finding a primitive on each side is a formal shortcut that can hide two divergent pieces. Instead compute ∫ac−εf(x) dx\int_a^{c-\varepsilon}f(x)\,dx and ∫c+δbf(x) dx\int_{c+\delta}^b f(x)\,dx separately, then let ε→0+\varepsilon\to0^+ and δ→0+\delta\to0^+ independently. Only if both limits are finite may their sum be called the ordinary improper integral. This procedure also determines whether numerical quadrature can be meaningfully applied to each truncated piece with a tail error estimate.

A sound numerical report should include the mathematical status of the integral. For a convergent improper integral, state the finite truncation, the bound on what was excluded, the quadrature method and bound or estimate, and the final precision. For a divergent integral, report divergence rather than a large cutoff-dependent number. For a principal value, name the symmetric or otherwise specified cutoff and do not imply ordinary convergence. These distinctions matter more than adding extra displayed decimal places.

The word “cancellation” has two meanings that should not be confused. A convergent signed integral may have positive and negative finite contributions that cancel partly; this is ordinary integration. A principal value of 1/x1/x instead cancels two contributions whose magnitudes individually diverge. The latter cancellation depends on a coordinated cutoff and cannot be rearranged freely. This is analogous to the danger in conditionally convergent series: order or grouping may matter. In applications, specify the limiting procedure that the measurement or model actually represents rather than selecting a symmetric cutoff solely because it produces a convenient answer.

When a singularity is only removable, the situation is different again. If f(x)=sin⁡x/xf(x)=\sin x/x for x≠0x\ne0 and f(0)=1f(0)=1, the function extends continuously across zero, so ordinary Riemann integration applies on a closed interval around it. No one-sided divergent pieces need to be cancelled. A numerical implementation may evaluate the limit near zero with a stable special formula rather than directly dividing two tiny values. The correct computational treatment follows from that classification.

The symbol ∫1∞f(x) dx\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx abbreviates a limit over finite intervals: lim⁡R→∞∫1Rf(x) dx\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_1^R f(x)\,dx.
