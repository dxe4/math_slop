Accumulation over an area

A one-variable integral adds contributions from small intervals. A double integral adds contributions from small patches of a plane. Imagine a thin sheet whose density varies with position. A patch of area ΔA\Delta A near (x,y)(x,y) has approximate mass ρ(x,y)ΔA\rho(x,y)\Delta A. Adding these masses and refining the patches gives the sheet’s total mass. If coordinates are measured in metres and density in kilograms per square metre, each summand and the final integral have units of kilograms.

For a rectangle R=[a,b]×[c,d]R=[a,b]\times[c,d], subdivide both coordinate intervals. Choose a sample point (xi∗,yj∗)(x_i^*,y_j^*) in each small rectangle and sum ∑i,jf(xi∗,yj∗)ΔxiΔyj\sum_{i,j}f(x_i^*,y_j^*)\Delta x_i\Delta y_j. The double Riemann integral exists when these sums approach the same finite value as the largest patch diameter tends to zero, independently of the sample choices. Continuity on a closed rectangle is a convenient sufficient hypothesis. The factor ΔxiΔyj\Delta x_i\Delta y_j matters: summing values alone counts samples rather than physical area.

The notation ∬Rf dA\iint_R f\,dA records both the field being accumulated and its domain. When f≥0f\ge0 is a height, this gives volume above RR and below z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y). For heights that change sign it gives signed volume, so positive and negative contributions can cancel. Geometric volume between surfaces z=u(x,y)z=u(x,y) and z=v(x,y)z=v(x,y) with u≤vu\le v is instead ∬R(v−u) dA\iint_R(v-u)\,dA over their common projection. The integral ∬D1 dA=area⁡(D)\iint_D1\,dA=\operatorname{area}(D) gives area, not a mysterious special kind of volume.

A general bounded region can be enclosed in a rectangle and its integrand ff set to zero outside the region. For continuous ff on a region with a piecewise smooth boundary, the boundary does not affect the integral because it has area zero. This does not mean the boundary is irrelevant computationally: a coarse grid can include too much or too little area near it. Refinement has to resolve geometry as well as changes in the function.

A double integral is the limit of weighted sums, not simply two antiderivatives written side by side. Partition a region into small pieces AiA_i with area ΔAi\Delta A_i and choose a point pip_i in each. The sum ∑if(pi)ΔAi\sum_i f(p_i)\Delta A_i records the accumulated quantity. If ff is continuous on a compact region with a well-behaved boundary, refining the largest piece diameter drives these sums toward a common value. This construction explains why the factor dAdA carries square units and why changing a region changes the integral even if the formula for ff stays fixed.

For a constant integrand f=cf=c, the integral over DD is ∬Dc dA=carea⁡(D)\iint_D c\,dA=c\operatorname{area}(D). This simple check catches missing bounds and unit errors. If DD is a unit triangle of area 1/2 m21/2\,\mathrm{m}^2 and c=6 kg/m2c=6\,\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^2, the total mass must be 3 kg3\,\mathrm{kg}. A calculation returning 6 kg6\,\mathrm{kg} has likely treated the triangle as a square. With variable density ρ\rho attaining extrema on DD, one can bound the mass by ρmin⁡area⁡(D)≤∬Dρ dA≤ρmax⁡area⁡(D)\rho_{\min}\operatorname{area}(D)\le\iint_D\rho\,dA\le\rho_{\max}\operatorname{area}(D). Such bounds are useful sanity checks before accepting a complicated antiderivative.

Signed volume differs from physical volume. If ff changes sign, ∬Df dA\iint_D f\,dA subtracts regions below the reference plane from those above. The geometric volume between z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y) and z=0z=0 is ∬D∣f∣ dA\iint_D|f|\,dA, provided the integral exists. For f(x,y)=xf(x,y)=x on the symmetric square D=[−1,1]2D=[-1,1]^2, the signed integral is 00 by odd symmetry, but the physical volume is positive. Decide what the application asks for before dropping an absolute value or interpreting a negative answer.

A Riemann sum can use corners, midpoints, or any selected point in each cell when the function is integrable and the mesh tends to zero. At finite resolution, these choices can produce different approximations. For f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2 on [0,1]2[0,1]^2, a lower-left rule underestimates because the function grows in both coordinates, while an upper-right rule overestimates. Midpoint values often reduce error for smooth functions, but a singularity or discontinuous boundary can spoil familiar rates. The limiting integral is independent of sample choices under its hypotheses; finite grids are approximations.

Average value over a region is 1area⁡(D)∬Df dA\dfrac{1}{\operatorname{area}(D)}\iint_D f\,dA when 0<area⁡(D)<∞0<\operatorname{area}(D)<\infty. This normalizes accumulation back to the units of ff. If ff is temperature, the integral has temperature times area units, while the average has temperature units. A weighted average with nonnegative density ρ\rho and positive finite mass instead divides ∬Dfρ dA\iint_D f\rho\,dA by ∬Dρ dA\iint_D\rho\,dA. Keeping the denominator visible prevents confusing a total quantity with a typical value. The same structure later appears in probability expectations.

Iterated integrals and the conditions behind Fubini

An iterated integral calculates the area accumulation by slices. In ∫ab[∫cdf(x,y) dy] dx\int_a^b\bigl[\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\bigr]\,dx, hold xx fixed while integrating in yy. The inner integral becomes a function of xx; integrate that function next. The differentials tell you the evaluation order from inside to outside. They also tell you which symbols act as constants during each calculation. For example, integrating xyxy in yy gives xy2/2xy^2/2 because xx is fixed.

For a continuous ff on a closed rectangle R=[a,b]×[c,d]R=[a,b]\times[c,d], the double integral equals either iterated integral. Uniform continuity here means: for every ε>0\varepsilon>0 one distance δ>0\delta>0 controls ∣f(P)−f(Q)∣<ε|f(P)-f(Q)|<\varepsilon for every pair P,Q∈RP,Q\in R closer than δ\delta. The theorem card turns that one uniform threshold into an inner-sum error bound and proves continuity of the integrated slice function. Use the continuous-rectangle result first; the broader measure version below is an optional later return.

Fubini’s theorem is particularly simple for continuous functions on rectangles, but the order of operations remains literal. In ∫01∫02(x+2y) dy dx\int_0^1\int_0^2(x+2y)\,dy\,dx, integrate in yy first while xx acts as a constant. The inner result is 2x+42x+4, and the outer integral is 55. Reversing order gives ∫02∫01(x+2y) dx dy=5\int_0^2\int_0^1(x+2y)\,dx\,dy=5. Writing new bounds that describe a different rectangle would invalidate the comparison even if the algebraic integrand were unchanged.

Without an applicable hypothesis, two iterated calculations need not agree. On the unit square away from the origin let f(x,y)=x2−y2(x2+y2)2f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}. For x>0x>0, its yy-antiderivative is y/(x2+y2)y/(x^2+y^2), so integrating yy first and then xx improperly gives π/4\pi/4; the reverse order gives −π/4-\pi/4. The exceptional edge slice and outer endpoint must be treated improperly, not silently assigned a value. In polar coordinates near the corner, ∣f∣ dA=∣cos⁡2θ∣ dr dθ/r|f|\,dA=|\cos2\theta|\,dr\,d\theta/r; on any angular sector where ∣cos⁡2θ∣|\cos2\theta| stays positive its radial integral diverges. Thus absolute integrability fails, and Fubini does not identify the two iterated values.

Optional later return: measure theory extends area beyond rectangles. A set has area zero if it can be covered by a countable collection of rectangles with arbitrarily small total area; ‘almost everywhere’ permits exceptions on such a set. A measurable function has threshold sets {(x,y):f(x,y)>t}\{(x,y):f(x,y)>t\} to which this extended area theory applies for every real tt. If a signed measurable function has finite ∬∣f∣\iint|f|, the broader Fubini theorem permits exchanging order despite exceptional slices. For a nonnegative measurable function, Tonelli permits exchange even when the common value is infinite. These are wider theorems with their own hypotheses. Work the continuous closed-rectangle proof here; return to measure theory later, and use the singular example now to see why an order swap needs a hypothesis.

The signed corner example has different iterated values because cancellation depends on integration order near its singular corner; its absolute integral diverges. A finite antiderivative in each order does not justify exchanging them. Check where a denominator vanishes and whether the relevant improper integral converges.

The order of integration can change difficulty dramatically even when both orders are valid. On the triangle 0≤x≤y≤10\le x\le y\le1, ∬Dey2 dA\iint_D e^{y^2}\,dA is easiest with xx inside: ∫01∫0yey2 dx dy=∫01yey2 dy=(e−1)/2\int_0^1\int_0^y e^{y^2}\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1 ye^{y^2}\,dy=(e-1)/2. A reliable check after reversing order is to test an interior point, a boundary point, and an exterior point against both descriptions. The order reversal in the ey2e^{y^2} triangle has a useful interpretation as weighted slices. At height yy, the horizontal length is yy, so that slice contributes yey2 dyye^{y^2}\,dy. The factor yy is geometric, not the result of a formal algebraic trick; it is precisely the width of the region at that height. If the region instead had width 1−y1-y, the remaining one-variable integrand would be (1−y)ey2(1-y)e^{y^2}, which is not simplified in the same way. Choosing an order therefore means choosing a slice geometry whose width interacts well with the integrand. A convincing solution records both the slice length and its variable range before evaluating the integral.

Why integration order agrees on a rectangle

If ff is continuous on a closed rectangle R=[a,b]×[c,d]R=[a,b]\times[c,d], either iterated integral equals the double integral over RR.

∬Rf dA=∫ab(∫cdf(x,y) dy)dx=∫cd(∫abf(x,y) dx)dy\iint_R f\,dA=\int_a^b\left(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx=\int_c^d\left(\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy
Reasoning and proof

Let R=[a,b]×[c,d]R=[a,b]\times[c,d] and define ω(δ)=sup⁡{∣f(P)−f(Q)∣:P,Q∈R, ∥P−Q∥≤δ}\omega(\delta)=\sup\{|f(P)-f(Q)|:P,Q\in R,\ \|P-Q\|\le\delta\}. Continuity on compact RR makes ff uniformly continuous, so ω(δ)→0\omega(\delta)\to0 as δ↓0\delta\downarrow0. It also makes ff bounded. The oscillation of ff within a cell of diameter at most δ\delta is at most ω(δ)\omega(\delta), so upper and lower rectangular sums differ by at most (b−a)(d−c)ω(δ)(b-a)(d-c)\omega(\delta). Hence the double Riemann integral exists and every sufficiently fine tagged grid sum has that limit.

For each fixed xx, the continuous slice y↦f(x,y)y\mapsto f(x,y) is Riemann integrable; put g(x)=∫cdf(x,y) dyg(x)=\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy. For any x,x′∈[a,b]x,x^{\prime}\in[a,b], ∣g(x)−g(x′)∣≤∫cd∣f(x,y)−f(x′,y)∣ dy≤(d−c)ω(∣x−x′∣)|g(x)-g(x^{\prime})|\le\int_c^d|f(x,y)-f(x^{\prime},y)|\,dy\le(d-c)\omega(|x-x^{\prime}|). Thus gg is continuous and its outer Riemann integral exists. This continuity step is needed before taking the outer-sum limit.

Choose any outer tagged partition with tags xi∗x_i^* and any inner tagged yy-partition of mesh at most δ\delta. At each fixed xi∗x_i^*, compare the inner sum with the integral over each yy-subinterval: their difference is bounded by its width times ω(δ)\omega(\delta), because the sampled and integrated values are at most δ\delta apart. Summing gives ∣Sy(xi∗)−g(xi∗)∣≤(d−c)ω(δ)|S_y(x_i^*)-g(x_i^*)|\le(d-c)\omega(\delta), uniformly in ii. Multiplying by outer widths and summing gives ∣SR−∑ig(xi∗)Δxi∣≤(b−a)(d−c)ω(δ)|S_{R}-\sum_i g(x_i^*)\Delta x_i|\le(b-a)(d-c)\omega(\delta). As both meshes tend to zero, the first sum tends to the double integral and the second to ∫abg(x) dx\int_a^b g(x)\,dx. Their limits therefore agree.

Interchange xx and yy in the same argument to obtain the other iterated order. The proof uses the full closed rectangle and continuity; on a nonrectangular region the bounds must describe the actual slices, and an unbounded signed integrand needs a separate convergence theorem. Finite regrouping alone would not justify either limiting passage.

Geometry determines the bounds

A vertically simple region has a description a≤x≤ba\le x\le b and g1(x)≤y≤g2(x)g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x). Its integral is ∫ab∫g1(x)g2(x)f(x,y) dy dx\int_a^b\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. Each vertical line meets the region in one interval, possibly collapsing at an endpoint. A horizontally simple description instead uses c≤y≤dc\le y\le d and h1(y)≤x≤h2(y)h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y), leading to integration in dx dydx\,dy. Curved bounds belong to the inner integral; the outer bounds describe the full projection on its coordinate axis.

To reverse order, first sketch or describe the inequalities. Find the new projection, then solve for the lower and upper intersection of a slice with the boundary. Do not merely swap dxdx and dydy while retaining the old bounds. If the active boundary changes as the outer variable moves, split the integral there. Regions with holes or multiple components often require several simple pieces. Add their integrals, avoiding overlap of positive area.

For the part of the unit square below y=x2y=x^2, vertical bounds are 0≤x≤10\le x\le1, 0≤y≤x20\le y\le x^2. At fixed 0≤y≤10\le y\le1, x≥0x\ge0 and y≤x2y\le x^2 give y≤x≤1\sqrt y\le x\le1. Thus ∫01∫0x2f(x,y) dy dx=∫01∫y1f(x,y) dx dy\int_0^1\int_0^{x^2}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1\int_{\sqrt y}^1f(x,y)\,dx\,dy. The point (3/4,1/4)(3/4,1/4) belongs to the region and rejects the incorrect x≤yx\le\sqrt y bound. The exterior point (2/5,3/10)(2/5,3/10) satisfies the incorrect x≥yx\ge y bound, but 3/10>(2/5)23/10>(2/5)^2, so it violates y≤x2y\le x^2. Changing order is useful when one antiderivative is unavailable or awkward. On 0≤x≤y≤10\le x\le y\le1, integrating ey2e^{y^2} first in yy has no elementary antiderivative. Using xx as the inner variable gives 0≤y≤10\le y\le1 and 0≤x≤y0\le x\le y. The xx-integral is simply yey2ye^{y^2}, whose yy-integral is (e−1)/2(e-1)/2. No approximation was needed; the geometry created a factor that makes substitution work.

Some regions are not simple in one direction without splitting. A disk with a central hole has horizontal slices that can consist of two disjoint intervals. One can split the integral into left and right pieces, subtract the inner disk from the outer disk, or use polar coordinates. Each approach describes the same domain but has different algebraic convenience. A single pair of lower and upper bounds that bridges the hole would integrate over points that do not belong to the region.

A region bounded by y=xy=x and y=x2y=x^2 for 0≤x≤10\le x\le1 has vertical bounds x2≤y≤xx^2\le y\le x. Reversing them requires solving x2≤yx^2\le y and y≤xy\le x: for fixed 0≤y≤10\le y\le1, y≤x≤yy\le x\le\sqrt y. The area can be computed either way: ∫01(x−x2) dx=1/6\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx=1/6 or ∫01(y−y) dy=1/6\int_0^1(\sqrt y-y)\,dy=1/6. Agreement of two independently derived setups is a strong check, but both could share a conceptual error if the region was misunderstood, so a sketch remains useful.

For triple integrals, a solid between z=u(x,y)z=u(x,y) and z=v(x,y)z=v(x,y) is easiest when zz is innermost. An order diagram can prevent dependency errors. In ∫ab∫g1(x)g2(x)∫u(x,y)v(x,y)f(x,y,z) dz dy dx\int_a^b\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}\int_{u(x,y)}^{v(x,y)}f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx, the zz limits may depend on xx and yy, the yy limits may depend on xx, and outer xx limits are constants. Mass, moments, and three-dimensional slices

For a sheet of nonnegative areal density ρ\rho, its mass is M=∬Dρ dAM=\iint_D\rho\,dA. The first moment about the y-axis is ∬Dxρ dA\iint_D x\rho\,dA, while the moment about the x-axis is ∬Dyρ dA\iint_D y\rho\,dA. The coordinate multiplying the density is the distance coordinate perpendicular to the named axis, with its sign. If 0<M<∞0<M<\infty and these moments are finite, the centre of mass is xˉ=(∬Dxρ dA)/M\bar x=(\iint_D x\rho\,dA)/M and yˉ=(∬Dyρ dA)/M\bar y=(\iint_D y\rho\,dA)/M. First moments have units kg m; dividing by mass returns a length.

The centroid is the centre of mass for uniform density. For a nonuniform sheet, a geometric symmetry only forces a coordinate of the centre of mass when the density shares that symmetry. Rotational inertia is a different moment: about the zz-axis through the origin it is Iz=∬D(x2+y2)ρ dAI_z=\iint_D(x^2+y^2)\rho\,dA, with units kg m2\mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm{m}^2. Squared distance weights material far from the axis more heavily and never introduces negative contributions.

A triple integral replaces patches by small boxes with volume ΔV=ΔxΔyΔz\Delta V=\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z. For a solid EE lying above a planar domain DD between surfaces z=u(x,y)z=u(x,y) and z=v(x,y)z=v(x,y), integrate zz first: ∭Ef dV=∬D[∫u(x,y)v(x,y)f(x,y,z) dz]dA\iiint_E f\,dV=\iint_D\left[\int_{u(x,y)}^{v(x,y)}f(x,y,z)\,dz\right]dA. For f=1f=1, the inner answer v−uv-u recovers the double-integral volume formula. Volumetric density has units kg/m3\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^3, so its triple integral gives mass.

Moments have units of mass times distance. With density in kg/m2\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^2 and coordinates in metres, MM is in kg\mathrm{kg} while ∬Dxρ dA\iint_D x\rho\,dA is in kg m\mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm m. Dividing gives a length. A reported center of mass outside the convex hull of a nonnegative-density sheet is impossible: each coordinate is a weighted average of coordinates in the region. This bound is a powerful diagnostic. It fails if the “density” takes negative values, because signed weights no longer model ordinary mass.

For a three-dimensional solid, mass is M=∭Eρ dVM=\iiint_E\rho\,dV and the xx coordinate of its center of mass is xˉ=1M∭Exρ dV\bar x=\dfrac1M\iiint_E x\rho\,dV when 0<M<∞0<M<\infty and the moment is finite. Moments of inertia use squared distances from an axis: for the zz-axis, Iz=∭E(x2+y2)ρ dVI_z=\iiint_E(x^2+y^2)\rho\,dV. These quantities answer different questions. A center of mass locates balance; moment of inertia measures resistance to rotational acceleration and has units kg m2\mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm{m}^2. The same integration machinery handles both once the physical factor in the integrand is identified.

Symmetry can remove work when both region and density respect it. If a lamina and its density are invariant under x↦−xx\mapsto-x, then the xx moment is zero because xρx\rho is odd, so xˉ=0\bar x=0 when the total mass is positive. If the region is symmetric but density is heavier on one side, the conclusion fails. Before using symmetry, check the entire integrand and the domain transformation. For a volume, analogous reflection arguments can set one or more center coordinates to zero without evaluating every triple integral.

A density that is nonnegative but zero on a large part of the region still yields a valid center of mass if total mass M>0M>0. If M=0M=0, division by MM is undefined; the “center” cannot be obtained from the formula. In probability, the analogous normalization requires total probability 11. This condition is easy to omit when manipulating symbols. State mass positivity before dividing and verify it numerically when a density is supplied as data rather than as a simple formula.

Singularities and numerical evidence

Unbounded density does not automatically imply infinite mass. On 0<x≤10<x\le1, 0≤y≤10\le y\le1, the density x−px^{-p} is nonnegative. The location and dimension of a singularity matter. The preceding example is singular along an entire edge. In the next chapter, polar coordinates show how the shrinking area around an isolated point changes the threshold. Always specify the domain, excluded set, and limiting procedure. For nonnegative functions, expanding truncated domains gives a well-defined accumulation, possibly infinite. For signed functions, cancellation can conceal infinite positive and negative contributions, as in the failed Fubini example.

A midpoint grid over a bounding rectangle is a practical approximation: test whether each midpoint lies inside the region, add its function value if it does, and multiply by the cell area. For a smooth function on a rectangle, midpoint quadrature has a familiar second-order refinement pattern. A curved region represented by an inclusion test introduces a discontinuity at its boundary, so the same rate should not be assumed. Boundary choices can cause irregular convergence even when the original function is smooth.

The lab uses a triangle and compares with an exact result. Deeper derivation · A double integral is weighted area, not just a nested symbol

Partition a planar region into small pieces of area ΔAi\Delta A_i. A double integral is the limit of sums ∑if(xi,yi)ΔAi\sum_i f(x_i,y_i)\Delta A_i when the pieces shrink suitably and the function is integrable. For f≥0f\ge0 this can represent volume under a surface, but signed values contribute signed volume. If ff is mass density per unit area, the same sum represents mass.   10. Problem 10. Interpretation Why can two iterated integrals disagree for a singular signed integrand?

    Hint. Check whether the integrand is absolutely integrable.

    Solution. Absolute integrability permits Fubini’s order exchange. A singular signed integrand can have nonintegrable positive and negative parts, so its two iterated orders can produce different cancellations. If two finite iterated values disagree, there is no finite Lebesgue double integral. A separately defined principal value uses a different convention.

    Hint. Draw a horizontal slice crossing the hole.

    Solution. Through the hole, a horizontal slice meets two disconnected pieces of the annulus. One continuous xx interval would include points outside the region.

    Hint. Consider a narrow peak missed by both grids.

    Solution. No. Similar midpoint estimates can both miss substantial mass or converge slowly near a singularity. Their difference is not a certified error bound; a guarantee needs verified assumptions and a valid bound.

Concept checks

  1. What area does each tile have in an n×nn\times n grid of the unit square?

    • 1/n1/n
    • 1/n21/n^2
    • n2n^2

    Answer. 1/n21/n^2. Each side is 1/n1/n, so the tile area is their product.

  2. When can order of integration safely reverse for a continuous function on a rectangle?

    • Never
    • Only when f≥0f\ge0

    Answer. Always under those conditions. Continuity on a compact rectangle meets the standard Fubini conditions.

Chapter synthesis

  • A multiple integral accumulates function values weighted by area or volume; units identify what is being accumulated.
  • Iterated integrals follow slices through the geometry. Reversing order requires new bounds and an appropriate Fubini or Tonelli hypothesis.
  • Density integrals give mass; coordinate-weighted integrals give first moments and centres of mass when the required quantities are finite.
  • Singularities require limits, and numerical refinement provides evidence whose reliability depends on both the integrand and the domain.

Common mistakes to check

  • “Reversing order means exchanging the differentials.” Bounds must also be reconstructed from the region; otherwise the new expression may describe a different set or contain invalid variable dependencies. Test with problem 11
  • “A finite grid result proves an improper integral converges.” On the strip 0<x≤10<x\le1, 0≤y≤10\le y\le1, the divergent integral of x−1x^{-1} has a finite midpoint sum at every finite resolution. Only a limiting argument establishes convergence. Test with problem 33
  • “The moment about the xx-axis uses xρx\rho.” It uses yρy\rho: yy is the perpendicular signed coordinate. The xx-coordinate of the centre of mass comes from the moment about the yy-axis. Test with problem 34
