Stationary points are candidates

An optimization problem begins with an objective and a feasible set. A manufacturer might minimize cost over possible dimensions, while a scientist might minimize a sum of squared measurement errors over model parameters. Write both ingredients before differentiating: minimize f(x,y)f(x,y) subject to (x,y)(x,y) belonging to KK. A local minimum compares nearby feasible points; an absolute minimum compares every point of KK. The distinction matters even when all the equations are easy to solve.

If ff is differentiable on an open neighborhood of an interior local extremum pp, then ∇f(p)=0\nabla f(p)=0. To see why, restrict ff to each coordinate line through pp. Each resulting one-variable function has a local extremum, so its derivative is zero. This necessary condition gives stationary points. We also inspect interior points where differentiability fails: the minimum of x2+y2\sqrt{x^2+y^2} occurs at the origin even though no gradient exists there. We use critical point to include stationary points and points where a needed derivative is undefined.

A vanishing gradient means that the first-order linear model is flat. It does not say that the surface is flat nearby, or that moving away raises the objective. The functions x2+y2x^2+y^2, −x2−y2-x^2-y^2, and x2−y2x^2-y^2 all have zero gradient at the origin, yet they describe a bowl, a dome, and a saddle. The third function increases along the x-axis and decreases along the y-axis. A saddle point has arbitrarily nearby points with values both above and below its own value.

The interior qualification is essential. On the square 0≤x,y≤10\le x,y\le1, the function x+yx+y has its minimum at (0,0)(0,0), where its gradient is (1,1)(1,1). No feasible direction from that corner decreases both coordinates. The correct derivative condition depends on which motions the domain permits. Solving ∇f=0\nabla f=0 over the plane and stopping would miss both absolute extrema of this simple constrained problem.

A critical point is an interior point where the gradient vanishes or a needed derivative does not exist. Thus ∣x∣+y2|x|+y^2 has a critical minimum at the origin although fxf_x is undefined. For a constrained region, partition the search into smooth interior, nonsmooth interior, smooth boundary, singular boundary, and corners. The necessary stationary equation applies only in the smooth interior.

A stationary equation can have many solutions. For f(x,y)=x2y−y3/3f(x,y)=x^2y-y^3/3, the equations 2xy=02xy=0 and x2−y2=0x^2-y^2=0 meet only at the origin, but the local shape still needs classification. Along y=0y=0, f=0f=0; along y=xy=x, f=2x3/3f=2x^3/3 takes both signs on either side. The origin is not an extremum. Solving equations locates candidates; testing the objective on nearby feasible points decides what they mean. A contour plot can suggest promising test directions but cannot by itself certify every nearby value.

Optimization language distinguishes local from absolute. A local minimum at pp compares f(p)f(p) only with feasible points in some neighborhood of pp; an absolute minimum compares it with every feasible point. For f(x)=x4−2x2f(x)=x^4-2x^2 on the whole real line, zero is a local maximum while ±1\pm1 are absolute minima. In several variables a stationary point can be a local minimum but not the lowest among distant regions. State the comparison set and whether boundary points are included before interpreting any derivative condition.

The feasible set can change the answer even when the formula does not. Minimize x2+y2x^2+y^2 over all of R2\mathbb R^2 and the origin is the unique solution. Restrict to x+y≥1x+y\ge1 and the nearest feasible point becomes (1/2,1/2)(1/2,1/2) on the boundary, where the unconstrained gradient is (1,1)(1,1), not zero. The active constraint blocks motion toward the origin. This is the geometry behind multiplier and inequality-constraint methods: at an optimum, permissible first-order directions must not decrease the objective.

An explicit existence check prevents wasted algebra. The function f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2 on R2\mathbb R^2 has a minimum but no maximum because it grows without bound. On the open unit disk, it has a minimum at zero and supremum one but no maximum. On the closed unit disk, both are attained. These three problems use the same formula and different sets. Before finding critical points, ask whether an extremum is guaranteed, merely possible, or ruled out by unboundedness or an excluded boundary.

One can use a directional test before calculating a full Hessian. If a feasible line through pp has values both above and below f(p)f(p) arbitrarily close to pp, then p is not a local extremum. For x2−y2x^2-y^2 the coordinate axes give exactly such a contradiction. If every tested direction rises, the result remains inconclusive: a curved route may still fall. The function in chapter 20 with a hidden curved-path limit is a reminder that finite families of straight lines do not exhaust a neighborhood. Positive-definite Hessian or a global inequality is the kind of uniform evidence that can turn favorable directional observations into a proof.

The Hessian measures quadratic shape

Assume ff has continuous second partial derivatives near a stationary point pp. Its Hessian is the symmetric matrix HH with rows (fxx,fxy)(f_{xx},f_{xy}) and (fyx,fyy)(f_{yx},f_{yy}), evaluated at pp. The second-order Taylor model is f(p+h)=f(p)+12hTHh+o(∥h∥2)f(p+h)=f(p)+\frac12 h^{\mathsf T}Hh+o(\lVert h\rVert^2). Here the remainder divided by ∥h∥2\lVert h\rVert^2 tends to zero. Thus a strictly positive quadratic term in every nonzero direction dominates the remainder close enough to pp and produces a strict local minimum.

For two variables put A=fxx(p)A=f_{xx}(p), B=fxy(p)B=f_{xy}(p), C=fyy(p)C=f_{yy}(p), and D=AC−B2D=AC-B^2. At a stationary point, D>0,A>0D>0,A>0 gives a strict local minimum, D>0,A<0D>0,A<0 gives a strict local maximum, and D<0D<0 gives a saddle. The theorem proof below completes the square and uses the o(∥h∥2)o(\|h\|^2) remainder; the determinant sign by itself is not a classification when D>0D>0.

When D=0D=0, the Hessian test gives no classification. At the origin x4+y4x^4+y^4 has a strict minimum, −x4−y4-x^4-y^4 has a strict maximum, and x4−y4x^4-y^4 has a saddle, although all three Hessians are zero. Direct inequalities or higher-order terms settle these examples. Checking a few straight lines can disprove a minimum by finding a descending direction, but agreement along finitely many lines does not establish a minimum over a neighborhood.

In more variables the corresponding condition is positive definiteness of HH for a strict minimum and negative definiteness for a strict maximum. A real symmetric Hessian is positive definite exactly when all its eigenvalues are positive. Mixed positive and negative eigenvalues imply a saddle. A zero eigenvalue leaves some directions unresolved by the quadratic approximation; it does not automatically imply that the point is a saddle or that there is a whole curve of minima.

A Hessian test is local and requires a stationary base point. At a nonstationary point, the linear term usually dominates the quadratic form for small steps, so the sign of the Hessian cannot classify an extremum there. For f=x+x2+y2f=x+x^2+y^2 at zero, the Hessian is positive definite, but the gradient is (1,0)(1,0) and negative x steps decrease ff. The actual minimum is at (−1/2,0)(-1/2,0). Always solve the first-order equations and check the point's feasibility before using second-order classification.

When D=0D=0, higher-order behavior can vary even if one quadratic direction is positive. For f=x2+y4f=x^2+y^4 at zero, the Hessian is diag⁡(2,0)\operatorname{diag}(2,0) and the point is a strict minimum. For g=x2−y4g=x^2-y^4, the same Hessian occurs, yet values along x=0x=0 are negative and along y=0y=0 positive, so zero is a saddle. This pair isolates the inconclusive direction: the second derivative in yy is zero, and fourth-order terms decide the sign there. An inconclusive test is a request for another argument, not a classification.

Positive definiteness can be interpreted through directional curvature. At a stationary point, the second derivative of f(p+tu)f(p+tu) at t=0t=0 is uTHuu^{\mathsf T}Hu for a unit vector uu. If this is positive for every direction, the smooth quadratic model rises away from the point. The smallest eigenvalue gives the weakest quadratic curvature; if it is very small, the function may look almost flat in one direction. This affects numerical optimization because steps along weak-curvature directions can converge slowly even when a strict minimum is mathematically clear.

Second-order Taylor's remainder is essential to a strict classification. A positive-definite quadratic term dominates an o(r2)o(r^2) remainder for sufficiently small rr, because the quadratic form is bounded below by a positive constant times r2r^2. Without a controlled remainder, the sign of second derivatives at the point alone may not describe neighboring values. Continuous second partial derivatives near the point provide the usual sufficient setting. The resulting conclusion is local; other feasible points may still have lower values.

The determinant D=AC−B2D=AC-B^2 is unchanged by rotating orthonormal coordinates because it is the product of Hessian eigenvalues. The signs of the eigenvalues describe curvature along principal directions. When D<0D<0, one eigenvalue is positive and one negative, so a saddle is unavoidable at a stationary point under the Taylor assumptions. When D>0D>0, both eigenvalues have the same sign; AA then identifies that sign. This spectral view explains the compact two-variable test and prepares the higher-dimensional version, where checking one determinant alone is insufficient to prove positive definiteness.

Absolute extrema require the whole domain

The extreme value theorem extends naturally to several variables: a continuous real-valued function on a nonempty compact set attains an absolute minimum and an absolute maximum. In Euclidean space, compact means closed and bounded. This theorem guarantees that winning candidates exist; it does not identify them. On an open disk a continuous function may approach its best value only at an excluded boundary point. On an unbounded closed set it may decrease without bound.

For a compact region with a manageable piecewise smooth boundary, first find all interior critical points. Next restrict ff to each smooth boundary piece using a parametrization, and optimize the resulting one-variable function. Include endpoints of the parameter interval, corners, intersections, and any points where the parametrization or constraint is singular. Evaluate ff at every candidate and compare the values. A boundary point may be a constrained minimum while failing to be a minimum in the surrounding plane.

This candidate method is a framework rather than a promise of finitely many equations. A constant function has infinitely many stationary points, and an entire boundary arc can share one value. In such cases describe the candidate family and its objective values instead of forcing a finite list. Derivatives also do not remove the need to check domain restrictions inherited from logarithms, denominators, or physical constraints.

Units help audit the model. If xx and yy represent lengths in meters and ff is cost in pounds, fxf_x has units pounds per meter. Bounds such as x≥0x\ge0 represent feasible dimensions, not optional algebraic decorations. A numerical optimizer that reports a cheaper negative length has solved a different problem. Finally, a maximum and minimum of the mathematical model need not be useful engineering designs if the model excludes material limits or measurement uncertainty.

For a rectangle [a,b]×[c,d][a,b]\times[c,d], the boundary consists of four edges. Restricting ff to x=ax=a, x=bx=b, y=cy=c, and y=dy=d produces four one-variable problems. Endpoints belong to two edges, but it is harmless to compare them twice. If f is differentiable in the interior, solve ∇f=0\nabla f=0 there; if not, include interior nonsmooth points. A systematic table of candidate coordinates and objective values is more reliable than trying to guess which corner or stationary point wins from a plot.

For a disk, parametrizing the circle x=Rcos⁡θx=R\cos\theta, y=Rsin⁡θy=R\sin\theta converts boundary optimization into a periodic one-variable problem. The interval [0,2π][0,2\pi] includes the same point at both ends, so endpoint bookkeeping is easy, but every critical angle still matters. Alternatively a multiplier equation can locate regular boundary candidates. The best method depends on the objective: a linear objective is simplest by Cauchy–Schwarz, while a polynomial on a circle may be easiest after substitution. The existence theorem supplies attainment, not the candidates themselves.

A noncompact domain can still have extrema; compactness is sufficient, not necessary. For f=x2+y2f=x^2+y^2 on R2\mathbb R^2, the origin is a global minimum because a sum of squares is nonnegative, despite the unbounded domain. Conversely, a continuous function on a bounded but open domain need not attain a maximum. Avoid the mistaken converse “not compact means no extrema.” Direct inequalities, coercivity, or limiting behavior can establish existence in particular noncompact problems.

When the boundary has a corner, a smooth-boundary multiplier equation may not apply there. Consider f=x+yf=x+y on the square [0,1]2[0,1]^2. Along each open edge, the restricted derivative is nonzero; all extrema occur at corners. A routine that checks only interior stationary points and smooth Lagrange candidates would return nothing. In higher dimensions, edges and faces form a hierarchy: optimize in each stratum and compare shared boundaries. This is why feasible geometry must be drawn or described before the calculus begins.

An absolute-extrema report should give both values and locations. Saying “maximum 2\sqrt2” omits where it occurs; saying “at (1/2,1/2)(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)” omits the objective value and whether other points tie. If a constant function is optimized, every feasible point is both a maximizer and minimizer. If the feasible set has disconnected pieces, each piece may supply separate candidates. A clear final comparison records all ties and indicates whether the extrema are attained or only approached as suprema and infima.

Lagrange multipliers and feasible directions

Suppose the feasible boundary is a smooth level curve g(x,y)=cg(x,y)=c. At a point where ∇g≠0\nabla g\ne0, tangent directions are perpendicular to ∇g\nabla g. If ff has a constrained local extremum there, its derivative along every tangent direction vanishes. Therefore ∇f\nabla f is also perpendicular to the tangent line, so ∇f=λ∇g\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g for some scalar λ\lambda. This geometric argument explains why gradients are parallel and why the constraint equation must accompany the derivative equations.

The standard necessary-condition theorem assumes ff and gg are continuously differentiable near the point and ∇g\nabla g is nonzero there. Solve fx=λgxf_x=\lambda g_x, fy=λgyf_y=\lambda g_y, and g=cg=c, then classify or compare the feasible candidates. These equations alone do not distinguish maxima, minima, and stationary behavior along the curve. Dividing by xx, yy, or λ\lambda can silently discard valid zero cases, so separate those cases before division.

Regularity is a real restriction. Let g=x2+y2g=x^2+y^2 and constrain g=0g=0. The feasible set is the single point (0,0)(0,0), which minimizes and maximizes f=xf=x over that set. Yet ∇f=(1,0)\nabla f=(1,0) cannot equal λ∇g=(0,0)\lambda\nabla g=(0,0). The multiplier equation misses this extremum because the constraint gradient vanishes. Always inspect feasible points where regularity fails separately. With several equality constraints, their gradients must be linearly independent for the usual multiplier theorem.

A multiplier can also describe sensitivity. If a smooth family of constrained optimizers p(c)p(c) exists and ∇f=λ∇g\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g, differentiating g(p(c))=cg(p(c))=c gives ∇g⋅p′(c)=1\nabla g\cdot p^{\prime}(c)=1. Consequently ddcf(p(c))=λ\frac{d}{dc}f(p(c))=\lambda. This interpretation requires that differentiable optimizer branch; switches between competing optima can create a corner in the optimal-value function. The units of λ\lambda are objective units divided by constraint units, and its sign depends on the chosen convention for the multiplier equation.

The multiplier equation says the objective gradient has no component tangent to a regular constraint curve. It does not say the gradients must point the same way: λ\lambda may be negative. For f=x+yf=x+y on the unit circle, the maximum and minimum occur at opposite points; the corresponding λ\lambda signs differ. The equation is necessary at a regular constrained extremum, not sufficient. A solution can be a constrained maximum, minimum, or saddle relative to the feasible set, so objective values or second-order feasible tests are still needed.

A regular multiplier candidate need not be an extremum. On g(x,y)=x2+y2=1g(x,y)=x^2+y^2=1, take f(x,y)=x3f(x,y)=x^3. At (0,1)(0,1), ∇g=(0,2)≠0\nabla g=(0,2)\ne0 and ∇f=(0,0)=0∇g\nabla f=(0,0)=0\nabla g, so the multiplier equations hold. Nearby feasible points (t,1−t2)(t,\sqrt{1-t^2}) have values t3t^3, positive for t>0t>0 and negative for t<0t<0. Thus this candidate is neither a constrained maximum nor minimum. Classify candidates by feasible motion or a justified constrained second-order test.

A simple ellipse problem shows the geometry. Maximize x+yx+y subject to x2+4y2=4x^2+4y^2=4. The equations (1,1)=λ(2x,8y)(1,1)=\lambda(2x,8y) give x=4yx=4y when λ≠0\lambda\ne0. The constraint then gives 16y2+4y2=416y^2+4y^2=4, so (x,y)=±(4/5,1/5)(x,y)=\pm(4/\sqrt5,1/\sqrt5). The objective is ±5\pm\sqrt5. Because the ellipse is compact and these are all regular boundary candidates, the positive point is the absolute maximum and the negative point the minimum. Directly parametrizing the ellipse gives the same result.

At singular feasible points and corners, inspect feasible motion directly; one smooth constraint normal may not describe every blocked direction. The singleton g=x2+y2=0g=x^2+y^2=0 in paragraph 3 is the simplest case. Normal-cone and inequality-multiplier methods are optional later extensions of this geometric idea.

The numerical value of λ\lambda depends on how the constraint is written. Replacing g=cg=c by 2g=2c2g=2c doubles ∇g\nabla g, so the multiplier in ∇f=λ∇g\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g is halved. The underlying feasible set and optimizer do not change. Its sensitivity interpretation also changes consistently because the right-hand parameter has been rescaled. This is why a reported multiplier should be paired with its exact constraint equation and units. It is not an intrinsic property of the point alone.

For an inequality g(x,y)≤cg(x,y)\le c, an optimum may lie in the interior where the constraint is inactive, or on the boundary where it is active. A plain equality multiplier search on g=cg=c examines only the latter. To solve a simple bounded-region problem, check interior stationary points first, then each active boundary. At an active boundary, feasible directions point to one side, so the sign of λ\lambda can carry information; the full Karush–Kuhn–Tucker theorem formalizes this under constraint qualifications. Here direct candidate comparison is often clearer and safer.

A numerical optimizer may report a point outside the feasible region because of roundoff, loose constraint tolerances, or a mismatch between units. A candidate only has meaning after feasibility is checked. For x2+4y2=4x^2+4y^2=4, a reported point (1.8,0.5)(1.8,0.5) gives 1.82+4(0.5)2=4.241.8^2+4(0.5)^2=4.24, so it is not on the ellipse even though it looks close in a plot. Projecting onto the constraint changes the point and objective value. Exact symbolic candidates, when available, give a useful reference for choosing tolerances and interpreting the numerical result.

For unconstrained minimization, gradient descent repeats pk+1=pk−α∇f(pk)p_{k+1}=p_k-\alpha\nabla f(p_k), where α>0\alpha>0 is a step size. Deeper derivation · The Hessian describes shape; constraints decide the answer

At an interior critical point of a twice differentiable function F(x,y)F(x,y), the Hessian records second-order variation. For F=x2+4y2F=x^2+4y^2, the Hessian is diag⁡(2,8)\operatorname{diag}(2,8), positive definite, so the origin is a strict local minimum. In fact F≥0F\ge0 everywhere and equals zero only at the origin, making it a global minimum. For G=x2−y2G=x^2-y^2, the Hessian has one positive and one negative eigenvalue, and the origin is a saddle: GG rises along the x-axis and falls along the y-axis.

A zero Hessian determinant is inconclusive. H=x4+y4H=x^4+y^4 has a strict minimum at the origin despite every second derivative there vanishing. K=x4−y4K=x^4-y^4 has a saddle with the same zero Hessian. Higher-order terms or direct inequalities are then needed. Numerical contour plots can suggest a shape, but a proof checks all sufficiently nearby directions.

On a closed bounded feasible region, global extrema may lie on the boundary even when interior critical points exist. Parameterize each smooth boundary piece, check its endpoints and corners, and compare all candidates. Lagrange multipliers can find constrained candidates where a smooth constraint has nonzero gradient, but the multiplier equation is a necessary condition under its hypotheses, not a guarantee of a maximum.   let [x, y] = start;

    y -= alpha * kappa * y;
    path.push([x, y]);
