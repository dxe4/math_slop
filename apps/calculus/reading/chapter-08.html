Existence comes before calculation

A graph can suggest a highest point without proving that one exists. An absolute maximum of ff on a domain DD is a value f(c)f(c) at a point c∈Dc\in D such that f(x)≤f(c)f(x)\le f(c) for every x∈Dx\in D. An absolute minimum reverses the inequality. A local maximum only compares nearby domain points: there is some positive distance within which no value exceeds f(c)f(c). Local describes the comparison region, not the size of the value.

The extreme value theorem says that a real-valued function continuous on a closed, bounded interval [a,b][a,b] attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there. Attains means the value actually occurs. The theorem provides existence, not a formula or uniqueness. Constant functions attain both extrema at every point. Continuity at an endpoint means continuity from within the interval; differentiability is unnecessary.

Each hypothesis matters. The continuous function f(x)=xf(x)=x on (0,1)(0,1) has neither absolute extremum, although its values are bounded. The endpoint values are missing. On [0,∞)[0,\infty), f(x)=xf(x)=x has a minimum but no maximum. On [0,1][0,1], define f(x)=xf(x)=x for x<1x<1 and f(1)=0f(1)=0. This function is bounded but does not attain its supremum 11 because continuity fails. Failure of a hypothesis does not prove that extrema are absent; it means this theorem no longer guarantees them.

Fermat’s stationary-point theorem supplies a different kind of information: if cc is an interior local extremum and ff is differentiable at cc, then f′(c)=0f^{\prime}(c)=0. At a local maximum, the difference quotients from the right are nonpositive and those from the left are nonnegative. If their common limit exists, it must be zero. A local minimum gives the reversed signs. This argument explains why the interior and differentiability requirements cannot be omitted.

Here a critical number means an interior domain point where f′f^{\prime} is zero or does not exist. Thus ∣x∣|x| has a critical number at zero even though its derivative is undefined there. A point outside the domain is never a critical number. On a closed interval, list interior critical numbers and endpoints separately when seeking absolute extrema. Some conventions count endpoints as critical; the separate endpoint check avoids any dependence on terminology.

The closed-interval condition can be understood through sequences. First the values are bounded: otherwise we could choose xn∈[a,b]x_n\in[a,b] with ∣f(xn)∣>n|f(x_n)|>n; compactness gives a convergent subsequence, contradicting continuity at its limit. Let MM be the supremum of the values, and choose points xnx_n whose values approach MM. Compactness of the interval gives a subsequence of those points converging to some c∈[a,b]c\in[a,b]. Continuity then gives f(c)=Mf(c)=M, so the supremum is attained. The same reasoning handles the infimum. This is a proof sketch using a compactness fact developed more fully in analysis; it explains why both boundedness and inclusion of the limiting endpoint matter. Removing an endpoint can let the maximizing points approach a location outside the domain.

The candidate method for an absolute extremum is a consequence of two theorems, not a mnemonic. The extreme value theorem supplies an actual winning point on a closed interval. If the winning point cc lies inside and the derivative exists, Fermat's theorem forces f′(c)=0f^{\prime}(c)=0. If the derivative does not exist, the point is still a candidate. Otherwise the winner is an endpoint. Thus checking endpoints and all interior critical points is exhaustive when the set can be completely found. A list of several numerical stationary points is not a proof that no others exist; an exact factorization or rigorous root isolation may be needed.

An endpoint can be an absolute maximum while its ordinary two-sided derivative is irrelevant. On [0,2][0,2], f(x)=xf(x)=x attains its minimum at zero and maximum at two, despite derivative one at every interior point. The extreme value theorem includes endpoints; Fermat's theorem does not. An interior corner can similarly be an extremum without a derivative, as ∣x∣|x| at zero demonstrates. This is why a solution that sets f′=0f^{\prime}=0 and stops is incomplete even when every calculation on that list is correct.

Rolle’s theorem and the mean value theorem

Rolle’s theorem states: if ff is continuous on [a,b][a,b], differentiable on (a,b)(a,b), a<ba<b, and f(a)=f(b)f(a)=f(b), there is a c∈(a,b)c\in(a,b) with f′(c)=0f^{\prime}(c)=0. Proof sketch: the extreme value theorem supplies an absolute maximum and minimum. If ff is constant, every interior point works. Otherwise some value differs from the common endpoint value, so at least one absolute extremum occurs inside. Fermat’s theorem then gives a zero derivative there.

The mean value theorem replaces equal endpoint heights with an arbitrary secant slope. For a<ba<b, under continuity on [a,b][a,b] and differentiability on (a,b)(a,b), there is an interior cc for which f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−af^{\prime}(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. To prove it, subtract the secant line: h(x)=f(x)−f(a)−m(x−a)h(x)=f(x)-f(a)-m(x-a), with mm the displayed average slope. Then h(a)=h(b)=0h(a)=h(b)=0, so Rolle gives h′(c)=0h^{\prime}(c)=0 and hence f′(c)=mf^{\prime}(c)=m. This is an existence statement: there can be several suitable points, and the theorem does not locate them.

If position s(t)s(t) is measured in metres and time tt in seconds, the secant slope and derivative both have units metres per second. If ss is continuous on a 10 s10\,\mathrm s closed interval, differentiable in its interior, and has net displacement +120 m+120\,\mathrm m, its instantaneous velocity is 12 m/s12\,\mathrm{m/s} somewhere between the endpoints. This concerns signed displacement and velocity; replacing displacement by total distance changes the interpretation. Corners in a position model must be checked before applying the theorem.

The hypotheses also explain a familiar counterexample. For f(x)=∣x∣f(x)=|x| on [−1,1][-1,1], the average slope is zero but the derivative is either −1-1 or 11 wherever it exists. No interior derivative is zero. The function is continuous, but differentiability fails at the corner. A sketch cannot repair a missing hypothesis.

A useful consequence is a bound on change. If ff satisfies the theorem’s hypotheses between xx and yy and ∣f′(t)∣≤M|f^{\prime}(t)|\le M throughout the intervening interior, then ∣f(y)−f(x)∣≤M∣y−x∣|f(y)-f(x)|\le M|y-x|. This makes a uniform sensitivity bound rigorous. If f′f^{\prime} is zero everywhere on an interval, applying the mean value theorem to any two points proves that ff is constant there. An interval is essential: a function may have derivative zero on two disconnected pieces while taking different constants on the pieces.

The mean value point need not be unique. For f(x)=sin⁡xf(x)=\sin x on [0,2π][0,2\pi], the secant slope is 00. The derivative cos⁡x\cos x vanishes at both x=π/2x=\pi/2 and x=3π/2x=3\pi/2, so there are two suitable interior points. For f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 on [1,3][1,3], the secant slope is 44 and f′(x)=2xf^{\prime}(x)=2x, so the point is uniquely c=2c=2. The theorem asserts at least one, not a prescribed number. A graph of parallel secant and tangent lines can suggest locations, but solving f′(c)f^{\prime}(c) equal to the average slope identifies them exactly when possible.

The derivative bound consequence can be applied on every subinterval, not just the original endpoints. If ∣f′∣≤M|f^{\prime}|\le M on an interval, then for any u<vu<v inside it, ∣f(v)−f(u)∣≤M(v−u)|f(v)-f(u)|\le M(v-u). This is a Lipschitz estimate: nearby inputs cannot produce output differences faster than the stated rate. A derivative bound is sufficient, not necessary; a function may be Lipschitz even if it has a corner, as ∣x∣|x| does. To use the mean value theorem directly at a corner, divide into smooth pieces or use a separate inequality.

A zero derivative throughout a connected interval implies constancy by the mean value theorem. It is not enough to know the derivative is zero at infinitely many isolated points. The function f(x)=x2sin⁡(1/x)f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x) for x≠0x\ne0, extended by zero at the origin, can have many stationary points while not being constant. Nor can constants on disconnected pieces be forced to agree: define zero for x<0x<0 and one for x>0x>0 on the domain R∖{0}\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}; its derivative is zero on both components. The interval hypothesis bridges every pair of points to which the theorem is applied.

On [−1,1][-1,1], compare f(x)=∣x∣−x2+μxf(x)=|x|-x^2+\mu x with a function that jumps at zero. The curved corner is continuous, but its left and right derivatives at zero are μ−1\mu-1 and μ+1\mu+1, so the mean value theorem cannot be invoked. Its endpoint secant slope is μ\mu; nevertheless, f′(−1/2)=f′(1/2)=μf^{\prime}(-1/2)=f^{\prime}(1/2)=\mu. A failed hypothesis removes the theorem’s guarantee, not every possible matching point. The two matches come from solving the derivative equations on their proper sides, not from the sketch.

Now define g(x)=(1+μ)xg(x)=(1+\mu)x for x<0x<0 and g(x)=(1+μ)x−2g(x)=(1+\mu)x-2 for x≥0x\ge0. Its secant slope on [−1,1][-1,1] is also μ\mu, but g(0)=−2g(0)=-2 while values from the left approach 00. Away from zero its derivative is 1+μ1+\mu, so no eligible interior point matches the secant; at zero no derivative exists. Rolle’s theorem leads to the mean value theorem

If a<ba<b, and ff is continuous on [a,b][a,b] and differentiable on (a,b)(a,b), there is some c∈(a,b)c\in(a,b) with f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−af^{\prime}(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.

f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−afor some a<c<bf^{\prime}(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\quad\text{for some }a<c<b
Reasoning and proof

Begin with Rolle’s special case f(a)=f(b)f(a)=f(b). The extreme value theorem gives an absolute maximum and minimum on the closed interval. If ff is constant, every interior derivative is zero. Otherwise at least one absolute extremum must occur at an interior point: the equal endpoint value cannot be both the unique high and unique low value when the function varies. At an interior differentiable extremum, positive and negative difference quotients have opposite sign restrictions, so their common limit can only be zero. This is Fermat’s stationary-point theorem.

For a general ff, subtract the secant line: g(x)=f(x)−[f(a)+(f(b)−f(a))(x−a)/(b−a)]g(x)=f(x)-[f(a)+(f(b)-f(a))(x-a)/(b-a)]. The new function inherits continuity and interior differentiability, and g(a)=g(b)=0g(a)=g(b)=0. Rolle’s theorem supplies cc with g′(c)=0g^{\prime}(c)=0. Differentiating the explicit line yields f′(c)−[f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a)=0f^{\prime}(c)-[f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a)=0, which is the desired equality. The theorem locates at least one instantaneous slope equal to the average slope but normally does not locate cc itself.

Both hypotheses protect the conclusion. A jump can break continuity, while ∣x∣|x| on [−1,1][-1,1] breaks differentiability at its corner despite equal endpoint values. On a disconnected domain there may be no uninterrupted interval to which the theorem applies. When using the result to bound change, state the closed interval and verify the derivative bound throughout its interior before multiplying by its length.

From signs to turning points

Suppose ff is differentiable on an interval. If f′>0f^{\prime}>0 throughout it, the mean value theorem applied between any x<yx<y proves f(y)>f(x)f(y)>f(x), so ff is strictly increasing. Negative derivative gives strict decrease. The weaker condition f′≥0f^{\prime}\ge0 guarantees nondecrease. A strictly increasing function need not have positive derivative at every point: x3x^3 is strictly increasing on the real line but has derivative zero at zero.

To construct a sign chart, find the domain, locate all critical numbers, and split the domain at those numbers and at missing domain points. Determine the sign of f′f^{\prime} on each resulting open interval. Factoring often gives an exact sign argument. Testing one point is justified only after establishing that the sign cannot change inside that interval; a continuous nonzero derivative cannot change sign by the intermediate value theorem. A disconnected domain must be treated piece by piece.

The first derivative test assumes ff is continuous at cc and differentiable on each side sufficiently near cc. A change of f′f^{\prime} from positive to negative gives a strict local maximum; negative to positive gives a strict local minimum. If the derivative keeps the same strict sign across cc, there is no local extremum there. These conclusions follow from the increasing and decreasing behavior on the two sides. They also work at corners where f′(c)f^{\prime}(c) is undefined.

Stationarity alone does not classify a point: x2x^2, −x2-x^2, and x3x^3 all have zero derivative at 00 but exhibit a minimum, maximum, and neither, respectively. For an absolute extremum on a closed interval, compare function values at every interior critical number and at both endpoints. The existence theorem makes this procedure conclusive when the candidate list is complete. It may be inconvenient or infinite for complicated functions, so finding a few numerical roots of f′f^{\prime} is not automatically a complete search.

For f(x)=x3−3xf(x)=x^3-3x, the derivative is f′(x)=3(x−1)(x+1)f^{\prime}(x)=3(x-1)(x+1). It is positive on (−∞,−1)(-\infty,-1) and (1,∞)(1,\infty), and negative on (−1,1)(-1,1). The first derivative test therefore gives a local maximum at x=−1x=-1 with value 22, and a local minimum at x=1x=1 with value −2-2. On a closed interval such as [−2,2][-2,2], endpoint values must still be compared: f(−2)=−2f(-2)=-2 and f(2)=2f(2)=2. Both an endpoint and an interior critical point can share an absolute extreme value.

The sign of the derivative tells how values compare within an interval, but it does not by itself compare values across a domain gap. For f(x)=1/xf(x)=1/x, the derivative is f′(x)=−1/x2<0f^{\prime}(x)=-1/x^2<0 on each of (−∞,0)(-\infty,0) and (0,∞)(0,\infty). It is decreasing on each component. It is not decreasing on the entire disconnected domain under the usual pairwise definition, because −1<1-1<1 while f(−1)=−1<f(1)=1f(-1)=-1<f(1)=1. A sign chart must keep excluded inputs visible; treating two branches as one interval can produce a false global statement.

The converse of the strict sign test is false at individual points. The strictly increasing function f(x)=x3f(x)=x^3 has derivative zero at zero. More generally, a function can increase throughout an interval even if its derivative vanishes on a set of points; the mean value theorem gives a sufficient test when the derivative is positive everywhere but not an equivalence point by point. To classify a candidate where f′f^{\prime} is zero, examine sign on neighboring intervals or another valid criterion. The number zero in a derivative table is a location for investigation, not a verdict.

Curvature, concavity, and an inconclusive zero

Concavity describes how slopes change. On an interval where f′f^{\prime} is increasing, the curve is concave up; where f′f^{\prime} is decreasing, it is concave down. Equivalently, for a differentiable concave-up function the graph lies above its tangent lines. When ff is twice differentiable, f′′>0f^{\prime\prime}>0 guarantees concave up and f′′<0f^{\prime\prime}<0 guarantees concave down. A decreasing function can be concave up: e−xe^{-x} has negative first derivative and positive second derivative.

An inflection point is a point on a continuous graph where concavity changes across the point. Zeros of f′′f^{\prime\prime} and points where f′′f^{\prime\prime} does not exist are candidates, not automatic inflections. For f(x)=x4f(x)=x^4, f′′(0)=0f^{\prime\prime}(0)=0 but f′′(x)=12x2f^{\prime\prime}(x)=12x^2 stays positive on both sides, so there is no change of concavity. For f(x)=x3f(x)=x^3, f′′(x)=6xf^{\prime\prime}(x)=6x changes sign at zero, which is an inflection. A vertical asymptote is not an inflection point because there is no graph point there.

One convenient second derivative test assumes f′(c)=0f^{\prime}(c)=0 and f′′f^{\prime\prime} is continuous near cc. If f′′(c)>0f^{\prime\prime}(c)>0, continuity makes f′′f^{\prime\prime} positive nearby, so f′f^{\prime} increases through zero and the first derivative test yields a strict local minimum. If f′′(c)<0f^{\prime\prime}(c)<0, it gives a strict local maximum. These are sufficient hypotheses chosen to make the reasoning transparent. If f′′(c)=0f^{\prime\prime}(c)=0, this test says nothing: x4x^4, −x4-x^4, and x3x^3 again demonstrate the three possibilities.

In a position model, a positive second derivative means velocity is increasing. It does not always mean speed is increasing: negative velocity becoming less negative describes slowing motion. Keep both signs and units visible. If position has units metres, its first derivative has units metres per second and its second derivative metres per second squared. For purely mathematical graphs in this chapter, x and f(x) are dimensionless unless units are specified.

Concavity can be tested through slopes of secants as well as through a second derivative. For a concave-up function on an interval, secant slopes increase as the interval slides right; a tangent line, where it exists, lies below the graph. If f′′≥0f^{\prime\prime}\ge0 throughout an interval and f′f^{\prime} is continuous with appropriate differentiability, the mean value theorem applied to f′f^{\prime} makes f′f^{\prime} nondecreasing. The endpoint signs of f′f^{\prime} alone do not establish concavity; a derivative can rise and fall between the endpoints. A second-derivative sign chart needs the same care about intervals and excluded points as a first-derivative chart.

A zero second derivative does not automatically mark an inflection. At the origin, x4x^4 has f′′(x)=12x2≥0f^{\prime\prime}(x)=12x^2\ge0 on both sides and remains concave up. The function x3x^3 has f′′(x)=6xf^{\prime\prime}(x)=6x, changing from negative to positive, so the origin is an inflection. A function can also change concavity where f′′f^{\prime\prime} fails to exist. The definition asks for a change in concavity across a graph point; the equation f′′=0f^{\prime\prime}=0 merely supplies possible locations when a second derivative is available.

The second derivative test is local. If f′(c)=0f^{\prime}(c)=0 and f′′(c)>0f^{\prime\prime}(c)>0 with enough regularity, cc is a strict local minimum. It need not be the absolute minimum on a larger allowed domain: another valley or an endpoint can have a lower value. Conversely, a global minimum can occur at an endpoint where the second derivative test does not apply. Separate the tasks of classifying a local shape and comparing actual values across the complete domain. This distinction will matter again in multivariable optimization.

L’Hôpital’s rule is a theorem about limits

Sometimes a quotient approaches the indeterminate form 0/00/0 or ∞/∞\infty/\infty. These forms do not specify an answer: x/xx/x, x2/xx^2/x, and x/x2x/x^2 near zero behave differently. L’Hôpital’s rule supplies an answer under additional assumptions. A useful one-sided version considers an open interval approaching an endpoint aa, which may be finite or infinite. Suppose ff and gg are differentiable on that interval, gg and g′g^{\prime} are nonzero there, and either ff and gg both tend to zero or both tend in magnitude to infinity. If f′/g′f^{\prime}/g^{\prime} tends to LL, finite or infinite, then f/gf/g tends to LL. Shrinking the interval is allowed.

For a two-sided limit at a finite point, verify the rule on both sides and check that the resulting limits agree. The functions need not be defined at the limiting point. Crucially, differentiating numerator and denominator separately is not the quotient rule: it is a special limit theorem that applies only after checking its hypotheses. The proof below derives the needed generalized mean value theorem from Rolle’s theorem.

Here is the mean-value mechanism. For functions F,GF,G continuous on a closed interval and differentiable inside, apply Rolle’s theorem to [F(x)−F(s)][G(t)−G(s)]−[G(x)−G(s)][F(t)−F(s)][F(x)-F(s)][G(t)-G(s)]-[G(x)-G(s)][F(t)-F(s)]. At some interior cc, [F(t)−F(s)]G′(c)=[G(t)−G(s)]F′(c)[F(t)-F(s)]G^{\prime}(c)=[G(t)-G(s)]F^{\prime}(c). When G′G^{\prime} never vanishes, its mean value theorem also makes G(t)≠G(s)G(t)\ne G(s), so the quotient of endpoint differences equals F′(c)/G′(c)F^{\prime}(c)/G^{\prime}(c).

For the 0/00/0 form at a finite endpoint, extend ff and gg by zero there and apply this generalized mean value result from the endpoint to xx. Its interior point approaches the endpoint with xx, so the derivative-ratio limit gives f(x)/g(x)→Lf(x)/g(x)\to L. An infinite endpoint can first be moved to a finite one by t=1/xt=1/x or t=−1/xt=-1/x; both derivatives acquire the same nonzero chain factor, preserving their ratio.

For the ∞/∞\infty/\infty form, choose a fixed anchor t0t_0 sufficiently near the approached endpoint. All derivative ratios beyond it are as close to finite LL as desired; for signed infinite LL, they exceed any chosen positive bound or fall below any chosen negative bound. The generalized mean value theorem transfers these bounds to [f(x)−f(t0)]/[g(x)−g(t0)][f(x)-f(t_0)]/[g(x)-g(t_0)]. Finally, f(x)/g(x)={[f(x)−f(t0)]/[g(x)−g(t0)]}[1−g(t0)/g(x)]+f(t0)/g(x)f(x)/g(x)=\{[f(x)-f(t_0)]/[g(x)-g(t_0)]\}[1-g(t_0)/g(x)]+f(t_0)/g(x). Since ∣g(x)∣→∞|g(x)|\to\infty, the last factor and the final term have the needed limits. The one-sided interval and nonzero-denominator hypotheses remain essential.

For example, as x→0x\to0, (ex−1)/x(e^x-1)/x is 0/00/0. The derivative denominator is 11 and the derivative quotient exe^x tends to 11, so the original limit is 11. As x→+∞x\to+\infty, ln⁡(x)/x\ln(x)/x is ∞/∞\infty/\infty, and the derivative quotient 1/x1/x tends to zero. Thus logarithmic growth is slower in this precise ratio sense.

Other indeterminate expressions need algebra before the rule is even a candidate. For xln⁡xx\ln x as x→0+x\to0^+, write ln⁡(x)/(1/x)\ln(x)/(1/x), giving a quotient of quantities unbounded in magnitude. The derivative quotient is (1/x)/(−1/x2)=−x(1/x)/(-1/x^2)=-x, so the limit is zero. For powers, taking a logarithm can convert the problem to a product, provided the base is positive; then recover the original limit using continuity of the exponential.

Check the form before each repeated application. As x→0x\to0, (1+x)/x(1+x)/x is not 0/00/0; its two-sided limit does not exist, although the quotient of derivatives equals 11. Also, failure of the derivative quotient to have a limit does not imply failure of the original limit. The rule gives a sufficient route to a conclusion, not an equivalence. Algebra, squeeze arguments, and known limits remain valid alternatives.

Repeated L'Hôpital applications require a fresh check after each differentiation. For (1−cos⁡x)/x2(1-\cos x)/x^2 as x→0x\to0, the first derivative quotient is sin⁡x/(2x)\sin x/(2x), still a zero-over-zero form. A second application gives cos⁡x/2→1/2\cos x/2\to1/2. On a punctured interval, the derivative of each denominator is nonzero at the stage where the rule is used. Alternatively, the known trigonometric limit sin⁡x/x→1\sin x/x\to1 resolves the first derivative quotient without applying the rule again. The theorem is a route to an answer, not a substitute for choosing a simple valid route.

The rule has no converse. Let f(0)=0f(0)=0 and f(x)=x2sin⁡(1/x)f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x) for x≠0x\ne0, and take g(x)=xg(x)=x. Then f(x)/g(x)=xsin⁡(1/x)→0f(x)/g(x)=x\sin(1/x)\to0. But f′(x)/g′(x)=2xsin⁡(1/x)−cos⁡(1/x)f^{\prime}(x)/g^{\prime}(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x) has no limit as x→0x\to0. Failure of the derivative quotient to converge did not prevent the original quotient from converging. This is why an unsuccessful L'Hôpital attempt cannot by itself prove that the requested limit does not exist; return to algebra or squeeze estimates.

An infinite quotient may also hide an orientation issue. A formula such as 1/x1/x near zero has neither a common finite nor a common one-sign infinite two-sided limit, even though the denominator tends to zero. Before differentiating a ratio, write the actual limiting form on the side under consideration. For a product such as xln⁡xx\ln x near zero from the right, rewriting it as ln⁡x/(1/x)\ln x/(1/x) creates an unbounded-over-unbounded quotient with a valid one-sided domain. The domain and the form are part of the theorem's hypotheses, not afterthoughts.

What the mean value theorem can and cannot certify

Suppose a differentiable temperature record T(t)T(t), measured in degrees Celsius, is continuous from t=0t=0 to t=5t=5 minutes and its heating rate never exceeds 3 °C per minute in magnitude on the interior. The mean value theorem says that for some cc between 00 and 55, T′(c)=[T(5)−T(0)]/5T^{\prime}(c)=[T(5)-T(0)]/5. Because ∣T′(c)∣≤3|T^{\prime}(c)|\le3, it follows that ∣T(5)−T(0)∣≤15|T(5)-T(0)|\le15 °C. This is a bound on the entire interval derived from a bound on every instantaneous rate.

If measurements instead show a 20 °C increase over those five minutes, the average rate is 4 °C/min. Under the continuity and differentiability hypotheses, the theorem forces at least one instant with T′=4T^{\prime}=4 °C/min. Consequently the “at most 3” rate claim, the smooth-record assumptions, or the measurements must be wrong. The theorem locates no specific time cc and does not say the rate is 4 throughout the interval.

The Chapter 08 Lesson 2 corner f(x)=∣x∣f(x)=|x| still warns against applying Rolle across zero: it is continuous and has equal endpoint values on [−1,1][-1,1], but its derivative is undefined at zero and never zero elsewhere. A jump instead breaks continuity. The finite-piece argument below explains when a global change bound can survive a corner.

More generally, if a continuous ff is differentiable inside [a,b][a,b] and ∣f′(x)∣≤M|f^{\prime}(x)|\le M there, applying the mean value theorem to any two points u,vu,v in the interval yields ∣f(v)−f(u)∣≤M∣v−u∣|f(v)-f(u)|\le M|v-u|. This Lipschitz estimate controls every pair, not just the endpoints. It is a useful bridge from local derivative information to a global error or safety bound; its units are output units per input unit times input distance.

The bound ∣f′∣≤M|f^{\prime}|\le M can certify an instrument's range. If a temperature record is smooth enough on a five-minute interval and an independently justified physical law bounds its rate magnitude by 33 °C/min throughout, then every pair of readings u,vu,v satisfies ∣T(v)−T(u)∣≤3∣v−u∣|T(v)-T(u)|\le3|v-u|. In particular, readings one minute apart cannot differ by more than 33 °C under the model. A larger observed difference signals that a premise failed—perhaps a sensor glitch, an unmodeled jump, or an incorrect rate bound. The theorem cannot identify which premise failed without extra evidence.

The mean value theorem does not say the rate equals its average at a time you can choose in advance. It also does not say the rate is near the average for most of the interval. A smooth function may move very slowly for much of a trip and rapidly for a short part while still having the required mean-value point. The theorem guarantees at least one exact match. To locate or bound where it occurs, additional information such as monotonicity of the derivative is needed.

When the derivative is bounded only on pieces separated by a corner, a similar global bound may still be assembled if the function stays continuous. Apply the mean value theorem on each smooth subinterval and add the absolute changes; the triangle inequality gives a bound over the whole route using the same maximum rate. This extension requires a finite partition whose pieces satisfy the hypotheses. It does not authorize ignoring a jump discontinuity, because a jump can create a finite output change over arbitrarily small input distance. The hypotheses tell us precisely which gaps a derivative bound cannot cross.

The absolute-value function illustrates how a global change bound can survive one corner. It is continuous everywhere and has derivative −1-1 on the negative half-line and +1+1 on the positive half-line. Although the ordinary mean value theorem cannot be applied across zero in one step, the reverse triangle inequality gives the exact bound ∣∣u∣−∣v∣∣≤∣u−v∣||u|-|v||\le|u-v| for every pair of real inputs. One could also split an interval at zero, apply the theorem on each smooth piece, and add the resulting bounds. By contrast, the step function that is zero for negative inputs and one for nonnegative inputs has a jump: choosing inputs −δ-\delta and +δ+\delta gives output difference one over input distance 2δ2\delta, so no fixed finite rate bound works as δ↓0\delta\downarrow0. Checking derivatives at a finite set of sampled times would miss that jump. A claimed uniform rate guarantee must control all eligible points and address every nonsmooth boundary where the smooth theorem cannot be applied directly.

Deeper derivation · A theorem is only as strong as its hypotheses

The mean value theorem states that if a<ba<b and ff is continuous on [a,b][a,b] and differentiable on (a,b)(a,b), then some c∈(a,b)c\in(a,b) satisfies f′(c)=[f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a)f^{\prime}(c)=[f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a). The theorem locates an instantaneous slope equal to the average slope; it does not tell us where cc is or that there is only one. Geometrically a tangent runs parallel to the secant through the endpoints. A jump or corner can break the conclusion because one of the hypotheses fails.

A powerful consequence is monotonicity. If ff is continuous on an interval and differentiable in its interior with f′(x)>0f^{\prime}(x)>0 there, apply the theorem to any a<ba<b in that interval: f(b)−f(a)=f′(c)(b−a)>0f(b)-f(a)=f^{\prime}(c)(b-a)>0. Thus ff is strictly increasing. If f′=0f^{\prime}=0 everywhere in the interior, the function is constant on the interval. This conclusion is global across the interval and cannot be justified by looking at a few derivative samples. For f(x)=x3−3xf(x)=x^3-3x, f′(x)=3x2−3f^{\prime}(x)=3x^2-3 changes sign at −1-1 and 11, dividing the real line into increasing, decreasing, increasing regions.

Critical points are candidates, not answers. For an absolute extreme on a closed interval, compare values at interior points where f′=0f^{\prime}=0 or f′f^{\prime} fails to exist and at both endpoints. The extreme value theorem guarantees maxima and minima when ff is continuous on a compact interval. On an open interval the extrema can be missed; f(x)=xf(x)=x on (0,1)(0,1) has neither an attained maximum nor an attained minimum. The visual derivative sign plot helps organize candidates but does not replace endpoint checks.
