A derivative becomes the unknown's defining rule

Previously we differentiated a known function to obtain its rate. A differential equation reverses that direction: the rate is specified, and the function must be found. In y′=f(t,y)y'=f(t,y), the independent variable tt often measures time and yy describes a state such as temperature or population. A solution on an interval II is a differentiable function y(t)y(t) whose values remain in the domain of ff and satisfy the equation at every point of II. A formula that works only at selected sample times is not a solution in this sense.

The equation y′=2yy^{\prime}=2y admits y(t)=Ce2ty(t)=Ce^{2t} for every real constant CC. An initial condition, such as y(0)=3y(0)=3, selects C=3C=3. Together, the rate equation and initial condition form an initial value problem, abbreviated IVP. We should specify the interval as well: some solutions exist for all real tt, while others become unbounded after finite time. A model used only for t≥0t\ge0 may nevertheless have a mathematical solution for negative tt. The modeling interval and the maximal mathematical interval are different questions.

A slope field depicts ff without first solving the equation. At a grid point (t,y)(t,y), draw a short segment with slope f(t,y)f(t,y). A solution curve must be tangent to the displayed direction wherever it passes. For y′=t−yy^{\prime}=t-y, the segments are horizontal along y=ty=t, slope upward below that line, and downward above it. The line y=ty=t itself is not a solution: its derivative is 11 although the prescribed derivative on it is 00. A set of horizontal field segments is a nullcline, not automatically a trajectory.

Axis scales matter in a slope field. If yy is measured in degrees Celsius and tt in minutes, ff has units degrees Celsius per minute; changing the time unit changes its numerical value. A picture suggests qualitative behavior but cannot establish existence or uniqueness. Its finite grid may miss a singularity or a narrow region of rapid change. Substitute any proposed exact solution back into both the differential equation and the initial condition before accepting it.

An equation can describe many functions until enough conditions are supplied. For y′=2yy^{\prime}=2y, every Ce2tCe^{2t} is a solution on R\mathbb R, and an initial value selects CC. A boundary condition, such as values at two different times, is a different kind of requirement and can be inconsistent or overdetermined for a first-order equation. The order of the equation suggests the usual number of independent initial data, but nonlinear singularities and restricted domains can complicate that count. State the interval on which the solution is claimed.

Checking a proposed solution means differentiating it and substituting into the original equation, not only matching the initial value. For y′=t−yy^{\prime}=t-y, the candidate y=t−1y=t-1 has derivative 11, while the right side is t−(t−1)=1t-(t-1)=1, so it is a solution. Its initial value at t=0t=0 is −1-1. A curve can pass through the correct starting point and still fail the rate equation elsewhere, so both checks are essential. A slope field shows this as a tangency condition along the entire curve.

A differential equation is a model only after variables and units are assigned. If yy is concentration in mg/L and tt is hours, y′y^{\prime} has units mg/(L·h). In y′=−kyy^{\prime}=-ky, kk must have units h−1\mathrm h^{-1}. A sum such as y′=t+yy^{\prime}=t+y is dimensionally inconsistent unless coefficients or nondimensional variables make the terms compatible. Units can reveal a missing rate constant before any solution is attempted. The same equation form can model population, temperature difference, or chemical concentration with different physical limits.

Read each slope-field mark as a local tangent suggestion, then compare it with a proposed full curve. A finite grid may miss a rapidly changing or undefined rate. For y′=t−yy^{\prime}=t-y, the exact curve y=t−1y=t-1 passes every substitution check; the nullcline y=ty=t is not a solution because its slope is 11 while the prescribed slope there is 00. State an initial point and validate the equation before treating a drawn trace as a trajectory.

A maximal solution interval may end even when the formula for ff is smooth everywhere. For y′=y2y^{\prime}=y^2 with y(0)=2y(0)=2, separation gives y(t)=2/(1−2t)y(t)=2/(1-2t), defined for t<1/2t<1/2. As tt approaches 1/21/2 from below, yy diverges, so this solution cannot be extended as a finite-valued solution through that time. Local existence and uniqueness near the initial point do not promise a global solution. This distinction matters when interpreting a model far beyond its observed time range.

An initial condition is a measured or specified state, not a guarantee that the differential equation fits observations. Given y′=2yy^{\prime}=2y and y(0)=3y(0)=3, the mathematical prediction is y(t)=3e2ty(t)=3e^{2t}. To test it as a growth model, compare measured ratios over equal time intervals: the model predicts the same multiplicative factor e2Δte^{2\Delta t} for every interval of length Δt\Delta t. Systematic departures may point to changing rates, resource limits, or measurement bias. The calculation and the empirical judgment are separate. A precise book should derive the prediction while making clear which assumptions produced it.

The blow-up solution 2/(1−2t)2/(1-2t) from above has maximal interval (−∞,1/2)(-\infty,1/2) through t=0t=0. Its algebraic pole prevents continuation as a finite solution, even though f(y)=y2f(y)=y^2 is smooth everywhere. A physical model can fail earlier than the mathematical interval. Keep the two boundaries separate when interpreting a long-range prediction.

Separation, growth, and the solutions division can lose

A separable equation has the form y′=a(t)b(y)y'=a(t)b(y). On an interval where b(y)b(y) is nonzero, choose an antiderivative HH with H′(y)=1/b(y)H'(y)=1/b(y). The chain rule gives dH(y(t))/dt=a(t)dH(y(t))/dt=a(t), so H(y(t))=∫a(t) dt+CH(y(t))=\int a(t)\,dt+C. The familiar notation dy/b(y)=a(t) dtdy/b(y)=a(t)\,dt summarizes this chain-rule argument. Before dividing, record every root of bb: each gives a constant equilibrium solution that the division would otherwise remove. After integration, determine which interval makes the resulting expression a valid solution.

If each member of a population contributes the same average net growth rate, P′=rPP^{\prime}=rP. Here PP is a continuous population approximation, tt might be measured in days, and rr has units day−1\mathrm{day}^{-1}. Separation yields ln⁡∣P∣=rt+C\ln|P|=rt+C and hence P(t)=P0ertP(t)=P_0e^{rt}. The zero solution is included separately. For P0>0P_0>0 and r>0r>0, the doubling time is (ln⁡2)/r(\ln 2)/r, independent of P0P_0. With r<0r<0 the same equation describes exponential decay. Constancy of the per-capita rate, not merely an upward trend, is the decisive modeling assumption.

Resource limitation motivates P′=rP(1−P/K)P^{\prime}=rP(1-P/K), with r>0r>0 and carrying capacity K>0K>0 in the same units as PP. The per-capita rate now decreases linearly with population. For P0>0P_0>0, separation and partial fractions give P(t)=K/[1+(K/P0−1)e−rt]P(t)=K/[1+(K/P_0-1)e^{-rt}]. One way to integrate is to note that 1/[P(1−P/K)]=1/P+1/(K−P)1/[P(1-P/K)]=1/P+1/(K-P). Thus the antiderivative is ln⁡∣P∣−ln⁡∣K−P∣\ln|P|-\ln|K-P|. The cases P=0P=0 and P=KP=K must be checked before this division; both are equilibria.

For 0<P0<K0<P_0<K the logistic solution increases toward KK without attaining it at any finite forward time. If P0>KP_0>K, it decreases toward KK; exceeding carrying capacity is not forbidden by the equation. Such a solution has a finite-time singularity in its backward extension, so a forward model need not exist for every negative time. Logistic growth approximates exponential growth when P/KP/K is small, but neither model establishes that actual birth rates, resources, or measurement practices stay fixed. Negative populations are algebraically possible initial data and physically inappropriate here.

Separation is an application of the chain rule on intervals where the divided factor is nonzero. For y′=y(1−y)y^{\prime}=y(1-y), dividing by y(1−y)y(1-y) excludes y=0y=0 and y=1y=1. Those constant functions still solve the original equation and must be restored. If a solution starts exactly at one of these equilibria and uniqueness holds, it remains there. A solver that reports only the nonconstant logistic family is incomplete. Checking zeros of b(y)b(y) before division is a general safeguard.

For y′=−kyy^{\prime}=-ky with k>0k>0, the solution y(t)=y0e−kty(t)=y_0e^{-kt} stays the same sign as y0y_0 and has half-life (ln⁡2)/k(\ln 2)/k when y0>0y_0>0. Half-life does not depend on the starting amount in this linear model. If data show a half-life that changes with concentration, the simple proportional-decay assumption may be inadequate. The exponential formula is an exact consequence of the differential equation, while the equation itself is an empirical modeling choice that should be checked against context.

Logistic growth has two positive parameters with distinct roles: rr sets the initial time scale, while KK sets a population scale and long-run level for positive initial data. The instantaneous per-capita rate P′/P=r(1−P/K)P^{\prime}/P=r(1-P/K) decreases as PP grows. For small PP relative to KK, this is close to rr, so early growth resembles an exponential. Near KK, the rate slows. Fitting rr and KK from short early data can be difficult because the curve has not yet revealed its carrying capacity.

For numerical evaluation of the logistic formula already derived, KP0ert/[K+P0(ert−1)]KP_0e^{rt}/[K+P_0(e^{rt}-1)] makes positivity transparent for r,K,P0>0r,K,P_0>0 and t≥0t\ge0, but erte^{rt} can overflow. The equivalent e−rte^{-rt} form is safer at large positive time. If 0<P0<K0<P_0<K, the normalized denominator 1+(K/P0−1)e−rt1+(K/P_0-1)e^{-rt} exceeds one and the population stays below KK; if P0>KP_0>K, it remains above KK. These domain and evaluation checks are separate from the separation algebra.

For a separable equation, an antiderivative can introduce absolute values and branch choices. In ∫dy/y=∫r dt\int dy/y=\int r\,dt, the equation ln⁡∣y∣=rt+C\ln|y|=rt+C describes nonzero solutions on an interval where yy keeps its sign. Exponentiating gives y=Certy=Ce^{rt} with C≠0C\ne0; the zero solution is then added separately. One should not silently cross through y=0y=0 by manipulating ln⁡∣y∣\ln|y|, because the antiderivative is undefined there. The original differential equation and uniqueness theorem determine whether a solution can cross an equilibrium.

A solution found by separation should always be substituted back, especially after exponentiating or taking roots. Algebraic transformations can introduce sign branches or lose a constant solution, and an implicit equation may define several branches rather than one function. For y′=y2y^{\prime}=y^2, integration gives −1/y=t+C-1/y=t+C on intervals where y≠0y\ne0. The zero function also solves the equation, and each nonzero branch has a pole where its denominator vanishes. A complete answer lists branches with their domains and checks the initial value against the chosen branch.

Linear equations and integrating factors

A first-order linear equation has the form y′+p(t)y=q(t)y'+p(t)y=q(t). Linear refers to how yy and its derivative occur: their coefficients may vary with time, but terms such as y2y^2 or yy′yy' are absent. The equation y′+ty=sin⁡ty'+ty=\sin t is linear even though its coefficients are not straight lines. On an interval where pp and qq are continuous, define μ(t)=exp⁡(∫p(t) dt)\mu(t)=\exp(\int p(t)\,dt). The multiplicative constant in μ\mu is arbitrary and nonzero; choosing it conveniently changes no solution.

Multiplication produces μy′+μpy=μq\mu y^{\prime}+\mu py=\mu q. Because μ′=pμ\mu^{\prime}=p\mu, the left side is the product derivative (μy)′(\mu y)^{\prime}. Integration therefore gives y=[C+∫μq dt]/μy=[C+\int\mu q\,dt]/\mu. For initial data at t0t_0, a less ambiguous form uses μ(t)=exp⁡(∫t0tp(s) ds)\mu(t)=\exp(\int_{t_0}^{t}p(s)\,ds), so μ(t0)=1\mu(t_0)=1. Then y(t)=[y0+∫t0tμ(s)q(s) ds]/μ(t)y(t)=[y_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}\mu(s)q(s)\,ds]/\mu(t). This remains a useful exact description when the final integral has no elementary antiderivative.

Newton's cooling model assumes a well-mixed object whose temperature TT changes at a rate proportional to its difference from ambient temperature AA: T′=−k(T−A)T^{\prime}=-k(T-A), with k>0k>0 measured in inverse time. Constant AA gives T(t)=A+(T0−A)e−ktT(t)=A+(T_0-A)e^{-kt}. If the room temperature changes, T′+kT=kA(t)T^{\prime}+kT=kA(t) is still linear, but inserting the current A(t)A(t) into the constant-ambient formula is generally incorrect. The integral accounts for the entire past forcing.

An integrating factor is chosen to manufacture a product derivative. For y′+2y=6y^{\prime}+2y=6, μ=e2t\mu=e^{2t}, so (e2ty)′=6e2t(e^{2t}y)^{\prime}=6e^{2t}. Integration yields y=3+Ce−2ty=3+Ce^{-2t}. With y(0)=1y(0)=1, C=−2C=-2 and the solution approaches three. Direct substitution verifies both the differential equation and initial condition. The limiting value is also the equilibrium obtained by setting y′=0y^{\prime}=0; the transient term quantifies how quickly the initial discrepancy decays.

The coefficient p(t)p(t) need only be continuous on the interval where the standard integrating-factor derivation is used. If p(t)=1/tp(t)=1/t, a solution interval must stay on one side of zero; a factor based on ln⁡∣t∣\ln|t| cannot be extended through a coefficient singularity by algebra alone. A reported formula should include its interval and any initial point. For variable ambient temperature A(t)A(t), cooling becomes T′+kT=kA(t)T^{\prime}+kT=kA(t), still linear, but the long-run behavior depends on the forcing rather than a fixed constant equilibrium.

For initial data, retain the normalized definite-integral formula proved below: μ(t)=exp⁡(∫t0tp(s) ds)\mu(t)=\exp(\int_{t_0}^{t}p(s)\,ds) and y(t)=μ(t)−1[y0+∫t0tμ(s)q(s) ds]y(t)=\mu(t)^{-1}[y_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}\mu(s)q(s)\,ds]. It remains exact even when the forcing integral is nonelementary. The choice μ(t0)=1\mu(t_0)=1 exposes the initial value and avoids an extra arbitrary constant.

Linear does not mean constant-coefficient or easy closed form. The equation y′+ty=sin⁡ty^{\prime}+ty=\sin t is linear, with integrating factor et2/2e^{t^2/2}, but its forcing integral may not have an elementary antiderivative. The definite-integral formula is still a valid exact solution, and numerical quadrature can evaluate it. Conversely y′=y2y^{\prime}=y^2 is nonlinear even though its separated solution is elementary. Classification concerns how the unknown function enters the equation, not whether an answer fits familiar elementary functions.

A physical model may include a constant input and proportional loss: y′=q−kyy^{\prime}=q-ky with k>0k>0. The equilibrium is q/kq/k, and y(t)=q/k+(y0−q/k)e−kty(t)=q/k+(y_0-q/k)e^{-kt}. If qq and yy describe mass per unit volume, qq has concentration per time units and kk inverse-time units. The solution approaches equilibrium from above or below without crossing it, as the exponential factor stays positive. This model appears in mixing tanks, drug clearance, and thermal relaxation, but each application requires checking the constant-rate assumptions.

The integrating factor is not arbitrary: any nonzero constant multiple of μ\mu works because it multiplies both sides of the product derivative equally. Choosing μ(t0)=1\mu(t_0)=1 is a normalization that makes the initial term transparent. If pp is continuous, μ\mu is positive and never zero, so division by μ\mu is safe on the interval. This positivity also shows uniqueness for the linear IVP: two solutions with the same initial value have a difference zz satisfying (μz)′=0(\mu z)^{\prime}=0 and z(t0)=0z(t_0)=0, hence z=0z=0 throughout the common interval. The algebraic method and uniqueness theorem reinforce each other.

Equilibria, stability, and the limits of uniqueness

For an autonomous equation y′=g(y)y'=g(y), the field does not explicitly depend on time. An equilibrium y∗y^* satisfies g(y∗)=0g(y^*)=0. A phase line marks those roots and the sign of gg between them; positive signs indicate increasing solutions, and negative signs decreasing ones. For logistic growth, gg is positive on (0,K)(0,K) and negative above KK. Arrows point toward KK and away from 00 on the nonnegative state domain. This predicts the long-term direction without solving the equation.

An equilibrium is stable if every sufficiently small initial displacement stays small for all future time, and asymptotically stable if nearby trajectories also converge to it. These statements concern a neighborhood, not every possible initial state. Under continuous differentiability near an equilibrium, g′(y∗)<0g^{\prime}(y^*)<0 implies local asymptotic stability and g′(y∗)>0g^{\prime}(y^*)>0 implies instability. This follows from the nearby sign pattern and can also be motivated by the linear approximation δ′≈g′(y∗)δ\delta^{\prime}\approx g^{\prime}(y^*)\delta. If g′(y∗)=0g^{\prime}(y^*)=0, the test is inconclusive: y′=−y3y^{\prime}=-y^3 attracts toward zero, whereas y′=y3y^{\prime}=y^3 repels.

A standard local theorem says that if ff and fyf_y are continuous on an open rectangle around (t0,y0)(t_0,y_0), an IVP y′=f(t,y)y^{\prime}=f(t,y) has a unique solution on some interval about t0t_0. More generally, continuity of ff and a local Lipschitz bound in yy suffice: on a smaller closed rectangle there is one finite LL such that ∣f(t,u)−f(t,v)∣≤L∣u−v∣|f(t,u)-f(t,v)|\le L|u-v| for every allowed t,u,vt,u,v there. One LL must work throughout that rectangle, not a new constant for each pair. Continuity alone gives local existence under the usual rectangle conditions but may not give uniqueness. These are sufficient tests; their failure alone proves neither nonexistence nor nonuniqueness.

For y′=3∣y∣2/3y^{\prime}=3|y|^{2/3} at y(0)=0y(0)=0, the quotient ∣f(ε)−f(0)∣/∣ε∣=3∣ε∣−1/3|f(\varepsilon)-f(0)|/|\varepsilon|=3|\varepsilon|^{-1/3} is unbounded as ε→0\varepsilon\to0, so no such local Lipschitz constant exists near zero. More decisively, for any a≥0a\ge0 define ya(t)=0y_a(t)=0 for t≤at\le a and ya(t)=(t−a)3y_a(t)=(t-a)^3 for t≥at\ge a. At the join, both one-sided derivatives are zero. Before it, ya′=f(0)=0y_a^{\prime}=f(0)=0; after it, ya′=3(t−a)2=3∣(t−a)3∣2/3y_a^{\prime}=3(t-a)^2=3|(t-a)^3|^{2/3}. Thus every yay_a and the zero solution satisfy the same IVP, proving actual nonuniqueness. By contrast, smooth f(y)=y2f(y)=y^2 has a unique local solution for y(0)=1y(0)=1, namely 1/(1−t)1/(1-t), which blows up at t=1t=1; uniqueness does not give global existence.

A phase line for g(y)=y(2−y)g(y)=y(2-y) has equilibria zero and two. Below zero, gg is negative; between zero and two, gg is positive; above two, gg is negative. Thus two attracts nearby positive and larger states, while zero repels on both sides: negative states move farther negative and can blow up in finite forward time. Stability is a local statement in both directions around an equilibrium, so the sign chart must include both sides before assigning a label.

When gg is C1C^1 near an equilibrium, the derivative test follows from its local sign: g(y)−g(y∗)=g′(y∗)(y−y∗)+o(∣y−y∗∣)g(y)-g(y^*)=g^{\prime}(y^*)(y-y^*)+o(|y-y^*|). A negative derivative makes g(y)g(y) point toward y∗y^* on both sides; a positive derivative points away. If the derivative is zero, the linear term gives no decision: −y3-y^3 attracts and y3y^3 repels. The phase line, with its state-domain qualification, settles those examples.

Uniqueness prevents solution curves of a well-behaved scalar autonomous equation from crossing in the (t,y)(t,y) plane at the same time. If two solutions share one point (t0,y0)(t_0,y_0) where the hypotheses hold, they agree on their common interval. In particular, a non-equilibrium trajectory cannot cross an equilibrium line in finite time under local uniqueness, because the constant equilibrium solution passes through any such meeting point. This gives qualitative information without solving the equation explicitly.

The waiting-time family just verified proves nonuniqueness at zero; a failed sufficient test by itself would not. For comparison, a field can fail a differentiability test while still being locally Lipschitz, so one should check the quantitative bound or construct two solutions before claiming failure. A later visual can vary the waiting time, but direct substitution at every time, especially the join, is the proof.

Stable does not necessarily mean convergent. For y′=0y^{\prime}=0, every initial value remains fixed, so an equilibrium is stable but a nearby nonzero solution does not approach a chosen equilibrium. For a frictionless oscillator, trajectories near equilibrium remain bounded and orbit rather than converge. Asymptotic stability adds convergence to the stay-nearby requirement. In applications, damping often determines whether a system merely stays bounded or actually settles.

Stability should be stated relative to the future-time direction and the model's domain. For y′=−y2y^{\prime}=-y^2, a small positive starting value decreases toward zero, while a small negative starting value decreases away and eventually blows up in finite forward time. Thus zero attracts on the physically feasible half-line y≥0y\ge0 but is unstable on the full real state space. Calling it simply “stable” without naming the state domain would be misleading. A phase-line sign chart on both sides exposes the distinction, and an application may then restrict attention to a justified feasible region.

Second-order equations: two initial conditions and oscillation

A second-order equation usually needs two initial conditions, such as position and velocity. For ay′′+by′+cy=0ay^{\prime\prime}+by^{\prime}+cy=0 with a≠0a\ne0, set D=d/dtD=d/dt and L=aD2+bD+cL=aD^2+bD+c. Trying eλte^{\lambda t} gives p(λ)=aλ2+bλ+c=0p(\lambda)=a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0. Deeper derivation · An initial value can fail to determine one future

An initial value problem combines a rate law y′=f(t,y)y^{\prime}=f(t,y) with a specified starting state. Continuity of ff near the starting point is enough for local existence, but uniqueness needs more control over how the rate changes with yy, such as a local Lipschitz bound. Standard smooth models meet this condition. The distinction matters when a model includes a square root, a switch, or a singularity.

Consider y′=∣y∣y^{\prime}=\sqrt{|y|} with y(0)=0y(0)=0. One solution is y(t)=0y(t)=0 for every tt. Another is y(t)=0y(t)=0 for t≤ct\le c and y(t)=(t−c)2/4y(t)=(t-c)^2/4 for t≥ct\ge c, for any waiting time c≥0c\ge0. At t=ct=c both the value and derivative from each side are zero, so the pieces fit differentiably and satisfy the rate law. The initial condition at t=0t=0 cannot choose among these futures. The derivative of ∣y∣\sqrt{|y|} with respect to yy becomes unbounded at zero, so a local Lipschitz guarantee fails there.

This is a mathematical warning about model interpretation. A numerical solver started at exactly zero may stay there forever, while a tiny rounding or modeling perturbation can initiate growth. A plotted trajectory cannot establish uniqueness. When explaining a physical model, state the valid state region, the regularity of its rate law, and which questions the initial data actually determine.
