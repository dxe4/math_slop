From a journey to an average rate

A position measurement answers where an object is. Two position measurements also tell us something about how it moves. If s(t)s(t) is position along a straight track, the change between times aa and a+ha+h is s(a+h)−s(a)s(a+h)-s(a). Dividing by the elapsed time hh gives an average velocity. For example, a cyclist who moves from the 12-metre mark to the 24-metre mark between 22 and 55 seconds has average velocity (24−12)/(5−2)=4(24-12)/(5-2)=4 metres per second. The calculation does not say that the cyclist maintained that velocity throughout the interval.

The same construction works whenever one quantity depends on another. A change in tank volume divided by elapsed time gives an average filling rate; a temperature change divided by a height change gives an average temperature gradient. If the input unit is UU and the output unit is VV, every difference quotient has unit V/UV/U. A negative rate means the output decreases as the input increases. Its meaning depends on the chosen direction and coordinate system, not on whether the situation is desirable.

On the graph y=f(x)y=f(x), the quotient f(a+h)−f(a)h\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} is the slope of the secant line through (a,f(a))(a,f(a)) and (a+h,f(a+h))(a+h,f(a+h)). We require h≠0h\ne0 and both inputs to lie in the domain. A negative hh selects a point to the left. It reverses both the input difference and the order of the output difference, preserving the correct slope. Comparing positive and negative hh will become essential when deciding whether a single instantaneous rate exists.

An average rate belongs to an interval, not to either endpoint alone. Consider s(t)=t2s(t)=t^2 metres for t≥0t\ge0. Over [1,2][1,2] its average velocity is 4−12−1=3\frac{4-1}{2-1}=3 m/s; over [1,1.1][1,1.1] it is 1.21−10.1=2.1\frac{1.21-1}{0.1}=2.1 m/s. Shortening the interval toward t=1t=1 makes the rate approach 22 m/s. These numbers are exact consequences of the given model. If the positions came from noisy measurements, dividing by an extremely short time could magnify measurement error; a smaller interval is not automatically a better physical estimate. Keep the mathematical limit separate from the accuracy of an instrument. Problem 1 also asks for the instantaneous rate at the same time. First complete its average-rate part; return to its second part after Lesson 2 defines and derives that limit.

There are two common ways to shorten an interval. A forward quotient uses [a,a+h][a,a+h] with h>0h>0, while a backward quotient uses [a+h,a][a+h,a] with h<0h<0 in the formula f(a+h)−f(a)h\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. Both describe a slope relative to the fixed base point aa. If their limits disagree, the graph has no single tangent slope there. If the model is defined only for times after launch, however, a backward quotient at the launch time is unavailable; its absence is a domain fact, not evidence of a corner. Always draw or state the allowed input set before taking a limit.

Average velocity is signed displacement divided by time. Average speed is total distance traveled divided by time, and they need not agree. A runner who goes 100 m east and then 100 m west in 40 s has average velocity zero along an east-positive axis but average speed 20040=5\frac{200}{40}=5 m/s. The distinction persists for instantaneous quantities: velocity is a signed derivative of position; speed is its absolute value. In higher dimensions, velocity becomes a vector and speed its magnitude. This chapter stays on a line so the difference can be seen without vector notation.

The derivative is a limit, not a zero-width quotient

Fix an interior point aa of the domain, with ff defined throughout some open interval around aa. The derivative f′(a)f^{\prime}(a) is the finite limit of the difference quotient as hh approaches zero. The word finite is part of the usual definition in this book. Positive and negative increments must approach the same real number. The equivalent notation lim⁡x→a[f(x)−f(a)]/(x−a)\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)-f(a)]/(x-a) emphasizes that the nearby input xx approaches aa while the base point stays fixed.

The quotient at h=0h=0 is undefined. We never obtain a derivative by dividing zero by zero. Instead, algebra may produce a simpler expression equal to the quotient for every sufficiently small nonzero hh. That expression can have a limit even though the original quotient is undefined at zero. This is exactly the removable-gap reasoning developed in chapter 4. A sequence of decimal calculations suggests an answer; an identity followed by a valid limit establishes it.

There are two related tasks. Computing f′(a)f^{\prime}(a) finds one number at one point. Computing f′(x)f^{\prime}(x) finds a new function whose domain consists of the points where the original function is differentiable. A function may exist at more points than its derivative does. At a boundary of a domain, such as t=0t=0 for a motion starting at time zero, only a right-hand quotient may be available. We call its finite limit a right derivative and state that convention explicitly; it is not a two-sided derivative.

A useful direct calculation is f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2. For h≠0h\ne0, (a+h)2−a2h=2ah+h2h=2a+h\frac{(a+h)^2-a^2}{h}=\frac{2ah+h^2}{h}=2a+h. The limit is 2a2a, so f′(a)=2af^{\prime}(a)=2a at every real aa. The difference between this secant slope and the derivative is exactly hh. This identity predicts how the lab should behave before any code is run and supplies a mathematical check independent of the plot.

To differentiate a reciprocal directly, assume a≠0a\ne0 and restrict hh so a+h≠0a+h\ne0. Then 1/(a+h)−1/ah=−1a(a+h)\frac{1/(a+h)-1/a}{h}=-\frac{1}{a(a+h)} for every nonzero permitted hh. The limit is −1/a2-1/a^2. The restriction on hh is not decoration: the original function does not exist at zero, and a derivative can be considered only where the base input belongs to its domain. The same direct method works for many rational expressions after combining fractions. The algebraic identity must be valid on a punctured neighborhood; substituting h=0h=0 into the original quotient remains invalid.

For f(x)=xf(x)=\sqrt{x} at a>0a>0, rationalize before taking the limit: a+h−ah=1a+h+a\frac{\sqrt{a+h}-\sqrt a}{h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt a} when h≠0h\ne0 and a+h≥0a+h\ge0. The graph can still have a vertical right-hand tangent. Notice that the formula 1/(2a)1/(2\sqrt a) already signals trouble as aa approaches zero, but that signal does not replace checking the endpoint definition.

A derivative formula is always paired with a domain statement. The derivative of 1/x1/x is −1/x2-1/x^2 on (−∞,0)∪(0,∞)(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty); writing it at x=0x=0 would describe a point outside the original function's domain. For x\sqrt{x} the ordinary two-sided derivative exists on (0,∞)(0,\infty); a right derivative at zero may be discussed separately. When a problem asks for f′(x)f^{\prime}(x), report both the rule and its valid inputs. When it asks for f′(a)f^{\prime}(a), verify that this particular aa is an interior point or make the one-sided convention explicit.

A difference quotient has two jobs that should be kept separate. For each nonzero hh, [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h[f(a+h)-f(a)]/h is the slope of an ordinary secant through two distinct graph points. The derivative is the limiting value of those slopes, provided the same finite limit is reached from positive and negative hh whenever both sides belong to the domain. Substituting h=0h=0 into the quotient is never the calculation: its denominator would vanish. For f(x)=∣x∣f(x)=|x| at a=0a=0, positive hh gives quotient 11 and negative hh gives −1-1; both secants are perfectly defined, but their slopes do not approach one common number. At an endpoint of a restricted domain, only one-sided secants may be available. Such a one-sided rate can be useful in a model, yet it should not silently be called a two-sided derivative on an open interval. Distinguishing these cases prevents a graph that merely looks smooth at one viewing scale from being treated as a proof of differentiability.

Tangents express local linear behavior

Once the limiting slope exists, there is a unique line through (a,f(a))(a,f(a)) with that slope. Its equation is y=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)y=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)(x-a). We call this the tangent line. This definition does not require the line to touch the curve at only one point or to stay on one side. For f(x)=x3f(x)=x^3, the difference quotient at zero is h2h^2, so the tangent is y=0y=0; the curve crosses it.

The limit definition gives a stronger interpretation than a drawing. Write f(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)h+r(h)f(a+h)=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)h+r(h). For nonzero hh, r(h)/hr(h)/h is exactly the difference quotient minus f′(a)f^{\prime}(a), and therefore tends to zero. Thus the error in the tangent approximation becomes small compared with the input displacement. Simply saying that the absolute error tends to zero would be weaker: continuity alone would make the constant approximation f(a)f(a) have that property.

For f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 at a=2a=2, the tangent is y=4+4(x−2)y=4+4(x-2). At x=2.1x=2.1 this predicts 4.44.4, while the actual value is 4.414.41. The error is 0.01=(0.1)20.01=(0.1)^2. The tangent line is the only linear function L(x)=f(a)+m(x−a)L(x)=f(a)+m(x-a) whose error is negligible compared with ∣x−a∣|x-a| as x→ax\to a, provided ff is differentiable there. For f(x)=1/xf(x)=1/x near a=1a=1, the tangent model is L(x)=2−xL(x)=2-x. Direct subtraction gives 1x−(2−x)=(x−1)2x\frac1x-(2-x)=\frac{(x-1)^2}{x} for x>0x>0. A symmetric secant alone cannot establish a tangent. For f(x)=∣x∣f(x)=|x| at zero, the centered quotient f(h)−f(−h)2h\frac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h} equals zero for every h≠0h\ne0. Continuity is necessary but not sufficient

Theorem: if ff is defined on an open interval containing aa and is differentiable at aa, then ff is continuous at aa. Proof: for h≠0h\ne0, factor f(a+h)−f(a)f(a+h)-f(a) as hh times [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h[f(a+h)-f(a)]/h. The first factor tends to zero and the second tends to the finite number f′(a)f^{\prime}(a). The product limit is zero. Hence f(a+h)f(a+h) tends to f(a)f(a), which is continuity. The same argument proves the corresponding one-sided statement at an endpoint.

The converse fails. For f(x)=∣x∣f(x)=|x| at zero, the quotient is ∣h∣/h|h|/h. It equals 11 for h>0h>0 and −1-1 for h<0h<0. The graph is continuous but has a corner: the two finite one-sided slopes disagree. No choice of value for f′(0)f^{\prime}(0) can repair that disagreement. Away from zero each branch is a line and is differentiable.

A cusp provides another failure. For f(x)=∣x∣2/3f(x)=|x|^{2/3}, the quotient at zero is sgn⁡(h)∣h∣−1/3\operatorname{sgn}(h)|h|^{-1/3}. From the left it tends to negative infinity; from the right it tends to positive infinity. Neither is a finite derivative. By contrast, the real cube-root function has quotient 1/∣h∣2/31/|h|^{2/3}, which tends to positive infinity from both sides. Its graph has a vertical tangent at zero, but it still has no finite derivative there. A vertical tangent is a geometric description, not an ordinary value of f′f^{\prime}.

Discontinuities automatically rule out differentiability by the theorem. Yet corners, cusps, and vertical tangents show that eliminating discontinuities is only a first check. A smooth-looking computer drawing also cannot certify differentiability: a narrow corner may be hidden between sample points. The definition concerns every sufficiently small allowed increment, not merely the increments a screen can display.

A practical piecewise-function test has two stages. First compare the values and one-sided limits to establish continuity. Then compute the left and right difference-quotient limits at the join. For f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 when x≤1x\le1 and f(x)=mx+bf(x)=mx+b when x>1x>1, continuity requires m+b=1m+b=1. The left slope at one is 22, while the right slope is mm. Differentiability therefore requires m=2m=2 and b=−1b=-1. Matching only the slopes while leaving a jump would be insufficient, because the derivative quotient uses the actual value f(1)f(1).

At zero, [f(h)−f(0)]/h=hsin⁡(1/h)[f(h)-f(0)]/h=h\sin(1/h) and its absolute value is at most ∣h∣|h|, so the squeeze theorem gives f′(0)=0f^{\prime}(0)=0. This argument uses only the derivative definition and the boundedness of sine. To decide whether f′f^{\prime} is continuous, one also needs its values at nonzero xx. The worked example later in this chapter displays the calculation with product and chain rules; treat that step as an optional return after Chapter 6 Lessons 1–3. There it gives f′(x)=2xsin⁡(1/x)−cos⁡(1/x)f^{\prime}(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x) for x≠0x\ne0. At xn=1/(2πn)x_n=1/(2\pi n), f′(xn)=−1f^{\prime}(x_n)=-1, while at yn=1/((2n+1)π)y_n=1/((2n+1)\pi) it equals 11; both sequences approach zero. Higher derivatives measure changes in rates

If f′f^{\prime} is itself differentiable, its derivative is the second derivative f′′f^{\prime\prime}. Repeating the construction gives third and higher derivatives where the required limits exist. These are successive derivatives, not powers: f′′f^{\prime\prime} does not mean (f′)2(f^{\prime})^2. If ff has output units VV and input units UU, the second derivative has units V/U2V/U^2 and the third has units V/U3V/U^3.

For position s(t)s(t), velocity is s′(t)s^{\prime}(t) and acceleration is s′′(t)s^{\prime\prime}(t). Speed is ∣s′(t)∣|s^{\prime}(t)|, so acceleration is not simply “how fast the object is moving.” A negative acceleration can increase speed when velocity is already negative. In the model s(t)=3t2+2ts(t)=3t^2+2t, with position in metres and time in seconds, first principles give velocity 6t+26t+2 metres per second; differentiating that linear function by its difference quotient gives acceleration 66 metres per second squared. The coefficients carry the units needed to make both terms positions.

Higher differentiability is an additional requirement. For f(x)=x∣x∣f(x)=x|x|, the quotient at zero is ∣h∣|h| and tends to zero. The derivative is 2x2x for x>0x>0, −2x-2x for x<0x<0, and zero at zero; together these say f′(x)=2∣x∣f^{\prime}(x)=2|x|. That derivative has a corner, so f′′(0)f^{\prime\prime}(0) does not exist. Being differentiable once does not guarantee being differentiable twice.

Velocity and acceleration answer different local questions. For differentiable velocity v(t)≠0v(t)\ne0, speed is ∣v(t)∣|v(t)| and its derivative is sgn⁡(v(t))a(t)\operatorname{sgn}(v(t))a(t), where a=v′a=v^{\prime}. Thus positive acceleration increases speed while moving in the positive direction, but decreases speed while moving in the negative direction. At an instant with v=0v=0, this sign formula cannot be used; speed may have a corner. For example, s(t)=t2s(t)=t^2 has v(t)=2tv(t)=2t and ∣v(t)∣=2∣t∣|v(t)|=2|t|, which is not differentiable at zero even though position and acceleration are smooth there.

The third derivative of position, when it exists, is sometimes called jerk. It measures how acceleration changes per unit time and has units of length/time cubed. A constant-acceleration quadratic position model has zero jerk. The polynomial s(t)=t3−6t2+9ts(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t has v=3t2−12t+9v=3t^2-12t+9, a=6t−12a=6t-12, and constant jerk 66. These are successive derivatives: each must be interpreted with its own units and domain. A derivative of order three is useful when abrupt changes of acceleration matter, but measuring it from sampled positions is especially sensitive to noise.

Acceleration alone does not determine a unique trajectory. If two position functions have the same second derivative on an interval, their difference has zero second derivative and is therefore a linear function At+BAt+B. Chapter 8 develops the mean value theorem: apply it to the first derivative to show that a zero second derivative makes that derivative constant on the interval, then apply it again to obtain At+BAt+B. The constants represent differences in initial velocity and initial position. For instance, s1(t)=3t2s_1(t)=3t^2 and s2(t)=3t2+4t+7s_2(t)=3t^2+4t+7 share acceleration 66 but follow different paths. A motion problem must give enough initial data, such as position and velocity at one time, before a unique position prediction is possible.

A constant acceleration tells us how velocity changes, not whether speed is increasing at every moment. With s(t)=s0+v0t−12gt2s(t)=s_0+v_0t-\tfrac12gt^2 and g>0g>0, acceleration is always −g-g. Before the apex, velocity v0−gtv_0-gt is positive and speed decreases at rate gg. After the apex, velocity is negative and speed increases at rate gg under the idealized model. At the apex the speed function is ∣v0−gt∣|v_0-gt|, which has a corner: its left rate is −g-g and right rate is +g+g. Position and velocity remain differentiable there. This is a useful warning against casually differentiating an absolute value at a zero crossing. The time of the apex follows from velocity being zero, while the fact that it is a maximum follows from the sign change from positive to negative. A numerical table with samples on both sides can suggest that pattern, but the derived velocity rule establishes it for the entire modeled interval.

Average speed over an interval can remain positive even when instantaneous speed vanishes at one interior time. In the ideal launch model, the ball pauses at the apex for an instant, but it covers positive distance both before and after. Zero velocity at a single time contributes no duration to the average; it does not erase the surrounding journey.

A complete motion model from first principles

A ball is thrown upward from ground level with vertical position s(t)=20t−5t2s(t)=20t-5t^2 metres for 0≤t≤40\le t\le4 seconds. This simple model assumes constant downward acceleration and no air resistance. Its coefficients carry units: 2020 is an initial speed in m/s and 55 multiplies t2t^2 with units m/s². The allowed time interval begins at launch and ends when the ball returns to the ground. Outside that interval the same quadratic is a mathematical expression, but it no longer describes this flight.

Derive velocity without borrowing a memorized power rule. For h≠0h\ne0 with t+ht+h still in the flight interval, s(t+h)−s(t)h=20h−5(2th+h2)h=20−10t−5h\frac{s(t+h)-s(t)}{h}=\frac{20h-5(2th+h^2)}{h}=20-10t-5h. Its limit as h→0h\to0 is v(t)=20−10tv(t)=20-10t m/s at interior times. At t=0t=0 the right derivative is 2020 m/s; at t=4t=4 the left derivative is −20-20 m/s. Those one-sided values describe launch and landing within the modeled domain, while the usual two-sided derivative requires an extension.

Velocity is positive until t=2t=2, zero at t=2t=2, and negative after. The ball therefore rises to s(2)=40−20=20s(2)=40-20=20 m, then descends. Its displacement from launch to landing is s(4)−s(0)=0s(4)-s(0)=0, but total distance traveled is 20 m up plus 20 m down, or 40 m. Average velocity over the four seconds is zero. These three quantities answer different questions, even though all are derived from the same position function.

A second difference quotient gives acceleration: v(t+h)−v(t)h=−10\frac{v(t+h)-v(t)}{h}=-10 m/s² for nonzero hh, so a(t)=−10a(t)=-10 m/s² throughout the interior. At t=3t=3, velocity is −10-10 m/s while acceleration is also negative; speed is increasing because the ball is moving downward faster. This checks the sign interpretation without treating negative acceleration as a synonym for slowing down.

The model can be challenged. Air drag makes the downward and upward halves asymmetric, and a launch from a platform changes the initial height. A measured trajectory may fit the quadratic only over a restricted interval. Differentiation describes the consequences of the model; it does not validate its assumptions. Compare measured positions against the predicted 20 m apex and 4 s flight before using its derivative for decisions.

Deeper derivation · The derivative contains continuity, but asks for more

Suppose ff is differentiable at an interior point aa. For h≠0h\ne0 write f(a+h)−f(a)=h f(a+h)−f(a)hf(a+h)-f(a)=h\,\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. The quotient approaches the finite number f′(a)f^{\prime}(a), while hh approaches zero. Their product therefore approaches zero. Hence f(a+h)→f(a)f(a+h)\to f(a): differentiability forces continuity. The argument needs a two-sided derivative at an interior point; one-sided derivatives at an endpoint give the corresponding one-sided continuity.

The converse fails. The function ∣x∣|x| is continuous at zero because ∣x∣→0|x|\to0, but its right difference quotient is 11 and its left difference quotient is −1-1. The derivative does not exist. A vertical tangent is another failure of finite differentiability: x1/3x^{1/3} has quotient h−2/3h^{-2/3} at zero, which grows without bound from both sides.

A derivative is a local linear approximation, not merely a slope drawn by eye. If f′(a)=mf^{\prime}(a)=m, then f(a+h)=f(a)+mh+r(h)f(a+h)=f(a)+mh+r(h), where r(h)/h→0r(h)/h\to0. The point at which a derivative is requested must belong to the function domain. At an isolated domain point there are no nearby distinct inputs from which to form a usual derivative limit; assigning a slope there would be a convention, not a consequence of the difference quotient. At an endpoint of an interval, a right or left derivative can still describe the rate from the allowed side. State that qualification in a motion model whose clock begins at launch or ends at impact. A numerical table may suggest such a one-sided rate, but its finite secants do not by themselves establish that the corresponding limit exists.

Concept checks

  1. What is the derivative of x2x^2 at x=3x=3?

    • 33
    • 66
    • 99

    Answer. 66. The difference quotient simplifies to 2x+h2x+h, whose limit at x=3x=3 is 66.

  2. If ff is differentiable at aa, what follows?

    • ff is continuous at aa
    • ff has a local maximum at aa
    • f′(a)>0f^{\prime}(a)>0

    Answer. ff is continuous at aa. Differentiability implies continuity, but neither extremum nor positive slope follows.

  3. A position derivative has what units?

    • metres·seconds
    • metres/second
    • seconds/metre

    Answer. metres/second. Differentiating position with respect to time divides length by time.

  4. At a corner with unequal one-sided slopes, the derivative is…

    • their average
    • zero
    • undefined

    Answer. undefined. A two-sided derivative needs matching one-sided difference-quotient limits.

  5. What does a tangent line approximate?

    • The function locally near its contact point
    • The function exactly everywhere
    • Only the function at one other point

    Answer. The function locally near its contact point. Differentiability gives a local linear model with error small relative to input displacement.

Chapter synthesis

  • A derivative is a finite limit of secant slopes; its units are output units divided by input units.
  • The tangent line gives a local approximation whose error divided by the input increment tends to zero.
  • Differentiability implies continuity, but corners, cusps, and vertical tangents show that the converse fails.
  • Higher derivatives describe changes in rates and require additional differentiability.

Common mistakes to check

  • “A tangent meets a graph only once.” The tangent to y=x3y=x^3 at zero crosses the graph there, and tangents can meet their curves again elsewhere; the defining property is the limiting slope. Test with problem 11
  • “Continuity guarantees a derivative.” The continuous function ∣x∣|x| has incompatible one-sided slopes at zero. Test with problem 15
  • “Smaller hh always improves a computed derivative.” As hh shrinks, subtraction and rounding can outweigh the gain from reducing secant error; a printed zero quotient can signal numerical failure. Test with problem 33
