Symmetry and shapes · Short read

Turn, flip—and mind the order

A square shows why doing two perfectly reversible moves in a different order can change the result.

About 6 minutes to read · Optional play and thinking at your pace

Bring: Following a labelled corner around a square.

Two moves on a labelled square

Label a square's corners 1, 2, 3, 4 clockwise, starting at the top left. We have two moves: a quarter-turn clockwise, and a flip in the fixed vertical line through the square's centre. A move carries the labels with the square.

Try the turn followed by the flip. Then start again and try the flip followed by the turn. Both moves are reversible. Both leave the square occupying the same outline. Must their order give the same labelled result?

A small hint

Follow corner 1. You only need one differently placed label to show that the two results differ.

Follow one corner

For a clockwise quarter-turn, the top-left position goes to the top right. For the flip, top left swaps with top right, and bottom left swaps with bottom right. The mirror line stays vertical relative to the page; it does not turn with the square.

The outline hides the difference

Reveal the argument

With the original quarter-turn and vertical flip, the two orders send corner 1 to different places, so they are different transformations. In fact, turn-then-flip leaves 1 at top left, 4 at top right, 3 at bottom right and 2 at bottom left. Flip-then-turn puts 3, 2, 1, 4 in those positions instead.

If we call that quarter-turn R and the flip F, mathematicians write the move performed first on the right: F∘R means R first, then F. This convention takes a little care.

F∘R≠R∘F

Operations whose order can be swapped without changing the result are said to commute. These two do not. Ordinary number multiplication can make commutativity feel inevitable: 2×3=3×2. Combining actions is another sort of multiplication, and it need not behave that way.

Try a different turn

Replace the quarter-turn with a half-turn. Does its order with the same vertical flip still matter?

Worked answer

For this pair it does not. Starting with labels 1,2,3,4 clockwise from top left, either order gives 4,3,2,1 in those positions. A half-turn takes each corner to its opposite, and the vertical reflection commutes with that move. One noncommuting pair does not mean every pair fails to commute.

A good place to stop

A square has become a small machine for combining actions. You have proved that order matters for one pair of moves and checked a pair for which it does not. Symmetry gives algebra something tangible to talk about.
