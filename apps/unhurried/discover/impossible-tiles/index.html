Puzzles and proof · Short read

A puzzle you can stop trying to solve

Two missing squares turn a small tiling puzzle into a proof of impossibility.

About 7 minutes to read · Optional play and thinking at your pace

Bring: Counting squares; the colour argument is introduced here.

Fourteen squares. Seven dominoes. A problem?

Draw a four-by-four board and remove its top-left and bottom-right squares. A domino covers exactly two neighbouring squares, sharing a full edge. You may turn a domino, but you may not overlap pieces or let one hang over the board. Can seven dominoes cover everything left?

The area seems right: sixteen squares minus two leaves fourteen. Try a few placements on paper if you like. There is another way into the question that does not depend on finding a clever arrangement.

A small hint

Colour the board like a chessboard. What must every domino cover?

Give the squares a second job

A count no arrangement can repair

Reveal the argument

Every legal domino covers one light square and one dark square: moving one square horizontally or vertically changes colour. Seven dominoes therefore cover seven of each. Our board has six light and eight dark squares, so it cannot be covered.

This is a proof of impossibility. It rules out every arrangement using one property all legal pieces share. Having an even number of squares was necessary, but it was not enough. The difference between the two colour counts is an invariant when we remove a legal domino: each count drops by one, so their difference stays the same.

(L−1)−(D−1)=L−D

A completely covered board has difference zero. This board starts at −2, so legal domino removals can never get it there.

Change the missing corners

Instead remove the two top corners. The colour counts now agree. Can you give an actual covering, rather than just a count?

Worked answer

Yes. Cover the two remaining squares in the top row with one horizontal domino. Each of the three full rows below takes two horizontal dominoes. That is 1+2+2+2=7 pieces.

Equal colour counts alone still do not guarantee a covering of an arbitrary remaining shape. Remove B1, A2, C3 and D4 instead, where letters name columns and numbers name rows. Six squares of each colour remain, but A1 is isolated: both its neighbours are missing. No domino can cover it. The interactive board includes this counterexample.

A good place to stop

You have used a preserved count to settle a puzzle without trying every move. You have also separated a necessary condition from a sufficient one: unequal colours forbid a tiling, while equal colours invite more investigation.
