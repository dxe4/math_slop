Decisions and fairness · Short read

When a group goes round in circles

Three sensible rankings can produce a majority that prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A.

About 6 minutes to read · Optional play and thinking at your pace

Bring: Reading a ranked list and counting votes.

Three friends choose a film

Three friends must choose between films A, B and C. Each supplies a complete ranking, with no ties. They decide to compare films two at a time: whichever a majority prefers wins that comparison.

Everyone's own ranking is consistent. If someone ranks A above B and B above C, they also rank A above C. It sounds reasonable to expect the group's comparisons to behave the same way. The following small example puts that expectation to the test.

A small hint

Count A against B, B against C, and C against A separately. A first-choice count answers a different question.

Read the whole rankings

Start with Alex's ranking A, then B, then C; Blair's B, then C, then A; and Casey's C, then A, then B. Before checking the counts, predict who prefers A to B. Then change one ranking and see whether the circle survives.

This order chooses C.

In the original setting, Alex and Casey prefer A to B, so A wins two votes to one. Alex and Blair prefer B to C, so B wins two to one. Blair and Casey prefer C to A, so C also wins two to one. Every comparison uses the same three complete rankings.

A majority can make a circle

Reveal the argument

The original group prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. Each comparison has a strict majority, yet no film beats both of the others. The majority relation forms a cycle even though none of the three individual rankings does.

A≻B,B≻C,C≻A

The symbol ≻ here means “wins the group's pairwise comparison.” It does not describe any one friend's ranking. This example is a majority cycle, often called a Condorcet cycle.

For the original friends, a knockout vote makes the order of comparisons matter. A beats B first, then loses to C: C wins. B beats C first, then loses to A: A wins. This does not prove that all elections cycle or that choosing fairly is hopeless. It proves a particular limitation of pairwise majority comparisons on these rankings.

Add a fourth friend

Drew joins with the ranking A, then B, then C. Recount all three comparisons. Does the same strict cycle remain?

Worked answer

A now beats B by three votes to one, and B beats C by three to one. A against C is tied two to two: Alex and Drew prefer A, while Blair and Casey prefer C. The original strict cycle is gone, but there is no strict winner of that tied comparison. A decision rule would need to say how it handles ties. In the activity, a tied comparison stops the knockout: it does not silently choose a winner. Having no strict cycle does not by itself produce a film that strictly beats both others.

A good place to stop

A group's preferences need not behave like one person's ranking. You have a complete counterexample, the exact ballots that produce it, and a reason to ask which voting rule a group actually intends to use.
