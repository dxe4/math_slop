Why odd numbers build squares

A growing square turns a numerical pattern into an argument that works for every positive whole number.

About 5 minutes to read · Optional play and thinking at your pace

Bring: Counting rows and columns in a square.

A pattern with a picture inside it

Add the first few positive odd numbers: 1; then 1+3=4; then 1+3+5=9; then 1+3+5+7=16. The totals are square numbers. Will the sum of the first n positive odd numbers always be n²?

Trying the next case is a useful way to explore. It is not a proof for all positive whole numbers. Instead, imagine making each sum into a square built out of small unit squares.

A small hint

To grow a three-by-three square into a four-by-four square, add a column and a row. Count their squares without counting the new corner twice.

Build a square one border at a time

The picture shows four cases. Its useful part is the construction we can repeat, rather than the number of cases we can draw.

Why the next border is always odd

Reveal the argument

Start with a one-by-one square: its area is 1. To grow an n-by-n square into an (n+1)-by-(n+1) square, put n cells in a new column beside it and n+1 cells in a new row below both. These pieces do not overlap. The border has n+(n+1)=2n+1 cells, the next positive odd number. Repeating the construction gives a square after every added odd number.

n2+(2n+1)=(n+1)2

The first square and the repeatable step give an induction proof: the sum of the first n positive odd numbers is n² for every positive whole number n. The drawing illustrates the step, and the row-and-column count explains why the same step works at any size.

Skip the long addition

What is 1+3+5+7+9+11? Then find the sum of the first twenty positive odd numbers without listing them.

Worked answer

The first sum has six odd numbers, so it is 6²=36. Twenty borders make a twenty-by-twenty square, with area 20²=400. The last number in that sum is 39, because the nth positive odd number is 2n−1.

A good place to stop

A pattern has become an explanation. You can now say both what the sum is and why the rule continues beyond the examples on the page.
