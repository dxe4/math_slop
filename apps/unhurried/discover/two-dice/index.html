Two ways to count, one wrong answer

Nine and ten each have two unordered dice combinations. Why aren't they equally likely?

About 5 minutes to read · Optional play and thinking at your pace

Bring: Addition and small fractions; no probability course needed.

A small bet on a dice total

Roll two fair six-sided dice independently. Which total is more likely: nine or ten? Before calculating, try writing the ways to make each sum.

Nine can be made as 3+6 or 4+5. Ten can be made as 4+6 or 5+5. Two ways each: a tie, perhaps? This is a useful wrong turn. Nothing is wrong with the additions. The question is whether the things being counted have equal chances.

A small hint

Imagine one die is blue and one is yellow. Is a blue three and yellow six the same outcome as a blue six and yellow three?

Give the dice names

Call the dice blue and yellow even if they look identical. An outcome is an ordered pair: first the blue result, then the yellow result. Each of the six blue results can accompany any of the six yellow results. That gives 6×6=36 equally likely outcomes under our fair, independent model.

The two orders of 3 and 6 are different outcomes. Swapping the two fives leaves the outcome (5,5) unchanged. That is the missing distinction in the first count.

Use the full grid to check the count. Count the ticked cells for nine, then change the target to ten. Why does (5,5) get just one cell? Each cell records a particular blue-and-yellow pair, so none is counted twice.

A worked setting. With no clue, 36 ordered pairs remain. 4 pairs match “total 9”. Probability: 4/36 = 1/9.

A complete comparison

Reveal the argument

Nine occurs in four of the 36 equally likely ordered outcomes. Ten occurs in three. Nine is therefore more likely: its probability is 4/36=1/9, while ten has probability 3/36=1/12.

P(9)=436>336=P(10)

We could count unordered pairs instead, but we would have to weight them correctly. An unequal pair such as {3,6} represents two ordered outcomes; the equal pair {5,5} represents only one. “Number of ways” is not a probability until the ways and their weights are clear.

The result depends on fair dice and independence. Loaded dice or a mechanism linking the rolls can change the probabilities. A long series of actual rolls may resemble these fractions, but it need not match them exactly.

One more comparison

Compare ten and eleven using the same ordered-outcome method.

Worked answer

Ten has (4,6), (5,5), (6,4): three outcomes. Eleven has (5,6), (6,5): two. Their probabilities are 3/36 and 2/36, so ten is more likely. An unordered count gives two pairs for ten and one for eleven; those pairs still do not all have the same weight.

A good place to stop

The small habit to keep is to name the outcomes before counting them. It can expose a hidden assumption in an apparently obvious answer—and it gives you a reliable way to settle this particular bet.
