Plain-language definitions with a small example and a route back into the mathematics.

106 terms

Integer

ℤ

A whole number, its negative, or zero. Integers do not include non-whole fractions.

For example: −3, 0, and 12 are integers; 2.5 is not.

Explore further: Divisibility as a witness · Arithmetic on a clock

Rational number

ℚ

A real number expressible as a/b for integers a and b with b≠0. Its decimal expansion terminates or eventually repeats.

For example: 7/4=1.75 and 1/3=0.333… are rational; √2 is not.

Explore further: The missing number in the rationals

Real number

ℝ

A number represented by a point on the continuous number line; it includes rationals and irrationals.

For example: √2 is real although it is not a fraction of integers.

Explore further: The missing number in the rationals · How close is close enough?

Complex number

ℂ · i

A number z=a+bi, where a,b are real and i²=−1.

For example: 3−2i has real part 3 and imaginary part −2.

Explore further: Multiplication that turns the plane

Absolute value

|x|

The distance of a real number from zero, so |x| is nonnegative.

For example: |−5|=5 and |5|=5.

Explore further: A corner that defeats the derivative · A minimum with no derivative

Inequality

< · ≤

A comparison such as <, ≤, >, or ≥; its truth can depend on assumptions about the variables.

For example: For every real x, x²≥0; x≤1 is true for x=0 but false for x=2.

Explore further: How close is close enough? · The special promise of a convex bowl

Summation notation

∑

A compact instruction to add terms indexed by a range; the limits specify which terms are included.

For example: ∑_{k=1}^3 k=1+2+3=6.

Explore further: Induction is a chain with a base · Fair games and expected score

Product notation

∏

A compact instruction to multiply indexed terms over the stated range.

For example: ∏_{k=1}^4 k=1·2·3·4=24=4!.

Explore further: Choosing without caring about order · Why anagrams need division

Factorial

!

For an integer n≥1, n! is the product 1·2·…·n; by convention 0!=1.

For example: 5!=120, and there is one way to arrange zero objects, so 0!=1.

Explore further: Why anagrams need division · Choosing without caring about order

Sequence

a_n

An ordered list whose terms are indexed, often by positive integers; a rule or recurrence may define its terms.

For example: a_n=1/n gives 1, 1/2, 1/3,… .

Explore further: A limit can change the shape of a theorem · A recurrence remembers its past

Series

∑

A sum of sequence terms. An infinite series is interpreted through the limit of its finite partial sums, if that limit exists.

For example: The first four partial sums of ∑_{n=1}∞1/2ⁿ are 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, 15/16; the series sums to 1.

Interval

(a,b) · [a,b]

A subset of the real line containing every point between any two of its points; endpoint brackets say whether endpoints are included.

For example: [2,5) contains 2 and 4.9 but not 5.

Explore further: How close is close enough? · One tolerance for the whole interval

Coordinate

(x,y)

A number or ordered tuple that locates a point relative to a chosen coordinate system.

For example: In the plane, (−1,2) is one unit left and two units up from the origin.

Explore further: A walk is a vector · Reading a landscape without seeing the mountain

Function

f:X→Y

A rule assigning each input in a stated domain exactly one output in a codomain.

For example: f(x)=x² on ℝ assigns 3 the single output 9.

Explore further: When can a function be undone? · How to read a definition without fear

Domain and codomain

f:X→Y

The domain is the allowed set of inputs; the codomain is the declared set in which outputs must lie. The actual outputs form the image.

For example: For f:ℝ→ℝ, f(x)=x² has domain ℝ, codomain ℝ, and image [0,∞).

Explore further: When can a function be undone? · Relationships before ingredients

Image and preimage

f[A] · f⁻¹[B]

The image of a set is its set of outputs. The preimage of a target set is all allowed inputs whose outputs land in that set.

For example: For f(x)=x², the preimage of {4} is {−2,2}, while the image of {−2,1} is {4,1}.

Explore further: When can a function be undone? · Draw the solutions of a polynomial

Function composition

g∘f

The composition g∘f applies f first, then g; it is defined for inputs whose f-output is an allowed input of g.

For example: If f(x)=x+1 and g(x)=2x, then (g∘f)(3)=8.

Explore further: The chain rule as two magnifying lenses · A structure-preserving translation

Inverse function

f⁻¹

A function f has an inverse when it is one-to-one and onto its codomain; the inverse reverses the input-output pairing.

For example: f:ℤ→ℤ, f(n)=n+2 has inverse f⁻¹(n)=n−2. Squaring on ℝ has no inverse function because 2 and −2 have the same square.

Explore further: When can a function be undone? · A map that respects the operation

Graph of a function

y=f(x)

The set of input-output pairs (x,f(x)), often drawn in a coordinate plane. A vertical line meets a function graph at most once.

For example: The graph of y=x+1 contains (2,3) and not (2,4).

Explore further: A rate at an instant · One dataset, several histograms

Parameter

m · b

A fixed choice that selects a member of a family of objects or models; it can vary between problems while remaining fixed within one calculation.

For example: In y=mx+b, m and b select a line; x varies along that selected line.

Explore further: A tiny parameter change can erase equilibrium · The special promise of a convex bowl

Proposition

A declarative mathematical statement that is either true or false under its stated interpretation.

For example: “7 is prime” is true; “x>0” is not a proposition until x is specified or quantified.

Explore further: What and, or, and not actually say · How to read a definition without fear

Conjecture

A claim believed from evidence or pattern but not yet established by a proof.

For example: One might conjecture after checking small n that n²+n+41 is prime for every nonnegative integer n; n=40 disproves the claim because 40²+40+41=1681=41².

Explore further: One counterexample ends a universal claim

Theorem

A mathematical statement established by a proof from definitions, assumptions, and accepted earlier results.

For example: The theorem that the sum of two even integers is even follows by writing them as 2a and 2b.

Explore further: A first direct proof with even numbers · How to read a definition without fear

Lemma

A proved result used as a step toward another result. The word describes a role, not a lower standard of proof.

For example: To prove a formula for all n by induction, first prove its base case and induction step as lemmas within the argument.

Explore further: Induction is a chain with a base · When the next step needs every earlier step

Proof

A finite mathematical argument showing that a conclusion follows from stated assumptions and valid inference. Checking many examples can suggest a proof but does not replace it.

For example: For any integer k, (2k)²=4k²=2(2k²), so the square of an even integer is even.

Explore further: A first direct proof with even numbers · What a proof actually owes

Counterexample

One allowed example for which a universal claim is false.

For example: n=2 refutes “every prime is odd,” since 2 is prime and even.

Explore further: One counterexample ends a universal claim · The missing number in the rationals

Implication

P⇒Q

The statement “if P, then Q” is false only when P is true and Q is false. It does not assert that P is true.

For example: “If n is divisible by 4, then n is even” is true; it says nothing about whether a given n is divisible by 4.

Explore further: The surprising truth of if–then · Proving backward by contrapositive

Converse

The converse of “if P then Q” is “if Q then P”; it is a separate claim and can have a different truth value.

For example: “If n is divisible by 4 then n is even” is true, but its converse fails at n=2.

Explore further: The surprising truth of if–then · Proving backward by contrapositive

Contrapositive

¬Q⇒¬P

The contrapositive of P⇒Q is ¬Q⇒¬P; these two statements are logically equivalent.

For example: To prove “if n² is odd then n is odd,” prove instead that even n has even square.

Explore further: Proving backward by contrapositive · The surprising truth of if–then

Necessary and sufficient

⇐ · ⇒ · ⇔

P is sufficient for Q if P⇒Q; P is necessary for Q if Q⇒P. “P iff Q” means both directions.

For example: Being divisible by 4 is sufficient but not necessary for being even; divisibility by 2 is necessary and sufficient for an integer to be even.

Explore further: The surprising truth of if–then · A map that respects the operation

Quantifier

∀ · ∃

A symbol that states how broadly a variable ranges: ∀ means “for every,” and ∃ means “there exists.”

For example: ∃x∈ℤ with x²=9 is true (x=3); ∀x∈ℤ, x²=9 is false.

Explore further: Every person has a book: whose book? · Negating an everyone claim

Quantifier order

∀x∃y · ∃y∀x

Changing the order of quantifiers can change a statement’s meaning and truth.

For example: “Every student read a book” allows different books; “there is one book read by every student” requires one shared book.

Explore further: Every person has a book: whose book? · Negating an everyone claim

Mathematical induction

P(n)

A proof method for statements indexed by integers: establish a base case and show truth at n implies truth at n+1.

For example: For n≥1, 1+…+n=n(n+1)/2: check n=1, then add n+1 to both sides.

Explore further: Induction is a chain with a base · When the next step needs every earlier step

Invariant

A quantity or property that remains unchanged through specified operations or steps.

For example: Under the operation of adding 2 to an integer, its parity (whether it is even or odd) stays unchanged: n and n+2 have the same parity.

Explore further: An invariant says what cannot happen · Every edge has two ends

Existence and uniqueness

∃!

An existence claim guarantees at least one object meets conditions; uniqueness says no two distinct objects do. They are separate obligations.

For example: The equation x+2=5 has a solution, x=3, and any solution must equal 3.

Set

{…}

A collection of distinct objects, called elements. Repeating an element does not change the set.

For example: {1,1,2}={1,2}.

Explore further: A set is its members, not its drawing · Why n objects have 2^n subsets

Membership

∈ · ∉

The relation x∈A says that x is an element of set A; x∉A says it is not.

For example: 2∈{1,2,3}, while 4∉{1,2,3}.

Explore further: A set is its members, not its drawing

Subset

⊆

A⊆B means every element of A is also an element of B; equality is allowed.

For example: {1,2}⊆{1,2,3}, and every set is a subset of itself.

Explore further: A set is its members, not its drawing · Why n objects have 2^n subsets

Union and intersection

∪ · ∩

A∪B contains elements in at least one of A,B; A∩B contains elements in both.

For example: If A={1,2}, B={2,3}, then A∪B={1,2,3} and A∩B={2}.

Empty set

∅

The set with no elements, written ∅. It is a subset of every set.

For example: {n∈ℤ:n²=−1}=∅ because no integer square is negative.

Explore further: A set is its members, not its drawing · Why n objects have 2^n subsets

Power set

𝒫(A)

The set of all subsets of a set A, including ∅ and A itself. An n-element finite set has 2ⁿ subsets.

For example: If A={a,b}, then P(A)={∅,{a},{b},{a,b}}.

Ordered pair

(a,b)

A pair (a,b) in which order matters: equality requires both first and second entries to match.

For example: (1,2)≠(2,1), though {1,2}={2,1}.

Explore further: Dots, lines, and what a graph forgets · A walk is a vector

Relation

~

A relation on a set specifies which ordered pairs of its elements count as related.

For example: On integers, a~b when a−b is divisible by 3 is a relation.

Explore further: Grouping by an equivalence relation · Can a loop be shrunk?

Equivalence relation

~

A relation that is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive; it groups objects into equivalence classes.

For example: Equality of remainders modulo 3 partitions integers into classes 0,1,2.

Explore further: Grouping by an equivalence relation · Collapse a normal subgroup

Equivalence class

[a]

The set of all objects related to a chosen object under an equivalence relation.

For example: Modulo 3, the class of 1 is {...,−5,−2,1,4,7,...}.

Explore further: Grouping by an equivalence relation · Collapse a normal subgroup

Finite and countable

A set is finite if it has a fixed finite number of elements. It is countably infinite if its elements can be listed in a sequence indexed by positive integers.

For example: The even integers are countably infinite via 0,2,−2,4,−4,…; the real interval (0,1) is uncountable.

Limit

lim

A value L is the limit of f(x) as x approaches a when f(x) can be made arbitrarily close to L by taking x sufficiently close to a, with x≠a.

For example: For f(x)=2x, lim_{x→3}f(x)=6; f need not be defined at x=3 for this limit notion.

Epsilon-delta definition

ε · δ

A limit f(x)→L as x→a means: for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that 0<|x−a|<δ implies |f(x)−L|<ε.

For example: For f(x)=2x at a=3, choosing δ=ε/2 makes |2x−6|<ε whenever 0<|x−3|<δ.

Continuity

A function is continuous at a when it is defined there, its limit as x→a exists, and that limit equals f(a).

For example: f(x)=x² is continuous at 2; the function equal to 0 for x<0 and 1 for x≥0 is not continuous at 0.

Explore further: One tolerance for the whole interval · A corner that defeats the derivative

Derivative

f'(a)

The derivative at a, when it exists, is the limit of the difference quotient [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h as h→0; it gives local rate and tangent slope.

For example: For f(x)=x², the quotient at a is 2a+h and tends to 2a, so f'(a)=2a.

Explore further: A rate at an instant · A corner that defeats the derivative

Differentiability

A function is differentiable at a if its finite derivative limit exists there. Differentiability implies continuity at a, but continuity alone is not enough.

For example: |x| is continuous at 0 but its left and right slopes −1 and 1 differ, so it is not differentiable there.

Definite integral

∫

For a suitably integrable function on [a,b], its definite integral is the limit of approximating sums of function values times subinterval widths; it records signed accumulation.

For example: For f(x)=2 on [0,3], ∫₀³2 dx=6.

Riemann sum

∑f(xᵢ*)Δxᵢ

A finite approximation to an integral formed by choosing sample points and summing function value times interval width.

For example: For f(x)=x on [0,2] with two right-endpoint rectangles, the sum is 1·1+2·1=3.

Fundamental theorem of calculus

Under standard hypotheses such as continuity of f, differentiation of its accumulation F(x)=∫ₐˣf(t)dt gives F'(x)=f(x); a continuous antiderivative also evaluates the definite integral.

For example: Since an antiderivative of 2x is x², ∫₁³2x dx=3²−1²=8.

Chain rule

For differentiable functions with a defined composition, the derivative of g∘f is g'(f(x))f'(x).

For example: If y=(3x+1)², then y'=2(3x+1)·3=18x+6.

Partial derivative

∂f/∂x

A derivative of a multivariable function with respect to one input while the other inputs are held fixed.

For example: For f(x,y)=x²+3y, ∂f/∂x=2x.

Explore further: Partial derivatives can miss a cliff · A compass that points uphill

Gradient

∇f

For a differentiable scalar function, the gradient vector consists of its partial derivatives and points in a direction of greatest local increase in Euclidean coordinates.

For example: For f(x,y)=x²+y², ∇f(1,2)=(2,4).

Differential equation

An equation involving an unknown function and one or more of its derivatives. A solution is a function that satisfies it on a stated interval.

For example: y'=y has solutions y=Ceˣ for constants C; y=eˣ is one example.

Explore further: Read a differential equation without solving it · The mathematics of a cooling cup

Convergence

A sequence converges to L if its terms eventually stay as close to L as any chosen positive tolerance requires.

For example: a_n=1/n converges to 0 because for any ε>0, n>1/ε gives 1/n<ε.

Uniform convergence

Functions f_n converge uniformly to f on a domain if one index bound works for every point in that domain at once.

For example: f_n(x)=x/n converges uniformly to 0 on [0,1], since |x/n|≤1/n everywhere.

Big O notation

O(g)

For functions near a stated limit, f=O(g) means |f|≤C|g| for some fixed C in a sufficiently small neighborhood; for sequences the analogous bound holds for all sufficiently large indices.

For example: As x→0, sin x−x=O(x³); the constant and neighborhood need not be optimal.

Taylor polynomial

A polynomial built from a function’s derivatives at a point, used as a local approximation when suitable smoothness assumptions hold.

For example: For eˣ near 0, 1+x+x²/2 is its degree-2 Taylor polynomial.

Explore further: A polynomial approximation with an error receipt · A smooth function its Taylor series misses

Numerical error

The difference between a computed approximation and the target quantity; its meaning depends on the chosen absolute or relative scale.

For example: Approximating π by 3.14 has absolute error about 0.00159.

Explore further: Why a computer loses a tiny number · A bad problem or a bad algorithm?

Vector

v

An element of a vector space; in familiar coordinates it is a tuple that can be added and scaled. It can represent displacement or another structured quantity.

For example: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1).

Explore further: A walk is a vector · A compass that points uphill

Dot product

u·v

For Euclidean coordinate vectors, the dot product is the sum of componentwise products; it encodes lengths and angles.

For example: (1,2)·(3,4)=1·3+2·4=11.

Explore further: Why the dot product measures alignment · A compass that points uphill

Matrix

A

A rectangular array of scalars that can encode a linear map once input and output coordinates are chosen.

For example: [[1,2],[0,1]] sends (3,4) to (11,4) under the usual column-vector convention.

Explore further: A matrix is a rule for moving space · When two equations meet

Linear map

A map T between vector spaces satisfying T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v) and T(cv)=cT(v) for every allowed u,v,c.

For example: T(x,y)=(2x,2y) is linear; T(x,y)=(x+1,y) is not because it sends zero to (1,0).

Explore further: A matrix is a rule for moving space · A structure-preserving translation

Basis

A linearly independent set of vectors that spans the whole vector space; each vector then has a unique expression as a linear combination of basis vectors.

For example: The standard vectors (1,0),(0,1) form a basis of ℝ².

Linear independence

Vectors are linearly independent if the only combination equaling zero uses every coefficient zero.

For example: (1,0) and (0,1) are independent; (1,0) and (2,0) are dependent because 2(1,0)−(2,0)=0.

Eigenvector

A nonzero vector v for a linear map or matrix A such that Av=λv for some scalar λ, called its eigenvalue.

For example: For A=[[2,0],[0,3]], (1,0) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 2.

Determinant

det(A)

A scalar associated with a square matrix that records signed volume scaling and detects invertibility.

For example: For [[2,0],[0,3]], the determinant is 6; the map scales plane area by 6.

Explore further: What determinant actually counts · When two equations meet

Least squares

A method that chooses parameters to minimize the sum of squared residuals between observations and model predictions.

For example: For data points (0,1),(1,2),(2,2), a fitted line balances vertical residuals by minimizing their squared sum.

Explore further: The closest answer to an impossible system · Fit a line without squaring the trouble

Topological space

A set equipped with a chosen collection of subsets called open sets, satisfying axioms that include the empty set and whole space and closure under unions and finite intersections.

For example: On a finite set X, declaring only ∅ and X open gives a valid topology.

Explore further: Open means room to move · Continuity without drawing a curve

Open set

In a topological space, a member of the specified collection of open sets; in ℝ with its usual topology, every point has a small interval contained in the set.

For example: (0,1) is open in ℝ; [0,1] is not open in ℝ.

Topological continuity

A map is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open. This definition works without distances or coordinates.

For example: The identity map from a topological space to itself is continuous because each open set has itself as preimage.

Explore further: Continuity without drawing a curve · When two shapes count as the same

Homeomorphism

A bijective continuous map whose inverse is also continuous; it expresses topological sameness.

For example: A filled square and a filled disk are homeomorphic as spaces with boundary: a continuous radial stretching has a continuous inverse.

Explore further: When two shapes count as the same · Glue edges, change a space

Compactness

A space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover; in ℝ this is equivalent to being closed and bounded.

For example: [0,1] is compact in ℝ; (0,1) is not, covered by (1/n,1) for n≥2 with no finite subcover.

Explore further: Why finite subcovers matter · A sequence cannot escape a closed box

Connectedness

A space is connected if it cannot be split into two disjoint, nonempty open subsets relative to that space.

For example: The interval [0,1] is connected; {0,1} with the usual real topology is disconnected.

Explore further: A gap you cannot cover up · Open means room to move

Homotopy

A continuous deformation between maps, represented by a continuous map that varies over an interval parameter.

For example: On the plane, a loop can be continuously shrunk to a point; on a circle, one full turn cannot be shrunk to a point while staying on the circle.

Explore further: Can a loop be shrunk? · Loops around the circle form integers

Fundamental group

A group whose elements are based loops up to continuous deformation that fixes the base point; its operation is concatenation.

For example: The circle’s fundamental group is isomorphic to the integers, recording how many signed turns a loop makes.

Explore further: Loops around the circle form integers · Count turns without trusting a picture

Group

A set with an associative operation, an identity, and an inverse for every element. The operation need not be commutative.

For example: Integers under addition form a group: identity 0, and inverse of n is −n.

Explore further: A square as an algebra machine · How long until a symmetry returns?

Identity element

An element e that leaves every element unchanged under the operation on its specified side; in a group the identity is two-sided and unique.

For example: For addition on integers, 0+n=n+0=n.

Explore further: A square as an algebra machine · How long until a symmetry returns?

Homomorphism

A map between algebraic structures that preserves the specified operation (and, by consequence in groups, identity and inverses).

For example: f:ℤ→ℤ/5ℤ given by remainder mod 5 preserves addition: f(2+4)=f(6)=1=f(2)+f(4).

Explore further: A map that respects the operation · Collapse a normal subgroup

Kernel

ker(f)

For a group homomorphism, the kernel is the set of inputs mapped to the identity; for a linear map it is the set mapped to zero.

For example: For f:ℤ→ℤ/5ℤ by remainder, ker f={…,−10,−5,0,5,10,…}.

Explore further: A map that respects the operation · Missing dimensions have to go somewhere

Quotient structure

A structure formed by treating elements in the same appropriate equivalence class as identified; the relation must respect the operations.

For example: Integers modulo 5 identify n and m when 5 divides n−m.

Explore further: Collapse a normal subgroup · Remainders as quotient rings

Ring

A set with addition and multiplication where addition is an abelian group, multiplication is associative, and distributivity holds; conventions may vary on whether rings must have 1.

For example: ℤ is a commutative ring with identity 1; it is not a field because 2 has no integer multiplicative inverse.

Explore further: Polynomials are numbers with a variable · Remainders as quotient rings

Field

A commutative ring with identity 1≠0 in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.

For example: ℚ is a field because each nonzero a/b has inverse b/a; ℤ is not.

Explore further: Why division sometimes fails modulo n · Turn geometry into a ring

Group action

A rule by which group elements act as reversible transformations on a set, respecting identity and composition.

For example: Rotations of a square permute its four vertices, and combining rotations matches composing the permutations.

Explore further: A square as an algebra machine · Why subgroup sizes divide

Sample space

Ω

The set of possible outcomes for a specified random experiment; events are subsets of it.

For example: For one ordinary die roll, Ω={1,2,3,4,5,6}; the event “even” is {2,4,6}.

Explore further: A deck, a die, and the sample space · Why two chances can overlap

Probability

P(A)

A number from 0 to 1 assigned to an event, with total probability 1 and additive probabilities for disjoint events.

For example: For a fair six-sided die, P(even)=3/6=1/2.

Explore further: A deck, a die, and the sample space · Why two chances can overlap

Conditional probability

P(A|B)

For events A,B with P(B)>0, P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B), the probability of A within outcomes where B occurred.

For example: Given a fair die roll is greater than 3, the chance it is even is 2/3.

Independence

Events A and B are independent when learning whether one occurred does not change the probability of the other; equivalently P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B).

For example: Two separate fair coin flips are independent; “first flip is heads” and “at least one flip is heads” are not.

Random variable

X

A function assigning a numerical value to each outcome in a sample space.

For example: For two coin flips, X can count heads, taking values 0,1,2.

Explore further: Fair games and expected score · How many successes in ten tries?

Expected value

E[X]

The probability-weighted average of a random variable’s values; it describes a long-run mean under repeated comparable trials, not a promised outcome.

For example: A fair die has expected value (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6=3.5, though no single roll is 3.5.

Variance

Var(X)

The expected squared distance of a random variable from its mean; its square root is the standard deviation.

For example: For a constant random variable X=4, Var(X)=0 because it never departs from its mean.

Explore further: Same average, different surprises · Why two honest polls disagree

Probability distribution

A specification of probabilities for the possible values of a random variable, or a density/rule for a continuous variable.

For example: A fair coin’s head-count on one toss has P(X=0)=P(X=1)=1/2.

Binomial distribution

Bin(n,p)

The count of successes in n independent trials with the same success probability p.

For example: Three fair coin flips have probability 3/8 of exactly two heads: choose 2 positions, each sequence has probability 1/8.

Normal distribution

N(μ,σ²)

A continuous symmetric bell-shaped probability distribution determined by a mean and variance; approximations using it require conditions and can be poor in small or skewed samples.

For example: A standard normal variable has mean 0 and variance 1; its probability of lying in [−1,1] is about 0.683.

Statistical sample

Observed units or measurements selected from a population or process; conclusions depend on how sampling and measurement were done.

For example: The heights of 30 randomly selected plants from one greenhouse are a sample, not a census of all plants.

Mean and median

The mean is the sum divided by the number of observations; the median is the middle ordered value, or average of the two middle values.

For example: For 1,2,3,100 the mean is 26.5 and median is 2.5, showing the mean’s sensitivity to an extreme value.

Standard error

The standard deviation of a statistic’s sampling distribution; it describes how much the statistic would vary across repeated samples under a specified design.

For example: For independent observations with population SD σ, the sample mean has standard error σ/√n under the usual finite-variance model.

Confidence interval

An interval produced by a procedure whose repeated-sampling coverage is a stated rate when its assumptions hold; the rate describes the procedure, not a probability assigned to a fixed observed interval.

For example: A 95% interval method captures the true parameter in 95% of repeated samples under its model; a particular computed interval either contains the parameter or does not.

P-value

Under a specified null model, a p-value is the probability of data at least as incompatible with that model as the observed data, using a stated test statistic.

For example: A p-value of 0.03 means such-or-more-extreme outcomes have probability 3% under the null assumptions; it is not a 3% chance the null is true.

Correlation

A measure of association between variables; a common one, Pearson correlation, measures linear association and does not by itself establish causation.

For example: Ice-cream sales and swimming incidents may rise together in summer because temperature influences both.

Entropy

H(X)

For a finite random variable with probabilities p_i, Shannon entropy is −∑p_i log₂p_i bits; it measures uncertainty under this model.

For example: A fair bit has entropy 1 bit; a bit always equal to 0 has entropy 0.

Mathematical model

A mathematical representation of a selected part of the world, built with assumptions and intended for a stated purpose.

For example: A constant-speed model predicts distance d=vt; it fails when speed changes substantially.
