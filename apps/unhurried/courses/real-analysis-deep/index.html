Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

Build a proof portfolio on the real line

This selected deep route is about eighteen hours of work: roughly four hours reading ten articles, eight hours on eighteen proof tasks, and six hours drafting and revising the capstone portfolio. It asks for actual arguments about completeness, compactness, convergence and null sets. It offers a measured gateway to integration, with an explicit boundary: construction of Lebesgue measure, full proofs of Fatou’s lemma and dominated convergence, and Lᵖ theory require follow-on study. Deep means sustained proof work here, not a degree credential.

Entry recap. You should be able to negate a quantified statement, use absolute-value inequalities, write induction, and distinguish a finite sum from its limit. A supremum is the least upper bound in the ambient ordered system. For real intervals, nested closed intervals with widths tending to zero have one common point. For measure exercises, a measurable set has a measure assigned by an assumed countably additive theory; outer-null covers can be proved here, but the full theory is not constructed. If any of these are unfamiliar, work through the primer or accelerated diagnostic before attempting the proof portfolio.

Three arcs, ten linked lessons

  1. Real-line foundations: an explicit sequence tail, the rational gap, and a convergent subsequence. Bridge the first to the second by asking whether a candidate limit exists in the chosen number system. Bridge the second to the third by recognizing the same completeness principle inside nested intervals. Reconstruct the bisection proof from memory, including the strictly increasing subsequence indices.
  2. Continuity and integration: uniform continuity, Riemann failure, and uniform limits. The shared theme is controlling infinitely many cases with one choice. A δ can serve every input pair on a domain; an index N can serve every input point in uniform convergence; a Darboux partition would have to shrink all cell oscillations. Identify precisely which universal claim succeeds or fails in each proof.
  3. Null sets and a Lebesgue gateway: countable covers, the Cantor set, simple-function integrals, and dominated convergence. The first two establish contrasting cardinality examples with the same zero-length conclusion. The third defines a nonnegative integral from below. The fourth applies a named theorem and proves only a bounded finite-measure case using a stated measure lemma. Do not silently promote that special-case proof to the full theorem.

Eighteen proof tasks

Write each response as a claim, hypotheses, proof, and one sentence about what breaks when a hypothesis is removed. Tasks 1–6 concern foundations: (1) prove 5/n→0 with an explicit N; (2) negate the ε–N limit definition correctly; (3) construct rational hundredth brackets for √3; (4) show √2 is irrational by parity; (5) derive the nested-interval property from the least-upper-bound axiom; (6) prove Bolzano–Weierstrass in ℝ by bisection, naming the infinite-term invariant. A full answer to task 5 must explain why the supremum of left endpoints lies below every right endpoint; a full answer to task 6 must choose indices in increasing order.

Hints and worked checks for tasks 2 and 5

Task 2 hint: negate each quantifier in order, then negate the final strict inequality. Check: “aₙ does not tend to L” means there exists ε₀>0 such that for every positive integer N there is an n≥N with |aₙ−L|≥ε₀. The witness n may depend on N, while ε₀ stays fixed. Merely finding one bad term is insufficient.

Task 5 hint: write Iₖ=[aₖ,bₖ] and use nesting. Check: the left endpoints increase and are bounded by b₀, so c=sup{aₖ:k≥0} exists in ℝ. For any fixed k, every aⱼ≤bₖ: if j≥k, Iⱼ⊆Iₖ; if j<k, aⱼ≤aₖ≤bₖ. Hence c≤bₖ, while aₖ≤c, so c∈Iₖ for every k. If d also belongs to every Iₖ, then |c−d|≤bₖ−aₖ→0, forcing d=c. This proves existence and uniqueness under the shrinking-width hypothesis.

Tasks 7–12 concern functions: (7) prove x² is uniformly continuous on [−M,M] for M>0; (8) disprove its uniform continuity on ℝ with ε=1; (9) disprove uniform continuity of 1/x on (0,1); (10) show the rational indicator is discontinuous everywhere; (11) calculate its upper and lower sums on [a,b] for a<b; (12) prove uniform limits preserve continuity by the ε/3 argument. Each task should exhibit the exact quantifier reversal or fixed bound that matters. For task 11, use density inside each partition cell, not a numerical grid.

Tasks 13–18 concern measure and convergence: (13) give an ε-budget cover of a listed countable set; (14) prove the total length of Cantor stage n is (2/3)n; (15) prove the left/right choice map into the Cantor set is injective without ternary notation; (16) integrate a two-height nonnegative simple function on measurable disjoint sets A and B with μ(A)=1/3 and μ(B)=1/5, heights 2 and 4; (17) calculate the pointwise limit and exact integrals of n·1(0,1/n); (18) prove the bounded finite-measure special case of dominated convergence from continuity from above, spelling out where finite total measure is used. The answer to 16 is 2/3+4/5=22/15. The answer to 17 is zero pointwise and integral one at every stage. Task 18 must not claim a full DCT proof.

Mixed proof practice with a model solution

Let fₙ(x)=xⁿ on [0,1], and let C be the Cantor set. Define hₙ=1C+fₙ. Determine the pointwise limit, whether hₙ converges uniformly, and the Lebesgue integrals of hₙ and its limit. State every result used. This problem combines an uncountable null set with a moving error peak.

Model mixed solution

Because 1C does not depend on n, the pointwise limit is h(x)=1C(x)+f(x), where f(x)=0 for x<1 and f(1)=1. The error |hₙ−h| equals |xⁿ−f(x)|; its supremum on [0,1] is one for every n, so convergence is not uniform. The Cantor set is measurable and null because it is outer-null, so ∫1C=0. Also ∫₀¹xⁿdx=1/(n+1). Therefore ∫hₙ=1/(n+1)→0. The limit h is nonzero only on C∪{1}, a null set, so ∫h=0. We used measure-null facts and finite additivity or linearity for integrable nonnegative functions; neither the pointwise calculation nor the supremum error alone determines an integral.

Capstone: a linked counterexample collection

Prepare three panels, each pairing a valid convergence statement with an example that shows a missing hypothesis matters. Panel A: bounded real sequences have convergent subsequences; show why replacing ℝ with ℚ fails using rational brackets to √2. Panel B: uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous; use xⁿ on [0,1] to show pointwise convergence does not suffice. Panel C: under an L¹ dominator, almost-everywhere convergence permits integral convergence; use n·1(0,1/n) to show almost-everywhere convergence alone does not suffice. Add a fourth optional panel contrasting a countable null set with the uncountable Cantor set. In every panel give the exact theorem statement, verify all surviving hypotheses, calculate the decisive limit or integral, and name the lost hypothesis.

Model capstone solution

A: choose rational lower endpoints aₙ and upper endpoints bₙ bracketing √2 with bₙ−aₙ→0. The rational aₙ are bounded. Every convergent subsequence in ℝ has limit √2, because aₙ itself tends to √2 by squeezing. Parity proves √2∉ℚ, so no subsequence converges in ℚ; completeness of the ambient real line is essential. B: each xⁿ is continuous on [0,1]. For fixed x<1 it tends to zero, while at 1 it stays one. The limit jumps at 1. The error supremum is one for every n, so uniform convergence is the missing hypothesis. C: sₙ=n on (0,1/n) and zero elsewhere is measurable, and sₙ→0 everywhere. Yet ∫sₙ=1 and ∫0=0. An L¹ common dominator cannot exist; otherwise dominated convergence would contradict the exact integrals. More directly, such a bound must be at least of order 1/x near zero and therefore has infinite integral. The optional panel notes both ℚ∩[0,1] and C are null, but the former is countable and the latter uncountable by binary diagonalization.
