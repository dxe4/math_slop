Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Three families of stochastic models

This bounded deep route uses all nine topic lessons: finite walks and Markov chains, continuous-time arrivals and a single-server queue, and a branching family tree. Allow about fourteen hours including proof exercises, recaps and a multi-part capstone. “Deep” here means you will derive named small-model results and test their hypotheses. It does not mean you have proved the general ergodic theorem, mastered queueing networks, or learned every branching-process theorem.

Entry recap. A conditional probability focuses on a specified current condition. Independent draws allow products of their chances; mutually exclusive cases allow sums. For a finite Markov chain, a row transition matrix P records P(next=j | current=i), and the distribution advances as πₜ₊₁=πₜP. A stationary row solves πP=π. Exponential e⁻ˣ and the elementary integral ∫₀∞e⁻λᵗdt=1/λ appear in the arrival and queue lessons. A local algebra refresh: solving hᵢ=(hᵢ₋₁+hᵢ₊₁)/2 means recognizing equal adjacent differences. Each linked lesson also introduces its notation locally.

Keep two ledgers as you work. In the first record assumptions and units; in the second record exactly what has been established. For example, solving πP=π establishes an invariant distribution but does not alone establish convergence. A simulated path illustrates a possible outcome but cannot prove an eventual event. Those distinctions carry the proof work in this route.

Module 1: Foundations

  1. A random walk on a tiny map. Re-derive P(Sₙ=s) by choosing the right-step positions. Check parity before counting.
  2. How long until the token reaches an edge? Replace path enumeration by first-step equations. Prove hᵢ=i/N for a fair finite absorbing board and inspect the absorption argument.
  3. Memory lives in the state. Test a proposed state by comparing compatible histories. Add a phase variable when the visible label is insufficient.

The bridge is a choice of state and event. A free walk asks where the token is at a fixed time; an absorbing walk asks which boundary arrives first. In the latter, the current position and absorbing status make a sufficient state. Revisit the proof that absorption occurs eventually: every block of N fair steps has a uniform positive chance of hitting an edge, so perpetual avoidance has probability zero. The recurrence and its boundary conditions then describe an actual first-hit probability rather than an unsupported algebraic guess.

Module 2: Mechanisms and examples

  1. A playlist that chooses its next mood. Derive πₜ₊₁=πₜP by partitioning on today's state, then show normalization survives multiplication.
  2. What a Markov chain settles into. Solve balance equations and separate invariance from attraction. Write down both a periodic two-state counterexample and a reducible identity-matrix example.
  3. Random arrivals at an imaginary museum. Follow the binomial rare-event limit for a fixed count k and name the independent, stationary increment assumptions of the full process.

The bridge from a discrete chain to a Poisson process changes the time axis. Matrix powers describe repeated steps at integer chimes. A Poisson count describes how many events occur in a continuous duration, and its rate has physical units. They are both stochastic processes, but their formulas cannot be transferred by analogy alone. Within the finite chain, irreducibility and aperiodicity permit a convergence theorem; the article states that theorem and proves only its small worked examples. A queue will use continuous-time state changes, yet its stationary balance is again a flow argument.

Module 3: Limits and deeper questions

  1. The next chime has no appointment. Derive exponential survival as the zero-count event and the memoryless conditional ratio. Evaluate the mean wait by a tail integral.
  2. Why a short queue sometimes grows. State every M/M/1 assumption. Derive neighboring-state balance and explain why ρ<1 is needed to normalize the geometric weights.
  3. A family tree that may end. Derive the expectation recurrence and extinction fixed point. Use iteration from zero to choose the smallest root.

The bridge from Poisson arrivals to M/M/1 adds independent exponential service times and a server that works only while nonempty. These additions create the birth-death rates used in balance. The branching lesson changes the structure again: time advances by generations, and independent offspring lines make f(q) the extinction equation. Similar looking fixed-point formulas answer different questions. In the queue, a normalizable stationary law requires ρ<1; in branching, q=1 is always a fixed point, but iteration may select a smaller one.

Proof practice before the capstone

First challenge. On a fair absorbing board 0,…,N, set dᵢ=hᵢ−hᵢ₋₁. The recurrence says dᵢ₊₁=dᵢ, so dᵢ is constant. Summing N differences gives 1=hᴺ−h₀=Nd, hence d=1/N and hᵢ=i/N. This proof needs the fair weights; with bias p≠1/2, the differences follow a ratio instead.

Second challenge. In a two-state chain A→(3/4,1/4), B→(1/2,1/2), let cₜ=P(Xₜ=A). Then cₜ₊₁=(3/4)cₜ+(1/2)(1−cₜ)=1/2+cₜ/4. The fixed value c=2/3 solves c=1/2+c/4. Subtracting gives cₜ₊₁−2/3=(cₜ−2/3)/4, so convergence follows directly. Contrast the alternating chain: its stationary row exists, but its error changes sign without shrinking.

Third challenge. For offspring probabilities (1/4,1/4,1/2), f(s)=1/4+s/4+s²/2 and m=f′(1)=5/4. The fixed points are 1/2 and 1. Starting q₀=0 and applying f repeatedly stays at or below 1/2 and rises toward it. The smaller root is the extinction probability. Reporting 1 because it is a root would ignore the construction of the probability as a limit of finite-generation extinction events.

Capstone: justify four different limits

Part A: a fair walk starts at 3 between absorbing edges 0 and 8. Find the chance of hitting 8 first and justify eventual absorption. Part B: use the two-state matrix A→(4/5,1/5), B→(1/3,2/3). Find its stationary row and show convergence from any initial row without quoting a general theorem. Part C: a toy desk has independent Poisson arrivals at λ=3/hour and exponential service at μ=5/hour, one server, unlimited space. Find the stationary probability of zero and two people and explain what fails when λ=μ. Part D: a creature independently has 0, 1 or 2 children with probabilities 1/3, 1/6, 1/2. Find E[Z₂] from one ancestor and its extinction probability. For every part, identify which numerical statement comes from an exact model derivation and which, if any, would require observation to validate against a real setting.

Model solution

Part A: h₀=0, h₈=1 and hᵢ=(hᵢ₋₁+hᵢ₊₁)/2. The equal-difference proof gives h₃=3/8. From any interior position, eight consecutive left steps have chance 2⁻⁸ and hit an edge within eight steps, so repeated blocks bound nonabsorption by (1−2⁻⁸)ᵏ→0. This establishes eventual absorption under independent fair moves.

Part B: write π=(c,b). Balance across the A–B link gives c/5=b/3; with c+b=1 this gives (5/8,3/8). If cₜ is the A chance, cₜ₊₁=1/3+(7/15)cₜ, hence cₜ₊₁−5/8=(7/15)(cₜ−5/8). The error tends to zero from any c₀ in [0,1], proving convergence for this matrix. The stationary equation by itself would have been insufficient.

Part C: ρ=λ/μ=3/5. Neighboring-state balance gives πₙ=(1−ρ)ρⁿ, so π₀=2/5 and π₂=(2/5)(3/5)²=18/125. At λ=μ, ρ=1 and Σₙ≥0ρⁿ diverges; the weights cannot be normalized to a stationary probability distribution. A queue observed for only an hour still has finite-time probabilities. The stationary statement requires the full M/M/1 assumptions, including independent exponential services.

Part D: m=0·1/3+1·1/6+2·1/2=7/6, hence E[Z₂]=m²=49/36. The offspring generating polynomial is f(s)=1/3+s/6+s²/2. Solving q=f(q) yields 2/3 and 1. Iteration from zero selects the smaller root q=2/3. These are exact model values. To apply A to a real moving object, test whether steps are fair and independent; for B, assess whether its state and transition rows remain valid. To apply C or D to a real desk or species, examine arrival and service times or offspring counts and the relevant independence claims.
