Area and curvature through selected proofs

This thirteen-hour route is a proof workshop with two different meanings of area. In the Euclidean part you will prove claims about vectors, triangle angles and circle angles, then use Pick's lattice formula with its exact hypotheses. In the hyperbolic part you will work inside the Poincaré disk, derive a radial distance, and apply the curvature −1 triangle-defect theorem. The final comparison asks why the same numerical word “area” cannot erase the metric behind a region. This is sustained work on named results, not a complete course in differential or hyperbolic geometry.

Entry recap and scope. You need comfort with Euclidean triangle proofs, coordinate vectors, radians, a basic integral of a rational function, and the meaning of a limit. For vectors u=(a,b), ||u||=√(a²+b²) and u·v=ac+bd. For a lattice polygon, “interior” excludes boundary grid points, and an edge's grid intervals are counted by the gcd of its coordinate changes. For the disk, |z|<1 and a metric scale converts Euclidean coordinate increments into hyperbolic lengths. The linked lessons restore each local notation. The advanced result Gauss–Bonnet will be used with its hypotheses stated; deriving that theorem and proving disk curvature from first principles are outside this route.

As you read, maintain a proof ledger with columns for statement, hypotheses, decisive step and proof status. Label a full proof as such only if every nontrivial dependency was proved or clearly named. An exact worked instance may be valuable without establishing a universal theorem.

Module 1: classical proofs

  1. Why a detour cannot be shorter. Reconstruct the two-dimensional Cauchy–Schwarz step from (ad−bc)²≥0, then derive ||u+v||≤||u||+||v||. Prove the equality condition and test it with opposite arrows. This is a full coordinate proof whose metric is Euclidean length.
  2. Where triangle angle sums come from. State the noncollinearity assumption and explicitly identify the Euclidean parallel-line fact. Reproduce the angle transfer on a fresh triangle without copying the diagram.
  3. The angle at the circumference. Prove the inscribed-angle relation by drawing a diameter through the vertex, and split the argument into the sum and difference cases. Test what changes when the vertex crosses from one open arc to the other.
  4. Count area with lattice points. Count a polygon's boundary points with edge gcds and check its area independently. The article gives a proof sketch through primitive triangles; its primitive-triangle area lemma and complete triangulation bookkeeping are named dependencies, not silently proved.

The bridge in this module is proof structure. The vector inequality starts from algebra, the triangle-angle proof from a parallel line, and the circle theorem from equal radii. Pick's theorem moves from local lattice triangles to a whole polygon by subdivision and gluing. For each result, ask whether an assumption about the plane, the boundary, or the coordinate grid is doing the work. In particular, “area” in Pick means Euclidean area of a simple hole-free lattice polygon; neither a self-crossing path nor an unmarked hole meets that statement.

Module 2: non-Euclidean models

  1. One parallel axiom, different worlds. Record what counts as a line before counting parallels. The sphere uses great circles; the disk uses metric geodesics. The article deliberately stops short of an axiomatic independence proof.
  2. Straight lines inside a curved disk. Derive ∫₀ʳ2/(1−t²)dt=ln((1+r)/(1−r)). Use this to prove radial distance grows without bound. The general classification of disk geodesics is a stated model result.
  3. Triangle area from missing angle. Apply the geodesic-boundary form of Gauss–Bonnet to a curvature −1 triangle. Track where −Area enters the equation and why a nongeodesic boundary would add a term.

This second bridge changes the metric while keeping proof discipline. A disk drawing is finite in Euclidean coordinates yet its radial distances are unbounded. The curvature result then turns a triangle's missing angle into area in that hyperbolic metric. Euclidean grid area and hyperbolic metric area cannot be compared by treating their numeric values as the same physical unit. The two models can each be internally exact while answering different questions.

Worked mixed practice: two areas, two checks

First take the lattice rectangle with vertices (0,0), (3,0), (3,2), (0,2). It has I=2 and B=10, giving A=2+10/2−1=6 Euclidean square units. The coordinate area check is 3·2=6. The equality of these calculations tests point counting and the Pick hypothesis; it does not make Pick's theorem a theorem about all surfaces.

Now take a curvature −1 hyperbolic geodesic triangle with angles π/3, π/4, π/6. Their sum is 3π/4 and the defect is π/4, so Gauss–Bonnet gives hyperbolic area π/4. The calculation is conditional on constant curvature −1 and geodesic sides. The fact that 6 is larger than π/4 says nothing about which region is “visually larger,” because one area is measured on a Euclidean coordinate grid and the other in a hyperbolic metric. If the hyperbolic curvature were −1/4 with the same angle triple, its area would be 4·π/4=π, while the Euclidean rectangle would still have area 6.

For a proof-status check, the rectangle's counts and ordinary area are established directly, while the general Pick formula is a named theorem with only a sketch in the linked article. The hyperbolic area follows algebraically from Gauss–Bonnet, whose general proof is deferred. Record that distinction in the proof ledger.

Capstone: a polygon and a defect

Consider the Euclidean lattice triangle with vertices (0,0), (4,0), (0,3). Determine I and B by direct counting and calculate its area both by Pick and by base-times-height. Then consider a curvature −1 hyperbolic geodesic triangle with angles π/2, π/4, π/8. Determine its angle defect and hyperbolic area. Present a short proof dossier: prove one classical claim used in the route (the Euclidean angle-sum theorem or the 2D triangle inequality), identify the source of the hyperbolic area rule, and explain why the two area figures cannot be merged into one unqualified comparison. Finally, give a failure case for applying each formula without its hypotheses.

Planning hint

For the lattice boundary add gcd(4,0), gcd(4,3) and gcd(0,3). Count interior rows at y=1 and y=2. For the defect convert all angles to eighths of π. Choose a classical proof you can write from its first decisive step.

Model solution

The lattice boundary count is B=4+1+3=8. At y=1 the strict interior points are (1,1) and (2,1); at y=2 only (1,2) is strictly inside. Thus I=3 and Pick gives A=3+8/2−1=6. Independently, the triangle has base 4 and height 3, so A=(4·3)/2=6 Euclidean square units.

The hyperbolic angles add to 4π/8+2π/8+π/8=7π/8. Their defect is π/8, so curvature −1 Gauss–Bonnet gives area π/8 hyperbolic square units. The calculation assumes a genuine geodesic triangle in that constant-curvature plane. Its algebra is complete after invoking Gauss–Bonnet; the theorem itself is not proved by the angle arithmetic.

For a classical proof, let a Euclidean triangle have angles α, β, γ. Draw a parallel to its base through the opposite vertex. The two other sides are transversals, so alternate interior angles beside the vertex equal α and β. Along with γ they fill a straight angle; hence α+β+γ=π. This proof uses the Euclidean parallel-line theorem. It would fail on the sphere, where no great circle through a point can avoid another great circle.

The two numerical areas use different metrics and units. “6 is greater than π/8” is a valid arithmetic statement about numbers, but it is not a scale-free geometric comparison of regions. Pick would fail in its basic form for a lattice polygon with a hole: the 3-by-3 square ring has I=0, B=16 and area 8, whereas I+B/2−1=7. The hyperbolic defect formula would fail for an ordinary Euclidean triangle: its angles add to π at many different positive areas, so assigning defect zero as its area would be absurd.

Self-review rubric. Give one point each for direct lattice counts with no missing edge point, two matching Euclidean area calculations, a correct π/8 defect and conditional area, a full classical proof with its key assumption, and a clear metric distinction plus both failure cases. A complete dossier earns five. A response with correct numbers but an unqualified “therefore both areas are the same kind” misses the central reasoning. Finish with a bounded result

You can stop when your dossier distinguishes full classical proofs, a Pick-theorem application, and a Gauss–Bonnet application without blurring their dependencies. You have calculated exact areas in two geometries and exposed at least one false transfer between them. Continuing into a full hyperbolic metric proof, Gauss–Bonnet, or the lattice lemma behind Pick would require longer courses. This route deliberately leaves those named theorems as visible doors rather than pretending that one capstone closes the whole field.
