Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Build a transformation, then measure what it does

A two-dimensional linear map can turn a square into a slanted parallelogram, reverse its orientation, or flatten it into a segment. Two vectors tell us where every point goes. One number then tells us its signed area scale. This short course connects those descriptions and ends with a transformation you construct yourself.

Allow about 75 minutes for the two lessons and investigations. The scope is real, two-dimensional linear maps through the origin. This is not a full linear algebra course; translations, eigenvectors, and higher-dimensional determinants remain outside our main route.

Background recap: vectors and the standard basis

A vector (x,y) is an ordered pair. Add pairs coordinate by coordinate, and multiply both coordinates when scaling. The vectors e₁ = (1,0) and e₂ = (0,1) form the standard basis: (x,y) = xe₁ + ye₂. A linear map preserves addition and scalar multiplication. Its matrix columns record the images of e₁ and e₂, in that order. The determinant of columns (a,c) and (b,d) is ad − bc. The lessons explain why this expression measures signed area.

Read these two explorations

  1. A linear map from two basis images. Follow T(e₁) = (2,0) and T(e₂) = (1,1). Linearity turns those facts into T(x,y) = (2x+y,y).
  2. Determinant, signed area, and collapse. Connect the two matrix columns to the sides of the transformed unit square. Separate area magnitude from orientation.

The first lesson supplies the map; the second describes one feature of it. Two maps with the same determinant can still move points very differently. Keep an example of an individual vector alongside every area calculation.

From columns to the whole square

A point in the unit square has 0 ≤ x ≤ 1 and 0 ≤ y ≤ 1. Its image is x times the first column plus y times the second. Thus the square's vertices become (0,0), (2,0), (3,1), and (1,1), in order. This is the filled parallelogram generated by the columns, not just its boundary.

T(x,y)=x(2,0)+y(1,1)

The determinant is 2·1 − 1·0 = 2. The transformed unit square has ordinary area two, and the map preserves orientation. That does not say every length doubles: e₁ becomes length two while e₂ becomes length √2. Area scale is a different measurement from length scale.

A useful return check is T(1,2) = (4,2). You can get it from the coordinate formula or from one copy of the first column plus two copies of the second. Agreement connects the symbolic and geometric descriptions.

Worked composition challenge: does order matter?

Introduce the quarter-turn S(x,y) = (−y,x). Compare S∘T, meaning “apply T, then S,” with T∘S. Before opening the solution, predict whether they have the same area scale and whether they send (1,2) to the same point.

A hint

Substitute the output pair of the first map into the second map. To recover columns, evaluate the composite at e₁ and e₂.

Worked solution

Applying T first gives (2x+y,y). Applying S to that pair gives (S∘T)(x,y) = (−y,2x+y). Its columns are (0,2) and (−1,1), so its determinant is 0·1 − (−1)·2 = 2.

Reversing the order gives (T∘S)(x,y) = T(−y,x) = (x−2y,x). Its columns are (1,1) and (−2,0), with determinant 1·0 − (−2)·1 = 2.

For (1,2), the first composite gives (−2,4); the second gives (−3,1). The maps differ although both multiply area by two and preserve orientation. We computed both determinants directly; these two calculations alone are not a proof of a general rule about determinants of compositions.

Optional counterexample hunt: the identity map and S both have determinant one. Identify a vector they move differently. The determinant summarises area and orientation; it does not identify the map.

Construct a map with prescribed signed area

Build a linear map U with U(e₁) = (1,2) and determinant −3. Choose an integer-coordinate image for e₂. Find U(2,−1), the four vertices of the transformed unit square, its ordinary area, and its orientation. Finally, change only the second column to make the entire square collapse.

A hint

Write the second column as (u,v). The determinant is v−2u. For collapse, try making that column a multiple of the first.

Model construction and explanation

Choose U(e₂) = (2,1). Its determinant is 1·1 − 2·2 = −3, as required. Linearity gives U(x,y) = (x+2y,2x+y), so U(2,−1) = (0,3).

The square's vertices become (0,0), (1,2), (3,3), and (2,1), in order. Its ordinary area is |−3| = 3. The negative determinant means orientation reverses; it does not mean ordinary area is negative.

For collapse, replace the second column with (2,4). Then the image of (x,y) is (x+2y)(1,2). Every image lies on the line through (1,2), and the unit square maps to the segment from (0,0) to (3,6). The determinant is 4−4 = 0. The different inputs (2,0) and (0,1) now have the same image, so the map cannot be inverted.

There are many valid non-collapsed constructions: every integer u with v = 2u−3 satisfies v−2u = −3. Different choices have the same signed area scale without being the same map.

Two descriptions worth keeping together

Columns determine a linear map; the determinant describes its signed area scale. You have also seen why equal determinants do not make maps equal and why operation order matters. For a deeper question, try proving the determinant composition rule for arbitrary 2×2 matrices by expanding both sides.
