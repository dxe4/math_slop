Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Reconnect familiar arguments, then test their limits

This nine-hour route is for someone who has previously written analysis proofs and wants to reactivate them. It moves faster than the primer and adds a measured gateway. The concrete outcome is to audit convergence, continuity, Riemann integration and basic Lebesgue examples with correct hypotheses. It does not construct Lebesgue measure or prove dominated convergence in full. Allocate about three hours to nine article readings, three hours to proof audits and mixed work, and three hours to the capstone and repairs.

Entry diagnostic. Without consulting a page, write the ε–N definition of sequence convergence; prove 1/n→0; state the difference between pointwise and uniform convergence; and calculate the upper minus lower Darboux sum of a function that is one on rationals and zero elsewhere. If the first two are shaky, begin with the primer’s first module. If the last two are unfamiliar, use the linked articles as full lessons rather than quick review. You will need the basic meaning of countability and a geometric series for the measure gateway.

Read the three module arcs

  1. Sequences and compactness. Read the explicit tail proof, the rational gap, then the nested-interval subsequence proof. The bridge is completeness: an ε–N argument checks closeness to a proposed limit, while the supremum and nested-interval properties provide a real limit when one must be found. Reproduce the “infinitely many terms” invariant before proceeding.
  2. Functions and convergence. Read uniform continuity, Darboux failure, and uniform convergence. Both uniform notions require one choice that works across an entire domain, but one controls input pairs and the other controls function indices. The rational indicator tests integration by input cells; xⁿ tests whether a moving error peak defeats a shared index. Explain that distinction in your own words.
  3. Measure gateway. Read countable null covers, simple-function integration, and dominated convergence. The first gives a measurable-size insight; the second turns measured value sets into integrals; the third checks a hypothesis that prevents area from escaping a pointwise limit. These lessons assume the standard Lebesgue measure construction. Treat named measure lemmas and the full dominated-convergence theorem as results to apply, not as proofs supplied by this short route.

Twelve quick proof audits and one worked comparison

For each claim, write “valid” or “invalid,” then supply a one-sentence justification or repair. Sequence arc: (1) 1/n→0 because the first hundred terms decrease; (2) a bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence; (3) every bounded rational sequence has a rational convergent subsequence; (4) a rational supremum exists for the positive rationals with square below two. Function arc: (5) x² is uniformly continuous on [−2,2]; (6) x² is uniformly continuous on ℝ; (7) pointwise limits of continuous functions are continuous; (8) uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous. Measure arc: (9) all countable sets are outer-null; (10) every null set is countable; (11) the rational indicator has Lebesgue integral zero on [0,1]; (12) pointwise convergence of integrable functions always passes through the integral.

Audit key

1 invalid: give N>1/ε. 2 valid by real Bolzano–Weierstrass. 3 invalid using rational approximations to √2. 4 invalid by the parity proof and absence of a rational least upper bound. 5 valid via |x+y|≤4. 6 invalid by choosing a large x after δ. 7 invalid via xⁿ on [0,1]. 8 valid by the three-term ε/3 argument. 9 valid via geometric interval budgets. 10 invalid: the Cantor set is uncountable and null. 11 valid because rationals are measurable and null, although the Riemann integral fails. 12 invalid via n·1(0,1/n); domination by an L¹ function is a sufficient repair.

Worked mixed practice. Let fₙ(x)=xⁿ on [0,1]. Find its pointwise limit, decide uniform convergence, and compare ∫fₙ with the integral of its limit using a valid theorem.

Worked solution

For each x<1, xⁿ→0; at x=1 the limit is one. Thus f=0 on [0,1) and f(1)=1. The supremum of |fₙ−f| is one for every n, so convergence is not uniform; continuity is not preserved at 1. Nevertheless 0≤fₙ≤1 and the constant dominator 1 is integrable on this finite interval. Dominated convergence applies and gives ∫fₙ→∫f=0. Direct calculation agrees: ∫₀¹xⁿdx=1/(n+1). Uniform convergence is not needed for this integral conclusion.

Capstone: two integrals, one spike

Compare D=1ℚ∩[0,1] under Darboux and Lebesgue integration, then analyze sₙ=n·1(0,1/n). For each, state measurability or the needed density fact, calculate the relevant quantities exactly, and identify which theorem hypothesis fails or succeeds. Write a short final paragraph explaining why “pointwise zero almost everywhere” is not a sufficient integration rule.

Model solution

Every partition cell contains rational and irrational inputs, so D has infimum zero and supremum one on every cell. Lower Darboux sums are zero and upper sums one, hence no Riemann integral. The rationals are countable and outer-null; in the standard Lebesgue construction they are measurable with measure zero. D is a simple measurable function, so ∫D=1·0=0. For sₙ, the interval (0,1/n) is measurable, ∫sₙ=n(1/n)=1, and sₙ(x)→0 for every fixed x∈[0,1]. The limit has integral zero. There is no common integrable dominator: on dyadic bands near zero, such a function would need values of order 1/x and infinite total integral. Dominated convergence therefore cannot be invoked. In contrast, xⁿ from mixed practice has dominator one in L¹. The difference lies in a hypothesis about total mass, not simply in pointwise behavior.
