Enter the route

This route starts with a familiar act: counting responses and naming a “typical” value. Refresh the arithmetic first. A fraction such as 16/20 describes sixteen yes responses among twenty people who answered; it does not automatically describe all club members. A mean uses every measured value in an arithmetic sum. A median orders values and takes the middle rank. These two centers react differently to an extreme observation. The route needs fractions, simple axes, and the idea that repeated random draws can vary. Each lesson repairs its own notation, so you can begin here without another course.

The bounded outcome is to describe who a small fictional study reached, display and summarize its recorded values, then explain why two honest samples can disagree. This is a descriptive and elementary sampling route. It does not establish a complete inferential toolkit or certify that a particular club poll is unbiased. Work through the articles in order, pausing to calculate from raw counts before reading each answer. Allow roughly four hours including the two lesson exercises per article and the capstone below. Faster reading alone misses the distinction between a calculation and its target population.

One useful connection

The second module asks what to do after the data are collected. Compare the mean with the median on the same sorted list; the median can stay fixed when a largest value grows while the mean moves. Then leave the one observed list and imagine new samples. That change of viewpoint is the bridge from descriptive summaries to sampling variation. A repeated-sampling statement needs a model and a design.

Worked mixed practice

Mixed practice: A group records puzzle minutes 2, 3, 3, 4, 8 from five Saturday attendees. State the target if the organizer asks about all club members, then find the attendee mean and median. The target is all club members; the frame is Saturday attendees, so these five times alone do not represent absent members. Their mean is 20/5=4 minutes and their median is 3 minutes. Replace the 8 by 28: the mean becomes 40/5=8, while the median remains 3. This is the precise sense in which the median resists that one extreme change; the mean is sensitive. Draw one-minute histogram bins if useful, but no choice of bins fixes the frame.

Capstone and model solution

Capstone. Invent a club with 50 members. Twenty attend the meeting; 16 of those prefer a blue puzzle. Thirty are absent; suppose a later complete count finds that 9 prefer blue. The organizer first publishes the meeting-only percentage. Write a short audit naming target, frame, observed sample, meeting rate and full-club rate. Then suppose a different experiment draws four independent tiles with replacement from a jar whose blue chance is 1/2. Give two possible honest samples with different blue proportions and explain what is fixed and what varies. This joins selection bias to random variation without treating them as the same phenomenon.

Show a complete model solution

Model solution: the target is all 50 members, while the initial frame and observed response set are the 20 attendees. The meeting rate is 16/20=80%. In the complete follow-up the blue count is 16+9=25, so the club rate is 25/50=50%. The meeting figure overstates this invented population proportion by 30 percentage points; the cause is the attendance composition in this constructed census. For the jar, one sequence with three blue draws gives p-hat=3/4 and one with one blue gives p-hat=1/4. The model parameter p=1/2 stays fixed. Each exact pattern has positive probability under independent replacement sampling. Variation between these sample statistics is expected even when the jar mechanism is fair.

How to check your work

Outcome to practice: frame awareness is checked by the capstone audit; summary resistance is checked by the 8-to-28 replacement; sample-to-sample variation is checked by the four-draw jar example. Each task requires an explanation, not only a number.

Rubric, ten points: two for naming the target and frame separately; two for both rates and denominators; two for an accurate mean-versus-median distinction in the mixed practice; two for the two valid four-draw examples; two for distinguishing selection bias from random sampling variation. A satisfying stopping point is a correct audit with those distinctions in your own words. If you cannot say what the 80% denominator is, revisit the frame lesson before advancing.
