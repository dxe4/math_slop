Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

From event counts to repeated trials

This route joins eight lessons into one chain: finite event counts, conditional updates, independence, weighted scores, spread, and binomial counts. Set aside about nine hours, including roughly three hours of reading, three hours reproducing article exercises, one hour for the mixed problem, and two hours for the capstone and revision. The route ends with exact finite calculations. It does not yet justify a normal approximation or teach general inference.

Entry recap. A probability is a number between zero and one. For uniform elementary outcomes, divide a favorable count by the full count. P(A|B) uses the B cases as a new denominator, provided P(B)>0. An expectation is a weighted average; variance averages squared deviations from that mean. A binomial count requires a fixed number of independent trials with one constant success chance. Each article refreshes the needed notation, but fluency with fractions and simple algebra will make the route smoother.

Two modules, one probability model

Refresh core ideas

  1. A deck, a die, and the sample space. State the elementary outcomes before declaring any of them equally likely.
  2. Why two chances can overlap. Partition “or” into disjoint regions. This will also help partition all routes to an observed clue.
  3. The clue changes the denominator. Rewrite the joint event as P(A and B)=P(A)P(B|A), then reverse the order and compare.

The bridge to Bayes is the same joint event counted in two directions. P(hidden bag and red) can be read as prior bag chance times red likelihood, or as red chance times the posterior bag chance. No new axiom is needed for the update.

Work across examples

  1. A red marble changes the odds. Recreate its frequency table with a different prior, then check that posterior probabilities total one.
  2. A test for independence, not a feeling. Replacement is a mechanism that keeps trial probabilities fixed; without it, inspect the conditional chance again.
  3. Fair games and expected score. Calculate a weighted score before asking how far a play might depart from it.
  4. Same average, different surprises. Expand the squared deviation and check the shortcut identity.
  5. How many successes in ten tries?. Identify the trial assumptions first, then count success positions with combinations.

There are two distinct meanings of “many” here. Bayes uses many imagined repetitions to make one conditional fraction visible. The binomial law describes a count across n actual model trials. Variance describes the spread of a score or count, while an observed sample may still wander.

Mixed practice: compare a clue and a sequence

Bag A is chosen with chance 1/4 and contains red with chance 3/4. Bag B is chosen with chance 3/4 and contains red with chance 1/4. One red token is shown. Find P(A|red). Then suppose you know B was chosen and replace the token after each of four draws. Find the chance of exactly two reds, and the mean and variance of the red count.

Hint

Multiply each prior by its red likelihood. For the count, use n=4 and p=1/4, with C(4,2)=6.

Worked mixed-practice answer

A contributes (1/4)(3/4)=3/16 to red; B contributes (3/4)(1/4)=3/16. Their sum is 6/16, so P(A|red)=(3/16)/(6/16)=1/2. Despite A being less common, red is more likely there, and the effects balance. Under known B with replacement, X is Binomial(4,1/4). P(X=2)=6(1/4)²(3/4)²=54/256=27/128. Its mean is np=1 and its variance is np(1−p)=4(1/4)(3/4)=3/4. Without replacement, the binomial calculation would require reworking.

Capstone: two bags and ten draws

A hidden bag is chosen: A with chance 1/3, B with chance 2/3. A has red chance 3/4; B has red chance 1/4. A first red draw is observed. Find posterior odds A:B. Next, separately assume B is known and conduct ten fresh draws with replacement. Find the exact chance of exactly three reds, the expected count and variance. Explain why the first update and the ten-draw calculation answer different questions.

Model solution

Prior odds A:B are (1/3):(2/3)=1:2. The red likelihood ratio is (3/4)/(1/4)=3. Posterior odds are 3:2, giving P(A|red)=3/5. In joint-probability terms, A-red has chance (1/3)(3/4)=1/4 and B-red has chance (2/3)(1/4)=1/6; their ratio is (1/4):(1/6)=3:2. The red evidence has positive chance 1/4+1/6=5/12.

Under the separate known-B experiment, X∼Binomial(10,1/4). Its exact three-red chance is C(10,3)(1/4)³(3/4)⁷ = 120·3⁷/4¹⁰, approximately 0.2503. E[X]=10/4=5/2 and Var(X)=10(1/4)(3/4)=15/8. The first result asks which hidden bag produced one clue. The second assumes a known bag and counts successes in repeated independent draws. Treating posterior 3/5 as the per-draw red chance would mix those different roles.

A strong stopping point

You have connected event arithmetic with finite distributions. The next route adds proof practice and a qualified normal approximation. For now, be able to explain why a posterior bag probability is not automatically the success probability of every later draw. That distinction is more useful than memorizing several formulas without their assumptions.
