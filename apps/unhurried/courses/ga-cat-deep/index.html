Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Scope of this proof route

This fifteen-hour route is a selected proof study of universal constructions in Set and Mon. It assumes that you can read category and functor definitions, prove equality of functions, work with monoids and track contravariant arrows. The outcome is a dossier of explicit proofs: products and pullbacks in Set, the free-monoid/underlying-set hom-set correspondence, one finite Yoneda instance, and interchange for Cartesian product. It does not certify broad mastery of category theory. General Yoneda, general limits and monoidal coherence remain named boundaries.

Use a proof ledger with four columns: object and arrow types; hypotheses; existence construction; uniqueness or law check. A formula whose source and target do not match should be corrected before any calculation. For a product or pullback, always identify the proposed universal object, its fixed projection arrows and the arbitrary test object. For Yoneda, write Cop next to each contravariant functor. The time estimate includes rebuilding proofs and the capstone, not merely reading six articles.

Module 1: Universal constructions

  1. Pairing as a universal property is the starting proof. Hide its explanation and derive the mediator ⟨u,v⟩(t)=(u(t),v(t)) from the projection equations. Then prove uniqueness without assuming T finite. The bridge to pullbacks is that a product accepts every pair of arrows sharing a domain.
  2. A pullback matches shared data adds a common codomain Z and the condition f∘u=g∘v. Rebuild the matching subset P of X×Y. Before asserting the same pairing formula yields a mediator, show membership in P for every t. Record separately the square's commutativity, mediator existence and mediator uniqueness.
  3. Free construction and forgetful map shifts from matching pairs to extending generators. Here the arbitrary test datum is h:X→U(M), and the unique mediator is a homomorphism ĥ:F(X)→M. The bridge is the repeated pattern “given compatible input arrows, one factorization is forced,” though the categories and direction of the universal arrow differ. Check both the empty word and concatenation; prove the inverse hom-set maps, then inspect naturality in X and M.

After this module, compare the three existence formulas. A product's mediator takes t to (u(t),v(t)). A pullback's formula is identical but requires the compatibility equation to land in the subset. A free-monoid extension sends a word x1…xn to h(x1)···h(xn), and requires associativity plus the identity. None is proved by simply drawing its universal diagram.

Module 2: Natural structure

  1. A translation between translations repairs the naturality equation in a finite path category. The bridge to adjunction is that its hom-set bijection is natural in both variables, so changing generators or target monoid must commute with extension. Write the source and target of each side before simplifying.
  2. Know an object by its maps demands the most careful variance check. HA=HomC(−,A) and F both have domain Cop. Reproduce the two-object proof, then label the article's general argument as a sketch. The course assesses a finite instance and a correctly typed statement; it does not ask you to claim a fully proved general lemma.
  3. Put processes side by side supplies a different form of preservation: Cartesian product sends pairs of functions to a function on pairs, with interchange relating parallel and sequential composition. Prove its pointwise equality, and distinguish a canonical associator bijection from literal equality of nested pairs. This closes the route by returning to explicit Set calculations.

The common thread in this second module is compatibility under arrows. Naturality asks whether component comparisons commute with source arrows. Yoneda asks which such compatible families emerge from one element of F(A). Cartesian interchange asks whether a product operation preserves composition. These are distinct claims; retain their individual types in your notes.

Worked mixed practice before the dossier

Let X={a,b}, Y={r,s}, Z={0,1}; set f(a)=0, f(b)=1, g(r)=0, g(s)=1. The product X×Y has four pairs, but P={(a,r),(b,s)}. Take T={t} with u(t)=a, v(t)=r; the compatible cone has the unique mediator t↦(a,r). Now use the same two-letter set X={a,b} as generators of F(X), and let M=({0,1}, addition mod 2), assigning a↦1 and b↦0. The unique extension sends aba to 1+0+1=0 and ε to 0. These are two different universal constructions: the first matches two arrows into a common key set; the second extends one function into a homomorphism. Their uniqueness proofs differ as well: projection coordinates force a pair, while the homomorphism law forces every word from its letters.

For a naturality check, let C have one nonidentity arrow a:0→1, choose A=1, and let a contravariant F have F(1)={r,s}, F(0)={x,y}, F(a)(r)=x, F(a)(s)=y. The represented functor H1 has singletons H1(1)={11} and H1(0)={a}. Choosing r at 1 forces x at 0. This is a complete finite Yoneda instance; it illustrates the way naturality forces a whole compatible family from one value.

Capstone: a proof dossier with three universal arrows

Task. Submit three short proofs: (I) the product of arbitrary sets X and Y, (II) the pullback of arbitrary f:X→Z and g:Y→Z, and (III) the free-monoid extension for arbitrary h:X→U(M). For each, draw or describe the arrow types, give a construction, verify the required equations and prove uniqueness. Add an appendix checking the finite Yoneda instance in the preceding paragraph and one Cartesian interchange equation. Say which broader theorems you did not prove.

Model solution, I. Use X×Y with πX:X×Y→X and πY:X×Y→Y. Given u:T→X and v:T→Y, define m:T→X×Y by m(t)=(u(t),v(t)). Each projection composite returns the corresponding function at every t. If n has the same composites, its first and second coordinates at t are u(t) and v(t), so n(t)=m(t) for every t. Thus n=m. This proof includes T empty: the unique empty function is the mediator if the given maps exist.

Model solution, II. Set P={(x,y)∈X×Y:f(x)=g(y)} with projections pX:P→X and pY:P→Y. Its square commutes because each member of P satisfies the equality. If u:T→X and v:T→Y obey f∘u=g∘v, then (u(t),v(t))∈P for every t, so m(t)=(u(t),v(t)) defines m:T→P. The projections recover u and v. Any n with those projection equations has those same coordinates at every t and equals m. The membership step is where pullback differs from product.

Model solution, III. F(X) consists of finite words, with empty word ε and concatenation; i:X→U(F(X)) makes a letter a one-letter word. For h:X→U(M), define ĥ(ε)=eM and ĥ(x1…xn)=h(x1)···h(xn) in order. Associativity and the identity in M show ĥ is a homomorphism; U(ĥ)∘i=h. Any other homomorphism with this restriction must send ε to e and each word to the same ordered product of its letter images. Hence it equals ĥ. Restriction and extension are inverse, yielding HomMon(F(X),M)≅HomSet(X,U(M)). This result concerns the free monoid and the forgetful functor specifically.

Model appendix. For the stated two-object C, the value of a natural η:H1⇒F at 11 is either r or s. Naturality at a forces η0(a)=F(a)(η1(11)), namely x or y. Each choice yields a natural transformation, and identities add no constraint; thus there are exactly two, in bijection with F(1). For typed f:A→B, g:B→C, f′:A′→B′ and g′:B′→C′, both (g×g′)∘(f×f′) and (g∘f)×(g′∘f′) send (a,a′) to (g(f(a)),g′(f′(a′))). Hence interchange holds. The dossier proves these Set and Mon cases and the finite Yoneda instance. It does not prove arbitrary-category product existence, general Yoneda or monoidal coherence.

Rubric. Give three points each for product and pullback arrow types, existence and uniqueness; three for the free-monoid identity, concatenation and uniqueness checks; two for the finite Yoneda variance and complete instance; one for a typed interchange calculation; and one for clear proof boundaries, thirteen points total. A proof that only lists finite matching pairs cannot receive the general pullback points. A word map that omits ε cannot receive the monoid-identity point.
