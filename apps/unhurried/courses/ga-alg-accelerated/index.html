Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Rebuild a working algebra toolkit

This selected core route is for someone who remembers arithmetic and basic proof moves but wants to reconnect group and ring language. It starts with symmetries, uses cosets and homomorphisms to understand group structure, and ends with ideals and the difference between a ring and a field. Allow about eight hours: roughly four for reading and annotation, two for article exercises, and two for the mixed proof and capstone. This is a refresher on named results, not a replacement for a semester of algebra.

Entry check. Be able to read f(g(x)), compute remainders, identify gcd(5,12)=1, and follow an argument of the form “suppose a and b lie in a subset, then show their product lies there.” A group operation has an identity and inverses; a ring also has multiplication distributing over addition. The lessons repair details locally, so you can begin even if you have forgotten notation. Keep a separate sheet for conditions: finite for Lagrange, normal for group quotients, ideal for ring quotients, prime n≥2 for the full field claim.

Module 1: Groups

  1. A square as an algebra machine. Use labelled vertex images to test composition. The noncommuting turn and reflection prevent you from silently assuming all groups are abelian.
  2. How long until a symmetry returns?. Connect repeated action to a cyclic subgroup. The number of distinct powers is the element order, setting up a subgroup size for the next proof.
  3. Why subgroup sizes divide. Read the disjoint-or-equal coset argument line by line. It converts the concrete cyclic subgroup into the divisibility statement ord(g)| |G|.
  4. A map that respects the operation. Shift from subgroups inside a group to maps between groups. Reduction Z→Z/nZ has kernel nZ; its fibers are cosets. This explains why repeated inputs can share one output without losing operation structure.

The bridge across these four lessons is an audit of what each construction preserves. A symmetry group keeps actual motions. A cyclic subgroup keeps powers of one motion. A coset is a block of inputs with equal relation to H. A homomorphism preserves operation but can collapse distinct inputs. The word “kernel” names exactly the inputs collapsed to identity. None of these facts by itself licenses multiplication of arbitrary cosets; the deep route treats normality explicitly.

Module 2: Rings

  1. Polynomials are numbers with a variable. Track coefficients rather than only input-output tables. Distributivity is inherited from finite coefficient sums, and over F₂ the polynomials x² and x are different despite agreeing as functions.
  2. Remainders as quotient rings. An ideal absorbs multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. In Z every ideal is nZ, and that extra absorption makes product of residue classes independent of representatives.
  3. Why division sometimes fails modulo n. A field requires an inverse for every nonzero element. Bézout proves the unit criterion, while a composite n supplies a nonzero zero-divisor pair.

Here the bridge is from a ring's two operations to a legal collapse of both operations. Additive subgroups alone explain modular addition, but ideals explain why multiplication descends. A quotient ring may or may not be a field; the prime-modulus criterion decides the integer case. Work a new modulus such as 10 before reading the capstone solution.

Worked mixed example: a map, a subgroup and a unit

Let ρ:Z→Z/10Z send k to [k]. It is an additive homomorphism because ρ(a+b)=[a+b]=[a]+[b]. Its kernel is 10Z and its image is all ten classes. Under addition, [4] generates {[0],[4],[8],[2],[6]}; the first return is at 5[4]=[20]=[0], so its order is 5. Lagrange agrees: 5 divides the additive group's size 10. Under multiplication, however, [4] has no inverse because gcd(4,10)=2; [4][5]=[0]. One element can have perfectly good additive order and still fail multiplicative division. Confusing the operations would make all three conclusions seem contradictory when they are compatible.

For proof practice, justify the Lagrange step rather than cite it blindly: cosets of the five-element generated subgroup each have five members, and distinct cosets do not overlap. Here the second coset is [1]+⟨[4]⟩={[1],[3],[5],[7],[9]}, giving 10=2·5. That finite partition is a model of the general argument, not its whole proof.

Why seven divides and six does not

Explain why every nonzero residue supports multiplication-based division in Z/7Z while Z/6Z fails. Your response must (a) prove an inverse for an arbitrary nonzero [a] modulo seven, (b) exhibit a zero divisor modulo six, (c) locate both examples in the ideal/quotient picture, and (d) state why Lagrange's theorem is not itself a field test. As a separate short proof check, rederive the equal-coset count for a subgroup of a finite group and identify a false converse to Lagrange.

Hint

For prime seven, gcd(a,7)=1 whenever [a]≠[0], then invoke Bézout. For six, try [2][3]. In the proof check, focus on whether cosets can overlap partially.

Model solution

If [a] is nonzero modulo seven, seven does not divide a. Since seven is prime, gcd(a,7)=1. Bézout supplies integers u,v with ua+7v=1, so [u][a]=[1]. Thus every nonzero class has an inverse; Z/7Z is a field. Modulo six, [2] and [3] are nonzero but [2][3]=[6]=[0]. If [2] had an inverse, multiplying this equation by it would force [3]=[0], a contradiction. Both rings are quotient rings Z/(7) and Z/(6): 7Z and 6Z are ideals, so products of cosets are well-defined even where division fails. Lagrange concerns subgroup orders under a group operation. It tells us, for instance, that an element's additive order divides seven or six, but it does not establish multiplicative inverses for all nonzero residues.

For the proof check, if H≤G, the map h↦gh bijects H with each left coset gH. If aH meets bH, write ah₁=bh₂; then b=a h₁h₂−1 and bH=aH. Cosets therefore partition finite G into [G:H] blocks, each of size |H|, proving |G|=[G:H]|H|. The converse is false in general. For a concrete example, A₄ is the group of even permutations of four symbols. It has 12 elements: the identity, eight 3-cycles, and three double transpositions. It has no subgroup of order 6: such a subgroup would have index two and be normal, producing a nontrivial quotient homomorphism A₄→C₂. Every 3-cycle must map to the identity because C₂ has no element of order three. The 3-cycles generate A₄: for example (12)(34)=(132)(134), and relabelling gives the other double transpositions. Therefore every element would map to the identity, contradicting the proposed nontrivial quotient. Hence no order-six subgroup exists. This counterexample goes beyond the capstone's field calculation but tests the logical direction of Lagrange.

Rubric. Full work gives a Bézout-based arbitrary-element argument for seven, an explicit nonzero zero-divisor pair for six, the two named ideals and their role in well-defined multiplication, and a clear distinction between group-size divisibility and field inverses. The proof check must account for coset equality after overlap, not merely count a diagram. A false converse needs an actual group example and an explanation, not just the word “false.”
