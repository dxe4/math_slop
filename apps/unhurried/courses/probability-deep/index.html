Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Finite probability with proof boundaries

This deep route uses all nine probability lessons. It asks you to prove finite identities, derive a conditional update and moment formula, and state precisely what a smooth approximation does and does not say. Allow about sixteen hours: approximately four for close reading, four for redoing the lesson exercises without hints, four for the proofs below, and four for the capstone, exact calculations and revision. It is sustained practice within a bounded topic, not a research qualification or a complete measure-theoretic probability course.

Entry recap. A probability space assigns nonnegative masses summing to one to elementary outcomes. For events A and B, P(A∩B) is the mass of outcomes in both; conditional probability divides that joint mass by P(B) when P(B)>0. A finite random variable maps outcomes to numerical scores. Its expectation is a probability-weighted sum, and variance is mean squared deviation. A binomial count comes from independent Bernoulli trials with constant p. These definitions appear locally in the lessons; the course adds the connections and proof tasks.

Three modules and their bridges

Foundations

  1. A deck, a die, and the sample space. Prove the count ratio only after establishing equal elementary masses.
  2. Why two chances can overlap. Reprove the union identity by partitioning into three disjoint regions; it survives unequal elementary masses.
  3. The clue changes the denominator. Check the positive-denominator hypothesis and derive the multiplication identity.

Bridge: these are not three unrelated formulas. Additivity handles disjoint partitions, and conditioning renormalizes a positive-probability subspace. Their combination will turn an observed clue into an update on a hidden cause.

Mechanisms and examples

  1. A red marble changes the odds. Derive the odds ratio by writing the same joint masses from both directions.
  2. A test for independence, not a feeling. Verify the product equality from a model; test the boundary when one event has probability zero.
  3. Fair games and expected score. Prove finite linearity by expanding a sum over elementary outcomes, including dependent scores.

Bridge: independence simplifies products, whereas expectation linearity needs no independence. Keep that distinction explicit when you move from one score to a sum of trial indicators.

Limits and deeper questions

  1. Same average, different surprises. Expand the square to prove the shortcut identity and inspect a same-mean counterexample.
  2. How many successes in ten tries?. Count sequences, then derive mean and variance using indicators.
  3. When a bell curve is a useful sketch. State the fixed-p central limit result and distinguish the theorem from a finite-n accuracy guarantee.

The final bridge is from exact finite distribution to an approximation. A histogram can suggest shape but cannot prove convergence. Standardization gives a common scale; a separate limit theorem gives the qualified result.

Proof practice before the capstone

Task 1. For events A and B with P(B)>0, prove P(A|B)P(B)=P(B|A)P(A), assuming also P(A)>0. Then explain what survives if P(A)=0.

Hint for task 1

Write each side as the joint mass. The second conditional probability needs a positive denominator.

Worked proof for task 1

By definition P(A|B)P(B)=P(A∩B). With P(A)>0, P(B|A)P(A)=P(B∩A), which is the same event mass. If P(A)=0, the first equality still gives P(A∩B)=0 because A∩B⊆A, but P(B|A) is undefined. One cannot retain the displayed two-conditional formula without repairing its hypotheses.

Task 2. Let I₁,…,Iₙ be independent success indicators with chance p. Derive the mean and variance of X=∑Iᵢ.

Hint for task 2

An indicator satisfies Iᵢ²=Iᵢ. Expand the square of X−E[X] or use pairwise covariance terms.

Worked proof for task 2

E[Iᵢ]=p, so linearity gives E[X]=np without independence. Since Iᵢ²=Iᵢ, Var(Iᵢ)=E[Iᵢ²]−E[Iᵢ]²=p−p²=p(1−p). Expanding Var(∑Iᵢ) yields the sum of individual variances plus twice the sum of covariances. Independence makes E[IᵢIⱼ]=E[Iᵢ]E[Iⱼ] for distinct i,j, so every covariance vanishes. Therefore Var(X)=np(1−p). If trials are dependent, the covariance terms remain and the formula need not hold.

Capstone: one finite game, exact bars and a smooth sketch

Choose bag A with chance 2/5 and B with chance 3/5. A produces red with chance 3/4 and B with chance 1/4. First prove the posterior odds A:B after red. Then, in a new experiment using a known B bag, make 40 independent red-or-not draws with replacement. Derive the mean and variance of the red count, give an exact finite expression for P(X≤15), and give a continuity-corrected normal estimate. Explain a failure case with the same n but p=0.01, and distinguish the exact identity from the approximation.

Model solution

The two red joint masses are (2/5)(3/4)=3/10 and (3/5)(1/4)=3/20. Their sum is 9/20, which is positive. Conditional definition gives P(A|red)=(3/10)/(9/20)=2/3 and P(B|red)=1/3. Hence posterior odds A:B are 2:1. Equivalently, prior odds are 2:3 and the red likelihood ratio is 3, giving 2:1. This derivation assumes the two bags exhaust the possible hidden choices and that the stated priors and likelihoods are the model.

For known B and replacement, X∼Binomial(40,1/4). Using indicator sums, E[X]=40/4=10 and Var(X)=40(1/4)(3/4)=7.5, so σ=√7.5≈2.739. The exact probability is ∑ from k=0 to 15 of C(40,k)(1/4)ᵏ(3/4)⁴⁰⁻ᵏ. This expression is finite and exact; evaluating its terms gives about 0.9737551. The continuity-corrected normal sketch uses z=(15.5−10)/√7.5≈2.008 and gives Φ(2.008)≈0.9777. The numerical difference is about 0.004, so the sketch is useful but visibly imperfect. That number is an approximation, not a replacement definition of the binomial event.

With p=0.01 and n=40, the mean is 0.4 and most mass lies at zero. The distribution is strongly right-skewed, while a normal curve is symmetric and assigns positive area to negative counts. The fixed-p central limit statement concerns n growing with p fixed; it does not certify accuracy here. Even for p=1/4, exact binomial arithmetic is preferable if a small tail error would matter.

Rubric. Award one check each for a valid positive-denominator Bayes derivation; posterior odds consistent with posterior probabilities; an explicit replacement and independence assumption; the indicator argument for both moments; an exact binomial sum with bounds and exponents; a clearly labelled continuity correction and approximate normal value; and a concrete explanation of the extreme-p failure. A complete response earns all seven checks and uses “proof” only for the finite identities. The central limit theorem is cited as a theorem with conditions, not proved by these bars.

Where the proof route stops

You can now justify the finite algebra used in a small probability model and state when a normal approximation is only a sketch. The course has not proved the central limit theorem, nor has it covered general continuous probability spaces. If you can reproduce the two proof tasks and the capstone without the model solution, that is a strong stopping point before a more advanced text.
