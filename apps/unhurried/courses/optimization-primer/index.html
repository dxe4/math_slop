Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Turn a choice into a justified answer

This route begins with a sheet of card and ends with a small resource-allocation model. The thread is simple: name the quantity to optimize, state every feasible choice, find candidates, then say what proves that one candidate is best. A nice-looking graph is useful for orientation, but the final answer must survive arithmetic and a stated argument.

Entry recap. A function f(x) assigns a value to a choice x. A maximum is the largest feasible value; a minimum is the smallest. For a closed interval, endpoints belong to the problem. A straight line ax+by=c has constant objective value along it. If you need a reminder of derivative notation, f′(x) is the limit of secant slopes as a second input approaches x; the first lesson carries out the needed derivative calculation. You need algebra, coordinates and reading inequalities. The convexity article explains its own segment notation.

Allow about 230 minutes: roughly 90 for four linked lessons, 55 for the six short checks described below, 60 for the capstone, and 25 for revision. This is a bounded introduction to modeling and certificates. Stop after the capstone when you can explain why your chosen point is feasible and best; gradient algorithms and general nonlinear programming are later topics.

2. Candidates and guarantees

Then explain in a sentence why f′=0 finds a possible interior answer but cannot by itself prove a global one. The next module introduces geometry that sometimes supplies the missing global comparison.

3. Geometry

Read The special promise of a convex bowl, then Why the best mix often sits at a corner. The first is about a convex objective on a convex domain: local minima become global. The second is about a linear objective on a bounded feasible polygon: at least one vertex attains the optimum. Keep these statements distinct. A linear objective may tie along an entire edge. A convex function need not attain its infimum on an open set.

For six short checks, do two model checks (dimensions and feasible intervals), two candidate checks (one endpoint and one stationary nonminimum), and two geometry checks (a chord inequality and a tied optimal edge). The article exercises supply worked examples. Do them on paper before opening their solutions; correct the exact failed inequality or missing endpoint, rather than merely marking an answer wrong.

Worked mixed practice

A planner chooses x,y≥0 with x+y≤6 and x≤4, maximizing 2x+y. Start by deciding what is feasible, then find a vertex answer and a direct upper bound. This mixes the feasible-set habit from the box, the global-certification habit from local versus global minima, and the polygon method.

Hint

Use 2x+y=x+(x+y). Each parenthesis has a bound from a constraint.

Worked solution

The inequalities describe a bounded polygon. Since x≤4 and x+y≤6, the objective satisfies 2x+y=x+(x+y)≤4+6=10 for every feasible pair. The pair (4,2) is nonnegative, obeys x=4 and x+y=6, and attains 10. Thus it is globally optimal. The vertices are (0,0), (4,0), (4,2), and (0,6), with objective values 0, 8, 10, and 6. Vertex comparison agrees with the inequality certificate. Merely finding (4,2) as an intersection would have identified a candidate, not yet proved optimality.

Now change the objective to x+y. The same feasible region has every point on x+y=6 with 0≤x≤4 as an optimum. This countercheck prevents the “always one corner” misconception. The polygon theorem says some vertex optimum exists; it does not promise uniqueness.

Capstone: two items competing for capacity

A stall can make x small kits and y large kits, with nonnegative real quantities in this idealized model. Each small kit uses one unit of packing time; each large kit also uses one. Total time is at most 8 units. A small kit uses two units of a second resource and a large kit one, with at most 10 units available. Rewards are 4 per small kit and 3 per large kit. Form a two-variable linear program; draw or describe its feasible polygon; find the best reward and all maximizers. Then answer a sensitivity question: if the reward for a small kit falls from 4 to 3, what changes?

Model solution

The constraints are x≥0, y≥0, x+y≤8 and 2x+y≤10; maximize R=4x+3y. The vertices are (0,0), (5,0), (2,6), and (0,8). The non-axis intersection comes from subtracting x+y=8 from 2x+y=10, giving x=2 and y=6. Rewards at the vertices are 0, 20, 26 and 24, so the best is (2,6) with reward 26. A direct certificate is R=2(x+y)+(2x+y)≤2·8+10=26, with equality exactly when both resource inequalities are equalities. Their unique intersection is (2,6), so the optimum is unique.

When the small-kit reward becomes 3, the objective is R′=3x+3y=3(x+y)≤24. Every feasible point on x+y=8 is optimal. The second resource condition there is 2x+(8−x)=8+x≤10, so 0≤x≤2 and y=8−x. The winning set becomes the entire edge from (0,8) to (2,6); the value changes from 26 to 24. This is a sensitivity result derived from inequalities, not from redrawing the picture alone.

Rubric. Give one credit each for a correct objective and feasible set, complete vertices, checked objective values, a global certificate, and the sensitivity edge with its bounds. Five credits means the model and conclusion are independently justified. If only the vertex list is correct, revisit why bounded-polygon geometry covers every interior point. If the sensitivity answer names one point, check equality along the full edge.

A satisfying stopping point

You have completed the route when you can write a feasible set, reject invalid candidates, compare all vertices of a bounded polygon and give an inequality proving your answer. The box also shows how an interior derivative candidate can be fully certified by a sign argument.
