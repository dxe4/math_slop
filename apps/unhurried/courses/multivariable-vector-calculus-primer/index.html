Read a landscape, then follow a force

This route develops four connected ideas: a contour describes equal height, a gradient predicts first-order change, a polar coordinate cell has a changing area, and a field's work is accumulated along an oriented path. These selected tools give a useful introduction to multivariable thinking. The boundary theorems and their general proofs are beyond this primer.

Set aside about five hours for the four lessons, their exercises, the short tasks here, and a capstone. A reader who already recalls one-variable differentiation and integration, coordinate vectors, and dot products can start directly. If not, pause over this recap: a dot product (a,b)·(c,d)=ac+bd is positive when a force helps a movement and negative when it opposes it. A derivative is a limiting change per unit step, while an integral accumulates contributions. In two dimensions, a step has a direction as well as a size; that extra choice is the recurring theme.

The scope is concrete. You will compare directions on the quadratic height field f=x²+2y², compute a simple polar integral, and distinguish a gradient field from a route-dependent field. You will not be asked to infer general physical behavior from a drawing. Make a small coordinate sketch if it helps, but keep exact calculations beside it.

Three modules, with a bridge after each

Landscapes

  1. Reading a landscape without seeing the mountain. Read the level-set equation and compare finite east and north steps. The first bridge question is: does one contour tell you which way is steepest? It tells where height is unchanged, but not a first-order slope at an isolated point. Work the two exercises before moving on.

Local change

  1. A compass that points uphill. The gradient answers the previous question when the function is differentiable. Bridge from a contour to a direction: for f=x²+2y², at (1,0) the gradient is (2,0), perpendicular to the local contour there. The derivative along the unit east direction is 2; along north it is 0. This is a local statement. A longer northward move still raises height by a quadratic amount.

Coordinates and paths

  1. Why polar coordinates need an extra r. The factor r answers a different question: how much ordinary area a small coordinate cell represents. Bridge from slopes to accumulation by noticing that both require local information before totals can be formed. A gradient converts a tiny displacement into predicted height change; a Jacobian converts tiny coordinate area into physical area.
  2. Does a round trip cost any work?. Here the small contribution is force dotted with path displacement. Integrating along a curve accumulates those contributions, including signs. Bridge back to gradients: if the field equals ∇φ along the whole path, work is the endpoint change in φ. A polar area integral sums over a region; a work integral sums along a route. Do not interchange their differential elements.

Mixed practice: keep three meanings apart

At p=(1,0), use f=x²+2y². First find the derivative in the unit direction u=(3/5,4/5). Then compute the area of the radius-1 disk by polar coordinates. Finally compute the work of F=∇f=(2x,4y) along r(t)=(t,t), 0≤t≤1. Before revealing the answer, label each result as a rate, an area, or work.

Worked mixed solution

At p, ∇f=(2,0), so the directional rate is (2,0)·(3/5,4/5)=6/5 height units per unit distance. The disk area is ∫₀²π∫₀¹ r dr dθ=π square units. Along r(t), F=(2t,4t), r′=(1,1), and the work is ∫₀¹6t dt=3 in force-distance units. Endpoint checking gives f(1,1)−f(0,0)=3. Although π and 3 both arise from integrals, their domains and units differ.

To self-check setup, write what is varying before every integral: θ and r for area, t for route position. If the directional vector is not unit length, normalize it before using “steepest per unit distance.” If your path work changes after reparametrizing the same oriented route, revisit the derivative r′(t).

Capstone: two routes up the same hill

A traveler starts at A=(0,0) and ends at B=(1,1) on f(x,y)=x²+2y². Route C₁ is r(t)=(t,t), 0≤t≤1. Route C₂ goes first from (0,0) to (1,0), then to (1,1), each leg with parameter t from 0 to 1. For each route compute work done by F=∇f. Compare the first-order uphill rate at A and at B along the diagonal unit direction (1/√2,1/√2). Explain why different local rates can coexist with equal total work.

Model capstone solution

F=(2x,4y). Along C₁, F(r(t))=(2t,4t) and r′=(1,1), so work is ∫₀¹6t dt=3. Along C₂'s horizontal leg, F=(2t,0) and velocity is (1,0), giving ∫₀¹2t dt=1. On its vertical leg, F=(2,4t) and velocity (0,1), giving ∫₀¹4t dt=2. Total is also 3, equal to f(B)−f(A)=3. At A the gradient is zero, so diagonal first-order rate is 0. At B the gradient is (2,4), giving (2+4)/√2=3√2. Rates vary along a route; their signed integral can still equal the same endpoint difference for a gradient field.

Self-review rubric. A complete capstone has both path parametrizations with correct velocity, work values 3 and 3, diagonal rates 0 and 3√2, and an explanation that endpoint equality follows from F=∇f on the whole plane. A correct numerical total with no path setup has not yet shown how a line integral works. If the rate and work units get mixed, repeat the mixed practice. This is a satisfying stopping point: you can now interpret selected local and accumulated multivariable quantities without treating an illustration as a proof.
