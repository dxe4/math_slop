Three ways to control an infinite claim

This is a sustained proof workshop, about thirteen hours including exercises and a proof portfolio. It assumes you have written a direct proof and an ordinary induction proof before. Its named outcomes are to write strong induction, use an invariant to prove impossibility, and construct a bijection. The route also tests whether you can say what each method does not establish. It is a selected window into foundations, not a comprehensive credential in logic or set theory.

Entry recap. To prove a universal claim about positive integers, a finite test is insufficient. Induction connects all cases with a verified base and step. A legal-move system is a set of states with specified transitions; a property preserved by every transition is an invariant. A function is a bijection when it is injective, meaning no two inputs collide, and surjective, meaning every target is hit. Keep those obligations separate throughout. If a proof appears to say “obvious,” ask which definition or earlier theorem supplies the missing step.

Module 1: Inductive structure

  1. Induction is a chain with a base. Reconstruct the formula for 1 + ··· + n, writing the assumed case and the next target on different lines. The point is the logic of the transition, not speed of algebra.
  2. When the next step needs every earlier step. In the prime-factorization proof, mark where two smaller factor claims enter. Write “existence” above the conclusion and list the additional divisibility lemma a uniqueness proof would need.

The bridge is not a stronger conclusion; it is a better fit between the hypothesis and recursive structure. Ordinary induction gives the preceding case. Strong induction lets the step invoke every earlier case. Both are valid because a hypothetical first failure would have all previous cases true. A factor tree can branch to 5 and 17 while proving something about 85, so the previous single integer 84 is not the useful input. Nevertheless, neither form of induction allows you to assume the new case itself. Prove every branch decreases, and keep the result at “a prime product exists.”

Module 2: What persists

  1. An invariant says what cannot happen. Rewrite the +4/−6 move proof using induction on route length. Then add a new legal move and retest preservation rather than carrying the old conclusion over automatically.
  2. Existence and uniqueness are two jobs. Audit the quotient–remainder proof: it rules out a second valid pair by bounding a multiple of b. The general construction of a first pair is a distinct obligation, only sketched in the article.

These lessons connect through proof scope. An invariant is a necessary condition for reachability: if a target violates it, impossibility follows; if a target satisfies it, you still need a route. A uniqueness argument is also conditional: if two objects meet the conditions, they coincide; it does not guarantee that even one exists. In both cases the logical direction is easy to reverse by accident. Write “necessary” or “at most one” next to the corresponding conclusion until you can state it without that reminder.

Module 3: Infinity through maps

  1. Finite examples and infinite claims. Check f(n) = 2n twice: cancellation proves no collisions, and the definition of an even positive number supplies a preimage for every target.

The final bridge returns to existence and uniqueness language. Surjectivity says a preimage exists for each target. Injectivity says there is at most one preimage for each target; it does not supply existence. Together they make a bijection. A five-row table can suggest the pattern but cannot satisfy either infinite obligation. The proof’s arbitrary variables are doing the work. The example does not settle the cardinality of the real numbers or any unmentioned infinite set.

Worked mixed practice: a move system and a map

A counter starts at 2 and allows +6 or −4 at each move. A friend says every reachable state is even, so 8 must be reachable. Assess the reasoning and the conclusion. Then consider g(n) = 2n + 2 from the positive integers to positive even integers. Decide whether it is injective and surjective. In both parts, distinguish a necessary condition from the witness that establishes a positive claim.

Hint

Both moves preserve parity, but try to exhibit a route to 8. For the map, ask whether 2 has a positive integer preimage.

Worked solution

Every reachable state is even: the start 2 is even and both changes are even, so induction on the number of moves preserves parity. This alone does not prove 8 reachable; the friend reverses “reachable ⇒ even.” Here 8 does happen to be reachable in one move, 2 → 8 by +6. That explicit route proves it. For g, if 2a + 2 = 2b + 2 then a = b, so it is injective. It is not surjective onto all positive evens: 2 would require 2n + 2 = 2, hence n = 0, outside the positive-integer domain. The first few outputs, 4, 6, 8, …, make the missing target clear but the general preimage equation is the proof.

Now revisit strong induction with a different construction. Claim: every integer n ≥ 2 can be written as a sum of 2s and 3s. This claim is false at no starting index, but a step that relies only on n − 2 requires two bases: 2 = 2 and 3 = 3. From a representation of n − 2, append a 2 to represent n for every n ≥ 4. This short argument demonstrates that the base range must match the step size. A proof beginning only at 2 would leave the odd branch unanchored.

Capstone portfolio: defend an obstruction

A counter starts at 0 and legal moves add 6 or subtract 10. Prepare a one-page proof portfolio with four parts. First prove that 7 is unreachable by a property preserved under every move. Second give an explicit route to 2 or explain why none exists. Third decide whether the same property alone settles reachability of 4, and support your answer. Fourth construct a bijection from positive integers to the positive multiples of 4 and prove both required properties. Include one false converse or overclaim from your own reasoning and correct it. The result should make clear where you use induction, an explicit witness, or a function argument.

Model portfolio

Obstruction. Every legal move changes the state by an even integer. The start 0 is even. If a state after m moves is even, then adding 6 or subtracting 10 leaves it even. Induction on m proves every reachable state is even. Since 7 is odd, it is unreachable. In fact both move sizes are multiples of 2, and this is the invariant we used.

Positive route. The state 2 is reachable: 0 → 6 → 12 → 2 uses +6, +6, −10. The invariant could only say that 2 was compatible with parity; this explicit sequence proves reachability. For 4, parity alone says it is not ruled out by this invariant. An explicit route is 0 → −10 → −4 → 2 → 8 → 14 → 4, using −10, then four +6 moves, then −10. Thus 4 is reachable. A claim that every even state is reachable would need its own general proof; these two routes do not prove it.

Bijection. Let M = {4, 8, 12, …} and define f(n) = 4n for each positive integer n. If f(a) = f(b), then 4a = 4b and a = b, so the map is injective. Given any m in M, the definition of M supplies a positive integer k with m = 4k; then f(k) = m, so the map is surjective. It is bijective.

Corrected overclaim. “A state is even, therefore reachable” reverses the proved implication reachable ⇒ even. For this particular move set some even states may be reachable, but parity by itself never constructs the route. The same caution applies to a map: distinct outputs alone prove injectivity, not that every target is hit.

Rubric. Award two marks for the invariant proof with start, both move types, and induction over route length; two for a legal route to 2 and a justified decision about 4; two for a map whose domain and codomain are exact with separate injectivity and surjectivity proofs; and two for naming a false converse or scope overreach and repairing it. An eight-mark solution communicates both positive and negative claims without switching directions. Alternative legal routes receive full credit. The portfolio does not require proving the full reachable set.

Keep the proof boundaries visible

A useful stopping point is a portfolio you can defend aloud: every induction hypothesis is earlier than its target, every invariant is checked against every legal move, and both halves of a bijection are established for the stated sets. These are reusable proof habits. The prime-factorization lesson proved existence but not uniqueness; the quotient–remainder lesson proved uniqueness generally but only illustrated existence; the infinite-pigeonholes lesson proved one particular infinite pairing. Each boundary is part of the result, not an apology for it.
