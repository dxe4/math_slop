Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Start with a counted experiment

This sampler takes four lessons and one original two-dice design. Its bounded goal is to count a finite event, handle an overlapping condition, update a chance after a clue, and calculate the average score of a small game. Allow about four hours: roughly 80 minutes for reading, 70 for reworking the lesson examples, 40 for the mixed practice below, and 50 for the capstone and self-review. The optional small sittings below offer an attempt, a worked check and a question for next time; thinking time can make any one of them a whole evening.

Entry recap. A fraction a/b is a selected count divided by a total count. When every elementary outcome has the same chance, count favorable outcomes and divide by all outcomes. Ordered pairs matter for two dice: (2,5) and (5,2) are different paths. “Or” includes cases meeting both conditions. A conditional chance uses only cases compatible with the clue as its denominator. These points are refreshed in the linked lessons, so fractions and simple algebra suffice to begin.

The route does not attempt Bayes odds, variance, or normal approximation. Those require more modelling decisions and appear in later routes. Your stopping point here is a transparent finite game with a defensible chance and expected score, not a claim that one observed play must match its mean.

One small sitting at a time

Pick one idea and one attempt for tonight. Split a lesson whenever you want; thinking time can make a single question a whole sitting. When you return, try your recall question before checking the explanation. These are optional stopping points within the full four-hour route, including practice and a capstone.

1. Count outcomes with names

Begin the sample-space lesson. Write the ordered blue-and-yellow pairs that total eight, then check them in its grid. Stop when you can say why swapping two different faces gives a different pair.

Check this sitting

The pairs are (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2): five of 36 equally likely outcomes. The chance is 5/36 under the fair, independent model. Blue two and yellow six differs from blue six and yellow two; (4,4) remains the same pair when swapped. Leave yourself the recall question “Why is the denominator 36 rather than the number of possible totals?”

2. Keep an overlap once

Recall where the 36 ordered outcomes came from. Then begin the overlap lesson. On one fair die, list the faces that are even or at least five. Mark any shared face before counting.

Check this sitting

Six choices for each of two distinguishable dice give 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely pairs. For the single-die question, even gives {2,4,6} and at least five gives {5,6}. The shared six belongs in the union once, so the winning set is {2,4,5,6}: chance 4/6 = 2/3. Leave the question “Does ‘or’ allow both conditions?”

3. Change the denominator after a clue

Recall that “or” here includes both. Read the clue lesson through its two-dice example. Before opening the answer, find the chance of a total of seven given that at least one die is six. Use the row and column containing six.

Check this sitting

There are 6 + 6 − 1 = 11 surviving pairs: (6,6) belongs to both the row and column, so count it once. Only (1,6) and (6,1) total seven. The conditional chance is 2/11. The denominator counts pairs compatible with the clue. Before returning, ask “Why did the denominator change from 36?”

4. Give the outcomes a score

Recall what the eleven surviving pairs represent. Begin the expected-score lesson, following its six-face average and weighted table. Then return to two fair, independent dice with no clue: award two points for a total of seven and zero otherwise. Find the mean score.

Check this sitting

Six of 36 pairs total seven and 30 do not. The mean is 2 × (6/36) + 0 × (30/36) = 1/3 point. A single play scores zero or two, never 1/3. The two score labels cannot be averaged equally because they have different chances. Leave the question “What is the difference between an outcome, a score and its mean?”

5. Make the counts your own

Try the mixed practice on paper before opening its answer. Save the capstone for another sitting if you prefer. In your own solution, label the original outcome count, the survivors after the clue, and the score assigned to each result.

Stop after one calculation you can explain in words. On a later visit, reconstruct it without the model. A saved or explored mark records your choice; it does not assess your understanding.

Read in two short modules

A short starting pair

  1. A deck, a die, and the sample space. Before reading its answer, list all ordered outcomes of two distinguishable dice. Bridge to the next lesson by changing the target from one event to a choice of two win conditions.
  2. Why two chances can overlap. Identify the intersection before adding event counts. On one die, “even or at least five” includes six only once.

The first lesson justifies division by the size of a uniform sample space. The second partitions a union into disjoint pieces. Keep both justifications in view: a correct numerator built from the wrong denominator still gives a wrong probability.

One useful connection

  1. The clue changes the denominator. Treat a clue as restricting the original table of outcomes. Write the survivors down rather than trying to remember a formula alone.
  2. Fair games and expected score. Attach a numerical reward to each surviving or unscreened outcome, then weight it by its probability. A score of zero still belongs in the distribution.

Conditioning and expectation answer different questions. Conditioning changes which outcomes are under consideration; expectation averages numerical scores under a specified distribution. If a clue is given before a reward prediction, first update the distribution, then average scores with the updated weights.

Mixed practice with a complete check

A blue and a yellow fair die are rolled independently. A ticket wins if the blue result is six or the total is seven. Find the win probability. If the yellow result is known to be two, find the conditional win probability. A win earns three points; a loss costs one point. Find the expected score before the clue.

Hint

Count blue-six pairs, total-seven pairs, and their shared pair. After the clue, only six pairs remain. Use a positive and a negative score in the weighted average.

Worked mixed-practice answer

Six pairs have blue six. Six pairs sum to seven. Their shared pair is (6,1), so the union has 6+6−1=11 pairs out of 36. The win chance is 11/36. With yellow two, the compatible pairs are (1,2) through (6,2); only (5,2) sums to seven and (6,2) has blue six. Thus the conditional win chance is 2/6=1/3. Before the clue, the expected score is 3(11/36)+(−1)(25/36)=33/36−25/36=2/9 point. This fractional mean is not an available single-play reward.

If the clue were “yellow is seven,” its chance under this die model would be zero. The conditional probability would be undefined, not a secret seventh die face. That boundary is part of using the model honestly.

Capstone: design a two-dice card game

Make a card that says: “Roll distinguishable fair, independent dice. Win when the sum is eight or the blue die shows one. After seeing that yellow shows six, decide whether to keep playing. A win pays four points and a loss costs two.” Calculate the original win chance, the chance after the clue, and expected score before and after the clue. State one assumption about the dice and one reason that expected score is not a guaranteed payoff.

Model solution

The sum-eight pairs are (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2): five. Blue one supplies six pairs. Their intersection is empty because blue one would require yellow seven to sum to eight. Thus 11 of 36 pairs win, so P(win)=11/36. Given yellow six, the six possible blue results remain. Both (2,6), by its sum, and (1,6), by blue one, win. The conditional win chance is 2/6=1/3.

Before the clue, E[score]=4(11/36)−2(25/36)=44/36−50/36=−1/6 point. After yellow six, E[score|clue]=4(2/6)−2(4/6)=8/6−8/6=0 points. The rule assumes each die is fair and the dice do not influence one another, making ordered pairs uniform. One play can score only four or minus two, not either mean. A choice to keep playing may depend on preferences beyond expected points.
