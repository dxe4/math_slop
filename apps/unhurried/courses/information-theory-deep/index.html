A proof route through finite information

This route studies discrete sources with explicitly specified probabilities. Its central claims are the entropy chain rule, the nonnegativity of KL divergence, the binary prefix-code lower bound, and Huffman's expected-length optimality in its finite setting. It ends by checking what changes when probabilities are estimated from a tiny sample. Nine articles supply worked introductions; the work here asks you to reproduce arguments, compare edge cases, and solve a connected capstone. Allow about 630 minutes: approximately 233 minutes of article reading, 220 minutes of proof reconstruction and numerical checks, and 177 minutes for the capstone and review. The result is sustained practice with named finite results, not a credential or a treatment of continuous entropy and channel coding theorems.

Entry recap. A distribution is a nonnegative list summing to one. E[f(X)]=Σp(x)f(x). Base-two logarithms satisfy log₂(ab)=log₂a+log₂b and convert ratios to bits. A conditional probability divides a positive joint row by its marginal; no such division is defined for a zero-probability row. We take 0 log₂0=0 in entropy sums by continuity. The articles repair notation locally, so you can begin here with probability, weighted averages and logarithms, then pause for the proof tasks after each module.

Read, derive, and stress-test each module

Foundations

  1. How surprising is a symbol? Derive additivity from the product rule for independent observations. Write a counterexample in which the second symbol repeats the first; the two marginal surprises then overstate the pair surprise.
  2. Average surprise in a mystery bag Differentiate the binary entropy function on (0,1) to prove its one-bit maximum at 1/2, and check endpoints by continuity. Keep the zero-probability convention separate from the surprise of an impossible observation.
  3. Two clues can repeat information Rebuild the chain rule from p(x,y)=p(x)p(y|x), summing only over positive cells. State what happens to a row with p(x)=0 rather than inventing a conditional distribution for it.

Foundation checkpoint. For the table p(0,0)=p(1,1)=3/8 and p(0,1)=p(1,0)=1/8, each marginal is fair. Thus H(X)=1 bit, H(Y|X)=h₂(1/4), and H(X,Y)=1+h₂(1/4). Prove that last equality by expanding each positive joint log into a marginal log plus a conditional log. A numerical match is useful but does not replace the algebra.

Mechanisms and examples

  1. How much does one clue reveal? Use the chain rule in both orders to show symmetry. Then view I(X;Y) as KL divergence between the joint law and product marginals; mark nonnegativity as depending on Gibbs inequality until you prove that inequality in the later module.
  2. Make a code no symbol can confuse Derive Kraft necessity by counting occupied extensions at a common depth. Test a valid tree and a nonrealizable proposed length multiset. Distinguish a particular list of bit strings from a feasible list of lengths.
  3. Why common symbols get short codes Recreate the sibling exchange and contraction argument for positive weights. State that the target is expected length among finite binary prefix codes. The construction can tie; different trees can share the same optimum.

Coding checkpoint. Let K=Σ2^−ℓ(x) for a finite prefix code. Kraft gives K≤1. Put q(x)=2^−ℓ(x)/K, a probability distribution across the coded symbols. Direct expansion gives D(P||Q)=L−H(P)+log₂K. Since KL is nonnegative and log₂K≤0, rearrange to L−H(P)=D(P||Q)−log₂K≥0. This proof assumes positive P weights for the displayed finite ratios; zero-weight symbols may be kept with the standard zero-term convention if all needed codewords exist. It establishes the entropy lower bound for prefix codes without claiming every code attains it.

Limits and deeper questions

  1. A message through a fickle switch Calculate the fair-input joint table and distinguish mismatch chance ε from I(X;Y)=1−h₂(ε). At ε=1, the output is always inverted but still fully informative after reversal.
  2. The cost of a wrong probability model Prove Gibbs inequality for finite distributions using ln u≤u−1. Handle Q(x)=0 on positive P support separately as an infinite case. Use a two-symbol example to show asymmetry rather than merely asserting it.
  3. The tiny sample looks too predictable Enumerate all two-draw fair-bit samples. Then explain the general downward bias of plug-in entropy as an expectation statement from concavity, not a guarantee about each observed sample. The finite enumeration is a proof for one case; the general claim uses a separate theorem.

Proof checkpoint. Show where the hypotheses differ across these claims: a channel table needs an input law to yield information; KL needs support handling; plug-in bias needs a sampling law. A proof of an identity under stipulated probabilities does not validate estimated probabilities from data. This is the point at which the course connects model mathematics with its empirical boundary.

Mixed proof practice

First, assume X and Y are independent fair bits. Their joint law is uniform on four pairs, so H(X,Y)=2 and I(X;Y)=0. If Y=X instead, the marginals are unchanged but the joint law has only 00 and 11 with probability 1/2 each: H(X,Y)=1 and I(X;Y)=1. This pair of tables refutes any calculation of mutual information from marginals alone. Second, consider proposed binary lengths 1,2,2,2. Their Kraft sum is 5/4, so no prefix-free tree has those exact lengths. Rearranging source weights cannot repair an impossible length set. Third, consider P=(1/2,1/2), Q=(1,0). D(P||Q)=∞ because P can produce the second symbol while Q forbids it. This is a support failure, not a numerical rounding issue.

Try to write each result without checking the preceding paragraph, then state which theorem or arithmetic step licenses it. The practice is complete when your argument identifies the distribution, the log base or units, and the boundary case. A correct final number with no law attached can hide a wrong denominator or an unjustified independence assumption.

Capstone: audit a source, switch, code, and estimate

Use source S on A,B,C,D with probabilities (3/8,1/4,1/4,1/8). Use a separate input bit X with P(X=0)=2/3 through a binary symmetric switch with ε=1/3 and output Y. Complete five tasks. (1) Derive the entropy chain rule for the X,Y joint law, showing the positive-cell factorization. (2) Build a Huffman code for S, compute H(S), its weighted length and Kraft sum, and derive the general prefix lower bound using K and Q as in the coding checkpoint. (3) Compute I(X;Y). (4) For P=(2/3,1/3) and Q=(1/2,1/2), calculate both directional KL divergences. (5) If a fair bit source is sampled three times, enumerate estimates by the count of ones and find the expected plug-in bias. Keep the source, channel, divergence pair and sample experiment distinct; each has its own probability law.

Complete model solution

The channel joint cells are p(0,0)=4/9, p(0,1)=2/9, p(1,0)=1/9 and p(1,1)=2/9. For any positive cell, p(x,y)=p(x)p(y|x), so −log₂p(x,y)=−log₂p(x)−log₂p(y|x). Weighting by p(x,y) and summing gives H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y|X). Here H(X)=h₂(1/3)≈0.918 bit, and each conditional output row has probabilities (2/3,1/3), giving H(Y|X)=h₂(1/3). Thus H(X,Y)=2h₂(1/3)≈1.837 bits. The output margins are P(Y=0)=5/9 and P(Y=1)=4/9. Given Y=0, P(X=0|Y=0)=4/5; given Y=1, P(X=0|Y=1)=1/2. Therefore H(X|Y)=(5/9)h₂(1/5)+(4/9)·1≈0.846 bit, and I(X;Y)=h₂(1/3)−H(X|Y)≈0.073 bit.

For S, join D (1/8) with one of the 1/4 symbols, say C, making 3/8. Join that node with B (1/4), making 5/8, and join with A (3/8). One resulting code is A→0, B→10, C→110, D→111. It has Kraft sum 1/2+1/4+1/8+1/8=1 and weighted length (3/8)1+(1/4)2+(1/4)3+(1/8)3=2 bits. Entropy is (3/8)log₂(8/3)+(1/4)2+(1/4)2+(1/8)3≈1.906 bits. For any finite prefix tree with positive P weights, set K=Σ2^−ℓ≤1 and q(x)=2^−ℓ(x)/K. Then D(P||Q)=Σp log₂(p/q)=L−H(P)+log₂K. Gibbs inequality gives D≥0; since log₂K≤0, L−H(P)=D−log₂K≥0. Here the code's 2-bit mean exceeds entropy by about 0.094 bit, as expected for these weights.

For the divergence pair, D(P||Q)=(2/3)log₂(4/3)+(1/3)log₂(2/3)≈0.082 bit. Reversing gives D(Q||P)=½log₂(3/4)+½log₂(3/2)=½log₂(9/8)≈0.085 bit, showing asymmetry. Both are finite because both laws assign positive mass to both symbols. For three independent fair-bit draws, the count of ones k=0,1,2,3 has probabilities 1/8,3/8,3/8,1/8. The plug-in entropy is zero at k=0 or 3 and h₂(1/3)≈0.918 bit at k=1 or 2. Thus E[Ĥ]=(3/4)h₂(1/3)≈0.689 bit against true H=1 bit, for bias about −0.311 bit. This enumeration proves the bias for this sample size, while general bias needs the concavity argument.

Self-review rubric. Award one check each for a normalized joint table, a chain-rule derivation that handles only positive cells, correct code-tree merges and leaf depths, a justified Kraft and KL lower-bound argument, directional divergence with named averaging laws, and a sample enumeration with bias in the correct direction. A complete response has all six checks and states that the switch and source are separate models. If one proof step depends on a theorem you have not reconstructed, label it rather than presenting the numerical example as proof. You may stop when you can transfer these arguments to a new finite table and tree; further study could address block coding and estimation methods under new hypotheses.
