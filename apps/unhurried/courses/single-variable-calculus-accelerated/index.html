Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Calculus reconnection for rusty graduates

This route is for a reader who has previously differentiated and integrated but wants reliable transfer between tools. The goal is to rebuild three habits: check hypotheses before applying a theorem, carry units and endpoints through a calculation, and attach an error or limit argument to an approximation. The linked lessons are concise refreshers; this wrapper supplies a diagnostic, mixed tasks, and a capstone. Plan about seven hours including article exercises and the final comparison. It is a selected refresher, not a replacement for a full calculus sequence.

Diagnostic. Without notes, compute d/dx of (1+x²)³, evaluate ∫₀¹2x dx, and decide whether ∫₁∞1/x dx is finite. Answers are 6x(1+x²)², 1, and no: its cutoff value is ln R→∞. If the first fails, spend extra time on derivative tools; if the second fails, revisit the accumulation bridge; if the third fails, make the finite cutoff explicit in the approximation module.

Three exact modules

Derivative tools

Use A slope at an instant as a five-minute definition check, then work through The chain rule as two magnifying lenses. The first ensures that a derivative remains a limit of nonzero secants; the second turns that limit into a product of local conversions. Write down the inner function, its current output, and the outer derivative evaluated at that output. The remainder proof is worth studying because it still works when an inner increment equals zero for some h. Do not divide by that increment without checking it.

Integral tools

Read Why accumulation undoes differentiation and The average speed you must have reached. These two existence results have different shapes: the fundamental theorem identifies a derivative at each continuity point of an accumulated input, while the mean value theorem guarantees at least one interior derivative equal to an interval average. The shared habit is to name the interval and hypotheses. After reading, construct an example where a corner invalidates the mean value theorem but an accumulated continuous input still differentiates cleanly.

Approximation

Read Substitution is a change of ruler, An infinite journey with finite area, and A polynomial approximation with an error receipt. These lessons make different limits visible: transformed endpoints, an endpoint moving to infinity, and a polynomial residual. For each calculation, write what is being held fixed and what tends to a limit. Keep a one-page error ledger: algebraic substitution is exact; improper integration requires a cutoff limit; Taylor approximation has a bounded residual.

Transfer checks with solutions

1. Differentiate (2t+1)⁴ at t=1. Solution: the outer derivative is 4u³ at u=3, and the inner derivative is 2. The result is 4·27·2=216. The outer derivative is not evaluated at t=1.

2. Let A(x)=∫₀ˣcos(t²)dt. Find A′(x), even though no elementary antiderivative is needed. Solution: cos(t²) is continuous, so A′(x)=cos(x²). This is not 2x cos(x²); the upper endpoint is x, not x².

3. Evaluate ∫₀¹2t et²dt. Solution: u=t² and du=2t dt. Endpoints stay 0 and 1, so the value is ∫₀¹eudu=e−1.

4. Evaluate the area under x−3 from 1 to infinity. Solution: the finite value through R is [−1/(2x²)]₁ᴿ=(1−R−2)/2; the limit is 1/2.

5. For f(x)=x² on [−1,1], locate the mean-value point. Solution: the endpoint heights agree, so the average slope is zero. f′(x)=2x, giving c=0.

6. Bound the error in sin x≈x for |x|≤0.1 using a second-derivative bound M=1. Solution: the first Taylor polynomial is x and |R₁|≤|x|²/2≤0.005. A sharper bound is possible, but this one is certified.

7. If g(t)=1−t, what is ∫₀¹g′(t)dt? Solution: −1, matching g(1)−g(0)=0−1. A reversed coordinate direction changes sign.

8. Does a small residual observed near the expansion centre certify accuracy at a far-away point? Solution: no. The derivative bound and interval length in the remainder estimate must cover that point; a small local residual at one x gives no global promise.

Capstone: one integral, two estimates

Task. Estimate I=∫₀1/2 et²dt in two ways. First give lower and upper bounds from 1≤et²≤e1/4. Second use the Taylor polynomial 1+t²+t⁴/2 and an explicit error bound. State which estimate is tighter, with an error budget that makes clear what was proved.

Model solution. The coarse monotone bounds give 1/2≤I≤e1/4/2≈0.6421. For 0≤u≤1/4, Taylor's theorem for eu through degree two gives eu=1+u+u²/2+R₂(u), where 0≤R₂(u)≤e1/4u³/6. Substitute u=t². Integrating the polynomial on [0,1/2] gives J=1/2+(1/2)³/3+(1/2)⁵/10=1/2+1/24+1/320=523/960≈0.544792. The integrated remainder is at most e1/4/6·∫₀1/2t⁶dt=e1/4/(6·7·128)=e1/4/5376≈0.000239. Thus J≤I≤J+0.000239, a much tighter certified interval than the coarse [0.5,0.6421]. The method combines a Taylor receipt with an ordinary finite integral; it does not rely on a numerical calculator's unexplained output.

Rubric, 12 points. Give two for the coarse bounds, two for a correct Taylor polynomial, two for the sign and bound of the remainder, two for integrating the polynomial, two for integrating the error envelope, and two for comparing interval widths and stating the conclusion. A numerical estimate without a proved envelope earns at most six. The stopping point is a reproducible bound with each source of error named.

What has been reconnected

Derivative fluency is checked by tasks 1, 2, and 5. Integral accounting is checked by tasks 3, 4, and 7. Error control is checked by tasks 6, 8, and the capstone. If those arguments are comfortable, continue to proof-based analysis or a broader calculus text for techniques and applications not covered here.
