Prove a local promise, then test its edge

This is a selected proof route through local existence and uniqueness, scalar and planar stability, a fold, and one discrete map. It assumes comfort with derivatives, definite integrals, uniform convergence, completeness of real numbers and 2×2 eigenvalues. Allow about twelve hours: roughly three for six linked articles, two for the connective proof below, five for twelve proof and modeling tasks, and two for the mixed task and capstone with revisions. The aim is to understand a finite set of named results and counterexamples. It is not a credential in global dynamical systems, partial differential equations or chaos theory.

Entry recap. A continuous function on a compact rectangle is bounded. The mean value theorem turns a derivative bound |fy|≤L into a Lipschitz estimate in y. The supremum norm of a continuous function u on an interval is max|u(t)|. Uniform convergence means this norm of the difference tends to zero; a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. These facts power the local theorem. If any one is unfamiliar, prove it or refresh it before treating the contraction argument as established.

Contraction-method lesson: an actual local theorem

Here is the promised connective proof. Let a,b>0, and let f(t,y) be continuous on the closed rectangle |t−t0|≤a, |y−y0|≤b, with |f|≤M there. Suppose |f(t,u)−f(t,v)|≤L|u−v| for all states u,v in the rectangle at the same time t. Choose h>0 with h≤a, hM≤b and hL<1. Let X be the continuous functions on I=[t0−h,t0+h] satisfying |u(t)−y0|≤b everywhere, equipped with sup distance. Define the Picard operator

(Φu)(t)=y0+∫t0tf(s,u(s))ds

The integral is well defined, and |(Φu)(t)−y0|≤hM≤b, so Φ maps X into X. For u,v∈X, the Lipschitz assumption gives |(Φu)(t)−(Φv)(t)|≤hL‖u−v‖∞, hence ‖Φu−Φv‖∞≤q‖u−v‖∞ with q=hL<1. X is complete: a sup-norm Cauchy sequence converges uniformly to a continuous limit, and the closed bound |u−y0|≤b survives the limit.

To see why a contraction has a unique fixed point, start with any u0∈X and iterate un+1=Φun. Consecutive distances are at most qnd(u1,u0); summing the geometric tail makes (un) Cauchy. Completeness supplies a limit u, and the Lipschitz bound makes Φ continuous, so Φu=u. If v were another fixed point, d(u,v)=d(Φu,Φv)≤q d(u,v), forcing d(u,v)=0. Finally the fundamental theorem of calculus differentiates the fixed-point integral, giving u′=f(t,u) and u(t0)=y0. Conversely any differentiable solution staying in this rectangle satisfies the integral equation and so coincides with u on I. This proves local existence and uniqueness under the stated hypotheses. It says nothing about how long the solution remains in the rectangle afterward.

The delayed-square-root example violates the Lipschitz hypothesis at zero; its explicit solutions establish actual nonuniqueness. A failed theorem hypothesis alone would not. For f(t,y)=y², the hypotheses hold in every bounded rectangle, yet the solution from y(0)=1 blows up at t=1, showing why the conclusion is local.

Worked mixed proof: repair a tempting shortcut

A student says, “x′=−x³ has f′(0)=0, so the linearized equation x′=0 proves all nearby states stay put.” Identify the logical error. Then prove stability and attraction directly, and compare with x′=+x³.

Hint

Differentiate x−2 on nonzero solutions. For stability, produce an explicit δ for each ε.

Worked proof

The linearization has a zero eigenvalue and discards the leading nonzero cubic term; it proves no such claim. For x′=−x³, nonzero initial b gives x(t)=b/√(1+2b²t), while b=0 gives the equilibrium. Hence |x(t)|≤|b| for t≥0 and |x(t)|→0. Given ε>0, δ=ε proves stability, and the limit proves attraction. For x′=+x³, x(t)=b/√(1−2b²t) until its denominator vanishes. Given any δ, choose 0<b<min(δ,ε); this solution reaches ε at positive time before blow-up. So zero is unstable. Both derivatives at zero equal zero. The exact nonlinear terms, not the shared linearization, decide the outcomes.

Capstone: replace a nonunique rate carefully

Start with y′=2√|y|, y(0)=0. Construct two distinct solutions and verify each at its join. Then consider the regularized rule y′=g(y), g(y)=2(√(1+y²)−1), with the same initial value. Check a Lipschitz constant on ℝ, apply the local theorem proved above, and identify the local solution. Finally say whether that theorem by itself guarantees existence for all t and explain why the original failure of Lipschitz was not alone a proof of nonuniqueness.

Model solution

The constant y=0 solves the original equation. Another solution is y=0 for t≤1 and y=(t−1)² for t≥1. On either open piece the equation holds. At t=1 the left derivative is zero and the right difference quotient is h²/h→0, so the joined function is differentiable with y′(1)=0=2√|y(1)|. The two solutions differ at t=2.

For the corrected rule, g′(y)=2y/√(1+y²), so |g′(y)|≤2 for all real y. By the mean value theorem, |g(u)−g(v)|≤2|u−v|. Also g is continuous, so any finite rectangle around (0,0) satisfies the local theorem with L=2 and a sufficiently short h. Since g(0)=0, the constant function y=0 solves the initial-value problem. Local uniqueness makes it the only solution on the theorem's interval. The local theorem alone does not grant a global time interval; one would need an extension argument. In this particular model, the explicit constant zero function is already defined for all t, and repeated local uniqueness keeps any competing solution equal to it wherever both are defined. The original f(y)=2√|y| has an unbounded difference quotient near zero, so the sufficient theorem cannot be used there. The explicit delayed curve, not the failed condition, proves nonuniqueness.

Proof rubric. A complete submission states its interval and hypotheses; verifies a piecewise derivative at the join; gives an actual derivative bound for g; explains completeness and the contraction inequality rather than merely naming a theorem; supplies an ε–δ argument for stability tasks; and labels any computer trajectory as an observation. If one item fails, repair that argument before claiming the route's outcome. A satisfying stopping point is the ability to reproduce the local theorem and capstone with no appeal to an unseen graph. Further study can pursue continuation theorems, nonlinear planar systems and precise chaos definitions.
