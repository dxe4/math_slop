Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

A four-lesson sampler of changing chance

This route asks two compact questions: where can a token land after a few random steps, and what can a small state table say about tomorrow? It then switches from steps to arrivals, where the random quantity is a count during an interval. Allow roughly four hours for reading, pauses, exercises and the capstone. This sampler covers four selected articles; it does not promise queue theory or a general theory of stochastic processes.

Entry recap. A probability between zero and one measures a chance under a stated rule. Independent coin tosses let us multiply their chances along one path. Conditional probability changes the reference group: P(next Bright | current Calm) refers only to occasions currently Calm. A distribution is a list of chances totaling one. In the arrival lesson, λt is a rate multiplied by a duration and is therefore a unitless expected count. Each article refreshes its own notation, so this recap is enough to enter even if you have not followed another route.

At a satisfying stopping point, you should be able to enumerate a finite path, explain why a chosen state retains the relevant history, advance a three-state distribution by one step, and compute a single Poisson count with units. Keep your own written solutions to the checks below; the reading-complete control records progress, not mastery.

Module 1: A short starting pair

  1. A random walk on a tiny map. Draw all two-step paths from zero and distinguish the four paths from the three possible endpoints. Record why LR and RL both end at zero.
  2. Memory lives in the state. For each proposed state, compare two histories ending at the same visible label. If their next-step chances differ, add the missing phase or memory to the state.

The bridge between these lessons is the meaning of “current state.” The random walk's position is sufficient for its next position under the independent-step rule: from any position the next move has the same specified coin chance, regardless of the route there. Its entire path matters for a question such as “Have we ever touched zero?”, so that question may require an extra hit flag. State design depends on the prediction or event you intend to study.

Module 2: One useful connection

  1. A playlist that chooses its next mood. Treat each row as a conditional distribution. Before multiplying, inspect that all entries are nonnegative and every row totals one.
  2. Random arrivals at an imaginary museum. This is a different process: continuous time and counts in intervals. Convert the rate and duration to matching units before using λt.

The matrix lesson packages many one-step conditional chances. The arrival lesson packages count probabilities over a time window. Both begin with assumptions that make a calculation possible; neither is justified merely by the word “random.” The playlist requires a sufficient finite state and stable transition rule. The Poisson model requires constant rate and independent increments. Ask which assumption would be hardest to defend in a real playlist or museum.

Worked mixed practice

A two-state playlist has rows C→(3/4,1/4) and B→(1/2,1/2). Suppose its initial distribution is (1/2,1/2). Separately, a model bell rings at rate two per hour according to a Poisson process. Find the chance of C after one playlist step and the chance of exactly one bell in half an hour. State why the two calculations use different formulas.

Work it through

For Calm, split by the current state: (1/2)(3/4)+(1/2)(1/2)=3/8+1/4=5/8. The Bright chance is 3/8, completing the row. For bells, half an hour is 1/2 hour, so λt=(2/hour)(1/2 hour)=1. The Poisson count formula gives e⁻¹1¹/1!=e⁻¹≈0.3679. The playlist formula sums mutually exclusive routes to a next state; the bell formula counts all arrival patterns that produce one event in a duration under a constant-rate Poisson model. These events belong to separate stipulated processes, so there is no reason to identify 5/8 with e⁻¹.

As an explanation check, imagine the playlist actually follows a hidden three-day cycle Calm, Calm, Bright. A single row for visible Calm cannot predict the next visible label from both phases. The Markov article shows how to repair the state. This check matters before trusting a transition matrix.

Capstone: a playlist beside an arrival counter

Analyze a three-state playlist with moods C, B and T. Use these rows: C→(1/2,1/4,1/4), B→(0,3/4,1/4), T→(1/4,1/4,1/2). Begin certainly in B and calculate the distribution after one and two chimes. Explain why the current mood is sufficient under your stated rule. At a separate toy museum, arrivals follow a Poisson process at three per hour; calculate the chance of zero arrivals in 30 minutes. Identify one plausible feature of an actual museum that would break the count model.

Model solution

Every playlist row is nonnegative and sums to one. From B the first distribution is (0,3/4,1/4). One more multiplication gives Calm (3/4)(0)+(1/4)(1/4)=1/16; Bright (3/4)(3/4)+(1/4)(1/4)=10/16=5/8; Tense (3/4)(1/4)+(1/4)(1/2)=5/16. The entries total one. The state is sufficient because the stipulated next-step choice uses only the current mood and a fresh independent random draw. If the rule secretly depends on the previous song, this justification fails. For the museum, λt=(3/hour)(1/2 hour)=3/2, so P(N=0)=e^(−3/2)≈0.2231. Group arrivals in a tour bus would undermine independent individual increments and the simple single-arrival assumption.

Self-review rubric. A complete capstone has three normalized transition rows, both correct distribution vectors, a reason that the selected state captures the one-step rule, a unit-consistent λt=3/2, the exact zero-count expression, and a concrete model limitation. If the calculations are right but the assumption argument is absent, revisit the Markov and Poisson lessons before moving on. This route ends once those checks are defensible; hitting probabilities are an optional next question.
