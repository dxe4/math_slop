Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Audit a curve before you count its meetings

This selected core is for a reader comfortable with polynomial algebra, complex numbers, partial derivatives, basic proofs and equivalence classes. Allow about eight and a half hours including article exercises and the audit below. The route has two modules: local curve behavior and projective completion. Its outcome is the ability to identify a smooth or singular point, compute one projective point at infinity, and state the precise conditions under which Bézout counts intersections. It is a working review of these examples, not a complete curve theory.

Entry recap. A curve F(x,y)=0 is a set of solutions over a named field. At a zero p, the gradient ∇F(p) is the pair of partial derivatives. If nonzero, its perpendicular line is the linearized tangent. If zero, solve local branches rather than assigning a tangent from 0=0. In projective space [X:Y:Z] represents a nonzero triple up to nonzero scaling; the chart Z=1 contains ordinary affine coordinates and Z=0 is its boundary. A homogeneous polynomial has terms of equal total degree.

Module 1: local curves

  1. Draw the solutions of a polynomial. Use exact evaluation and field labels as the baseline. The same formula can behave differently over ℝ and ℂ.
  2. Tangents from derivatives of equations. Check F(p)=0 before computing the gradient. Work the circle example and derive the line normal to ∇F(p) at a smooth point.
  3. When a curve has no single tangent. Compare y²=x³ with y²=x²(x+1) near the origin. Both have vanishing gradient, but their local branch slopes distinguish cusp and node.

The bridge through this module is a narrowing of the question. A zero-locus test says whether p belongs. The gradient asks for its first-order behavior. When that test degenerates, lowest-degree terms and explicit branch equations can still answer a local question. Do not equate zero gradient of any chosen polynomial with a visibly singular set: (y−x²)² defines the same smooth parabola as y−x² while its gradient vanishes along it.

Module check. For F=y²−x²−x³ at (0,0), verify both partials vanish. The equation gives y=±x√(1+x) for x near zero, so the branch slopes tend to ±1. Calling the point merely “gradient zero” would miss its two crossing directions. This calculation prepares you to distinguish local multiplicity from a count of distinct plotted points.

Module 2: projective completion

  1. Where parallel lines meet. Homogenize affine line equations and solve at Z=0. Check that [1:2:0] and [2:4:0] are one point because scaling leaves the class unchanged.
  2. Why intersection counting needs hypotheses. Work the line–conic tangency where a single point contributes multiplicity two. State the general degree-product theorem but keep its proof boundary visible.

The bridge is that affine counting can omit a projective point, while counting distinct points can omit multiplicity. Over ℝ it can also omit nonreal complex points. The projective article repairs the first issue for lines; the Bézout article displays all three in a theorem whose field, components and multiplicities are named. Our line–conic substitution is a complete calculation in a special case. It does not prove Bézout for arbitrary curves.

Worked mixed audit

Consider the conic C: X²+Y²−Z²=0 and line L: Y−Z=0 over ℂ. In Z=1, substitution gives x²+1−1=x²=0, so the only affine intersection is (0,1). At Z=0, the line gives Y=0; the conic then gives X=0, the excluded zero triple. Thus the only projective point is [0:1:1]. Restricting C to L gives a square, so its line–conic multiplicity is two. Bézout predicts 1·2=2, consistent with the calculation.

Now inspect C at p=(0,1) using the affine equation F=x²+y²−1. Its gradient is (0,2), so the tangent is y=1, precisely L. This links local calculus, tangency and projective counting. The point is smooth on C despite the intersection being nontransverse. “Singular curve point” and “tangent meeting of two smooth curves” are different claims.

Capstone: correct an incomplete intersection claim

A colleague writes: “The real affine line y=2 does not meet x²+y²=1, so the degree-one line and degree-two conic contradict Bézout.” Audit the statement. Find the complex projective intersections, including a check at infinity. Give their multiplicities in this line–conic case and state the general theorem's hypotheses. Identify exactly which assumption the colleague left implicit.

Hint

Homogenize the line as Y−2Z=0. In Z=1, solve x²+3=0. At Z=0 check whether the homogeneous equations permit a nonzero triple.

Model solution

Over ℝ there is no affine solution because x²=−3. Over ℂ, x=±i√3, giving [i√3:2:1] and [−i√3:2:1]. They are distinct. At Z=0, the line forces Y=0, and the conic forces X²=0, so no nonzero triple remains: there is no point at infinity in this example. The restricted quadratic x²+3 has two distinct roots, each of multiplicity one. Their total is 2=1·2. Bézout concerns projective plane curves over an algebraically closed field with no common component, counted with intersection multiplicity. The colleague counted only real affine points; that is outside the theorem's setting. The theorem itself is not contradicted.

Rubric. A complete audit keeps real and complex coordinate sets separate, solves the homogeneous equations at Z=0, names both complex points, justifies their simple multiplicities by distinct quadratic roots, and lists algebraic closure, projectivity, no common component and multiplicity. Merely repeating “two intersections” without their coordinates or hypotheses is incomplete.

Stop with a reliable audit habit

After this route, you should be able to check a curve locally and challenge an unqualified intersection count. The course has not proved general Bézout or classified singularities. For algebra behind point sets, continue with coordinate rings and radicals.
