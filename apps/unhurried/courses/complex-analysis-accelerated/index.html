Reconnect calculations with their hypotheses

This route is for someone who has seen complex arithmetic, differentiation and integrals before and wants a compact, demanding refresher. It selects eight lessons and a calculation portfolio for roughly seven hours. The point is to recover a reliable method: establish the domain, find singularities, choose orientation, calculate, and test every discarded contour piece. It does not survey all complex function theory.

Entry diagnostic. Without reading ahead, compute (1+i)², the quotient [(z+h)²−z²]/h, and the residue of 1/(z²+1) at i. The answers are 2i, 2z+h, and 1/(2i). If the first two are difficult, spend more time on Module 1. If the residue answer is unfamiliar, keep the final module's local definition beside you. You should also be comfortable integrating a parametrized curve and using an improper integral; revisit those real-calculus ideas before the capstone if necessary.

Follow the exact catalog route

1. Holomorphic functions

Read Multiplication that turns the plane and A derivative must agree from every direction. Refresh the algebra, then prove a derivative by bounding the quotient's error for arbitrary h. The multiplier 2z is a local rotation and scale unless z=0. Record the difference between “has a derivative at this point” and “is holomorphic on an open region.” That distinction controls which contour results can be used later.

2. Contours

Read Two equations behind rigid behavior, Why the logarithm needs a cut, and A loop that counts turns. Use the Cauchy–Riemann equations to test candidate functions, but include the continuous-partials qualification before using the converse. Track a chosen Log domain on an actual loop. Then parametrize a circle and calculate ∮dz/z directly. The branch obstruction and nonzero winding are two views of the same loop around zero; neither licenses an integral through a pole on the path.

3. Residues and expansions

Read The boundary knows every interior value, A real integral solved by a complex detour, and A distant singularity limits a local series. Cauchy's formula is used under holomorphy near a closed disk. The residue example adds isolated poles and, for a real integral, a separate arc limit. The power-series lesson returns to local disks: a rational expression may be finite beyond the disk of one expansion. Treat all three as domain-sensitive calculations rather than interchangeable recipes.

Worked mixed practice: one rational function, three views

Let F(z)=1/(z²+4). Find its poles; compute ∮|z|=3F(z)dz counterclockwise; and give the Taylor series of F at zero with its radius. Before opening the solution, decide whether Cauchy's formula can be applied to F itself on the radius-three closed disk.

Hint

The poles are ±2i. Their residues have opposite signs. Expand using w=−z²/4.

Worked solution

The poles ±2i both lie inside |z|=3. The residue at 2i is 1/(4i), and at −2i it is −1/(4i). Their sum is zero, so the residue theorem gives a zero contour integral. Applying Cauchy's formula to F as a holomorphic numerator on this disk would be invalid: F has two interior poles. Around zero, F(z)=(1/4)/(1+z²/4)=(1/4)∑n≥0(−z²/4)n, valid for |z|<2. The exact radius is 2 because terms fail to tend to zero when |z|>2 and the poles lie at distance 2. The full contour integral can be zero despite the presence of poles: their signed contributions cancel.

Hypothesis audit: the tempting line “F is bounded on |z|=3, therefore holomorphic throughout |z|≤3” is false. Boundary values alone do not rule out interior poles. The residue theorem is appropriate precisely because it allows isolated interior poles and accounts for them.

Capstone: a justified real integral

Evaluate ∫−∞∞dx/(x²+9) using a real segment from −R to R and an upper semicircle. Give the enclosed pole and residue, state R's admissible range, prove an upper bound on the arc integral that tends to zero, and explain why the symmetric contour limit equals the ordinary improper integral. This is a method check as much as a numerical answer.

Model solution

Factor z²+9=(z−3i)(z+3i). For R>3 the upper contour encloses 3i but not −3i, and neither pole lies on the path. The residue at 3i is 1/(6i), so the positively oriented whole contour integral is 2πi/(6i)=π/3. On |z|=R, |z²+9|≥R²−9. The arc length is πR, hence the arc integral has modulus at most πR/(R²−9), which tends to zero. Also 0≤1/(x²+9)≤1/x² for |x|≥3, so both one-sided real tails converge. Thus the symmetric straight-segment limit equals their sum, the ordinary improper integral, and its value is π/3.
