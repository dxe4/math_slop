Count the surprising ways

Counting is easiest when the outcome is named before the arithmetic. In this route, a complete outcome might be a lunch, a committee, or a collision among categories. You need only integer arithmetic and the patience to list a small case. Set aside roughly seven hours including the lesson exercises, this page's mixed problem, and the event-planning capstone. The scope is four finite counting tools. You will not need probability, calculus, or a memorized bag of formulas. If a formula appears, recover it from the outcomes it counts.

Entry recap. A finite set has a count: {A,B,C} has three elements. A sequence such as AB remembers order; the group {A,B} does not. If two disjoint lists contain four and three outcomes, together they contain seven. If each of four first choices can be followed by exactly three second choices, there are twelve ordered pairs. These two operations—adding disjoint cases and multiplying successive choices—are the only arithmetic machinery needed here. Keep a one-line statement of “same outcome” beside each calculation.

Ordered modules and bridges

Module 1: Choices

  1. The multiplication principle — Count ordered selections from independent finite choice stages.
  2. Choosing without caring about order — Derive binomial coefficient from ordered selections.

The first lesson counts named stages, such as main then drink. The second asks when two ordered sequences name the same selected group. After both readings, make a two-column note: “chair then secretary” belongs under ordered choice, while “two volunteers” belongs under unordered selection. For four people, list all ordered pairs and circle the two orders that make each group. The division by 2 is justified because each distinct pair has exactly two orders.

Module 2: Guarantees

  1. Too many letters, too few mailboxes — Use pigeonhole principle and state the sharp threshold.
  2. A doorway into Pascal's triangle — Prove Pascal's identity by partitioning subsets.

The guarantee lesson changes the question from “how many outcomes?” to “what must happen in every allocation?” The Pascal lesson then returns to counting groups, splitting them by whether one fixed person belongs. Both rely on complete, disjoint cases. In the first, assume no collision and calculate box capacity; in the second, classify every group into yes or no. Do not treat a birthday example as a probability estimate. The statement about thirteen people is true even under an adversarial assignment of months.

Worked mixed practice

A community centre offers three workshop topics, each at two times, and has five volunteers. First count topic-time options: three topic choices followed by two time choices gives six. Then form a two-volunteer welcoming team: choose an unordered pair in C(5,2)=10 ways. If a registration includes both a topic-time choice and a team, there are 6×10=60 combinations because each of six choices can be paired with each of ten teams. If the volunteers instead take named host and greeter jobs, each team has two assignments, so the count is 6×5×4=120. Finally, if thirteen participants are assigned to twelve months for a birthday calendar, at least two share a month; that conclusion is independent of the 60 registration combinations. Explain each factor or capacity claim in a sentence before using the numbers.

Capstone

Plan a small event with four volunteers A, B, C, D and three activities. A two-person welcome team is selected, then one activity is chosen for the team to run. Count all plans. Count plans that include A on the team using a Pascal-style split. The venue has five numbered seats for the four volunteers; explain why this seating assignment is a different counting model and count it. Finally, imagine six attendee names placed into five check-in lanes. State one guaranteed fact, one fact that is not guaranteed, and a sharp five-attendee arrangement showing the threshold. Write a short note to an organizer explaining the assumptions behind each count.

Model solution

The team is an unordered pair from four volunteers, so C(4,2)=6. Each team may run any of three distinct activities, giving 6×3=18 plans. Of the teams, those containing A choose their other member among B,C,D in three ways; they yield 3×3=9 plans. The other C(3,2)=3 teams also yield nine, and 9+9=18 checks the first count. Assigning four named volunteers to four of five numbered seats is ordered: five choices for A's seat, then four for B, three for C, two for D, giving 5×4×3×2=120 seatings. It would be incorrect to divide by 4!, because exchanging volunteers between seats changes the assignment. With six attendees and five lanes, some lane contains at least two. We cannot guarantee that a particular lane contains two, or that any lane contains three. Five attendees can occupy one lane each, proving that six is the sharp threshold for a pair. A valid organizer note says teams are unordered, activities and seats are named, each person enters exactly one lane, and no distribution assumption was used.

Assessment rubric

Award two points for defining the team and finding six teams, two for the independent activity stage and 18 plans, two for the A/no-A partition with both counts, two for the seat assignment and its order explanation, and two for the lane guarantee plus sharp counterexample. A correct numeral without its outcome model earns at most half of that part. If your solution uses a different but equivalent count, check its bijection to these outcomes rather than matching the displayed algebra.

Outcomes and a stopping point

  • Apply product rule. Use the three-activities stage in the capstone and the topic-time calculation in mixed practice; name each complete ordered option.
  • Count selections. Use the four-volunteer welcome-team count and its include-A split in the capstone; justify why exchanging members changes nothing.
  • Find forced collisions. Use the six-attendee, five-lane conclusion and give the five-attendee arrangement that makes the threshold sharp.

You have a satisfying stopping point when you can look at a small problem and say whether it asks for stages, a group, a partition into cases, or a forced collision. For continued practice, change four volunteers to five and recompute by both a direct combination and an include-A split. The two results should agree because they count the same teams. Revisit any mismatch by listing a tiny case, not by guessing another formula.
