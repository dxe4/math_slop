Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Why one-variable calculus works: selected proofs

This route studies selected proof mechanisms behind a derivative, an integral, an existence theorem, an improper limit, and a Taylor approximation. It asks for algebraic inequalities, derivative and integral fluency, and comfort with “for every tolerance there exists a neighbourhood.” If those are rusty, use the primer first. Plan about twelve hours: roughly three for article reading, six for the proof tasks and revisions, and three for the capstone dossier. This is sustained work on named results, not a credential or a treatment of measure theory.

Keep a proof ledger with three columns: hypotheses, conclusion, and where each hypothesis is used. A correct computation can coexist with a false general theorem if a condition is missing. The closing test is whether you can identify and repair such a claim yourself.

The three modules

Limits and derivatives

Read A slope at an instant and A corner that defeats the derivative. The first gives an explicit tolerance choice for the derivative of x²; the second separates continuity from differentiability. Your bridge is to rewrite both conclusions in quantifier order: for every ε>0 there is a δ>0 such that all nonzero |h|<δ yield a quotient within ε of the claimed derivative. At the corner, show why no candidate derivative can satisfy that condition. The lesson is not that pictures are useless, but that a picture cannot quantify all sufficiently small steps.

Integral reasoning

Read Why accumulation undoes differentiation and The average speed you must have reached. The fundamental theorem proof controls a quotient by continuity of the input. The mean value theorem proof subtracts a secant line and uses a closed-interval extremum. They have different hypotheses and different conclusions. Place their assumptions side by side. For the mean value theorem, the extreme-value theorem is an invoked result from real analysis; this route does not reprove completeness of the reals. For the fundamental theorem, investigate what happens when the input jumps at the point of evaluation.

Approximation and counterexamples

Read An infinite journey with finite area, A polynomial approximation with an error receipt, and A smooth function its Taylor series misses. The bridge is that every infinity requires a specified limiting process. An improper integral moves a cutoff R to infinity; an infinite Taylor series moves degree n to infinity. A finite area or finite Taylor bound does not settle all analogous limits. The flat exponential example identifies an especially subtle failure: every coefficient can be correct while the series still misses the function. After each lesson, write the parameter that moves, what stays fixed, and a bound that genuinely tends to zero or fails to do so.

Twelve proof tasks with staged feedback

1. Explicit derivative. Prove d(x²)/dx at a equals 2a with a tolerance choice. Hint: cancel before taking a limit. Model: the quotient is 2a+h for h≠0; choose δ=ε. Then its distance from 2a is |h|<ε.

2. Continuity consequence. Prove differentiability at a implies continuity there. Hint: factor the height difference by h. Model: f(a+h)−f(a)=hQ(h), and Q(h) tends to finite f′(a), hence is bounded near zero. Thus the product tends to zero.

3. Failed converse. Give a continuous function not differentiable at zero and show both claims. Hint: absolute value. Model: ||h|−0|=|h|→0, but |h|/h is 1 for h>0 and −1 for h<0.

4. Flat-looking product. Prove x|x| has derivative zero at zero. Hint: simplify the quotient. Model: [h|h|−0]/h=|h|→0. A symbol containing |x| need not create a derivative failure.

5. Accumulation estimate. For f Riemann integrable on an interval containing x as an interior point and continuous at x, bound the difference between [∫ₓˣ⁺ʰf(t)dt]/h and f(x). Hint: subtract f(x) inside. Model: continuity gives |f(t)−f(x)|<ε on a sufficiently short interval; integrate to get error ≤ε|h|, then divide by |h|.

6. Jump counterexample. Find an integrable f for which A(x)=∫₀ˣf has no derivative at zero. Hint: choose different constant values on either side. Model: let f(t)=0 for t<0 and 1 for t≥0. Then A(h)=0 for h<0 and h for h>0. Its one-sided quotients are 0 and 1.

7. Rolle reduction. Reduce the mean value theorem to a zero-derivative result. Hint: subtract the secant line. Model: with m=[f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a), q(x)=f(x)−m(x−a) has q(a)=q(b). Rolle yields q′(c)=0, hence f′(c)=m.

8. Why endpoints count. Construct a function differentiable on (0,1) whose endpoint jump defeats the mean value conclusion. Hint: alter one endpoint of x. Model: set f(x)=x for x<1 and f(1)=3. Interior derivative is 1 while secant slope from 0 to 1 is 3; continuity at 1 fails.

9. Improper threshold. Prove ∫₁∞x−pdx is finite exactly when p>1. Hint: separate p=1. Model: for p≠1 the cutoff area is (R1−p−1)/(1−p). Its limit is 1/(p−1) when p>1 and infinity when p<1. For p=1, ln R→∞.

10. False height test. Refute “f(x)→0 implies ∫₁∞f converges” within nonnegative continuous functions. Hint: use a borderline power. Model: f(x)=1/x tends to zero, yet its cutoff integral ln R is unbounded.

11. Taylor receipt. Derive the bound for the linear Taylor polynomial of eˣ on [0,r] with r>0. Hint: bound the second derivative by er. Model: P₁(x)=1+x; the integral remainder is ∫₀ˣet(x−t)dt, so its absolute value is at most erx²/2 for 0≤x≤r.

12. Flatness mechanism. Explain why an exponential term beats every fixed pole at zero. Hint: y=1/x² and one term of ey's positive series. Model: for fixed k, |x|−ke−1/x²=yk/2e−y. Choose integer m>k/2; ey≥ym/m!, leaving an upper bound m!yk/2−m→0.

For each task, try the prompt, use the hint only if needed, then compare your proof against the model. Mark a proof incomplete if it uses a theorem but never checks its hypotheses. Tasks 1–4 map to limit-based derivatives, 5–8 to integral and existence reasoning, and 9–12 to limiting approximation.

Capstone: a three-function proof dossier

Task. Compare p(x)=x², c(x)=|x|, and b(x)=e−1/x² for x≠0 with b(0)=0. For each, decide continuity and differentiability at zero and justify the answer from a quotient. For p, give a first-degree Taylor approximation at zero and an exact residual. For c, explain why no first-degree Taylor polynomial is defined there. For b, prove all derivatives at zero vanish and exhibit a nearby point where its Taylor series is wrong. Finish with one paragraph separating differentiability, smoothness, and analyticity.

Model solution. p and c are continuous at zero because their values p(h)=h² and c(h)=|h| tend to zero. b is continuous by the dominance lemma in task 12, taking k=0. Their first quotients are h, |h|/h, and e−1/h²/h respectively. Thus p′(0)=0; c′(0) fails since its sides give ±1; b′(0)=0 by task 12 with k=1. The degree-one Taylor polynomial of p at zero is 0, and its exact residual is x². A first-degree Taylor polynomial of c would require c′(0), which does not exist. Repeated differentiation of b away from zero yields a polynomial in 1/x times e−1/x². Task 12 makes each such derivative and its difference quotient tend to zero at zero; induction gives b(n)(0)=0 for all n. Its Taylor series at zero is identically zero, but b(1/10)=e−100>0. Therefore p is analytic, c is continuous but not differentiable at zero, and b is smooth but not analytic there. Differentiability is one limiting rate, smoothness requires all finite orders, and analyticity requires equality to the infinite Taylor series on a neighbourhood.

Rubric, 16 points. Award three for the three continuity arguments, three for the derivative quotients and two-sided decision, two for p's exact Taylor residual, two for c's missing coefficient, four for a valid induction and exponential-dominance argument for b, and two for an exact nonzero witness and correct distinction among the three concepts. An unexplained “it is flat” receives no flatness-proof credit. The route's stopping point is a dossier that a reader could audit line by line, with every invoked theorem and assumption visible.

What this proof route establishes

The derivative tasks certify limit control and its boundary at corners. The integral tasks certify a local continuity argument and an existence argument with closed-interval hypotheses. The approximation tasks distinguish a convergent cutoff, a finite Taylor error, and an infinite series identity. These are selected results. Further study can supply proofs of the extreme-value theorem, more general integration theories, and conditions for analytic continuation; none is silently assumed as an outcome here.
