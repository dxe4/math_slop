Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Make a mathematical promise precise

This primer takes about five hours, including four article readings, six short proof attempts, mixed practice, and a capstone. It is for a reader comfortable with algebra and inequalities who wants a usable first picture of real analysis. The bounded outcome is to write and check a few quantified limit arguments, see why real completeness matters, and distinguish smooth rectangle behavior from a pathological bounded function. It is a selected introduction, not a replacement for a full proof course.

Entry recap. The symbol |x−a| means distance between x and a. “For every ε>0 there exists δ>0” means the requested output error comes first; the input tolerance may depend on it. “For every x” after δ means that same chosen δ must serve all eligible x. An upper bound lies above every element of a set, while the supremum is its least upper bound. If these phrases are unfamiliar, write one example with ε=0.1 and draw an interval before reading further. The articles repair their own local prerequisites, so a forgotten term need not end the route.

Four readings in three modules

  1. Quantified promises: How close is close enough? proves 1/n→0 with an explicit tail index. After reading, take ε=1/37 and choose an N. The bridge from sequences to functions is the role of the moving variable: n must eventually be large, whereas a function input x must be sufficiently close to a. In each case an adversary chooses ε first, and your response must cover every remaining eligible input.
  2. Completeness: The missing number in the rationals shows rational brackets around √2 while proving no rational endpoint can finish the job. The limit promise from module one cannot alone decide whether the proposed limit belongs to the number system. Real completeness answers that existence question. When the article invokes a supremum, name the set and specify the ambient system: ℚ and ℝ give different answers.
  3. Continuity and sums: One tolerance for the whole interval compares a δ near one point with a δ shared by all pairs. Then When rectangle sums refuse to agree shows that small cells can still have full oscillation. The connection is a discipline of quantifiers: “for all nearby points” gives local continuity, while “for all cells in every partition” exposes the rational indicator. A graph or finite sample cannot replace those universal claims.

Spend roughly 90 minutes reading and reproducing the worked examples, 80 minutes on the six proof attempts below, and 130 minutes on mixed practice, capstone, and revisions. These are study estimates rather than a speed target. At any module boundary you can stop with a complete result: an explicit sequence limit, a completeness counterexample, or a continuity and integrability distinction.

Six small proof attempts and one mixed problem

Before opening solutions in the articles, try these in order: (1) find N for 1/n<1/50; (2) explain why checking the first 1000 terms never proves a limit; (3) square 1.41 and 1.42 to bracket √2; (4) state which step in the rational-gap argument needs the real least-upper-bound axiom; (5) give a shared δ for x² on [−2,2] when ε=1/5; (6) say why every partition cell for the rational indicator has upper height one. A correct explanation should name both a hypothesis and a reason; a memorized slogan earns no credit in the self-check.

Mixed practice. Define aₙ=3/n and f(x)=x² on [−2,2]. For ε=0.06, find a tail index N that makes |aₙ|<ε. For the same ε, find one δ that makes |f(x)−f(y)|<ε whenever |x−y|<δ. Finally explain why your N and δ do different jobs.

Worked mixed solution

Because 0.06=3/50, we need 3/n<3/50, or n>50. N=51 works for every n≥51. On [−2,2], |x+y|≤4, hence |x²−y²|≤4|x−y|. Choose δ=0.015=3/200; then |x−y|<δ implies an output difference strictly below 4·0.015=0.06. N governs all late indices of one sequence. δ governs all close pairs across a domain. Both are responses to an ε request, but their universal clauses differ.

Capstone: three promises, two repairs

Write a gallery with three correct arguments and two repaired false ones. A: prove 2/n→0 with an explicit N(ε). B: prove x↦3x is continuous at a=2 by giving δ(ε). C: prove x² is uniformly continuous on [−1,1]. False claim D: “Because 1/n is small for n=1000, its limit is zero.” False claim E: “Because rationals occur in every interval, their indicator must be Riemann integrable.” Repair both with a quantified statement or a counterargument. Keep the whole gallery to one or two pages; the task is clarity of logical scope.

Model solution

A: for ε>0 choose integer N>2/ε; then n≥N gives 2/n≤2/N<ε. B: |3x−6|=3|x−2|, so δ=ε/3 works. C: |x²−y²|=|x−y||x+y|≤2|x−y| on [−1,1], so δ=ε/2 works for all pairs. D fails because one term says nothing about an infinite tail; replace it by A’s “for every ε, every n≥N” argument, with 1 instead of 2 in the numerator. E fails because each positive-width cell contains rational and irrational inputs; its supremum is one and infimum zero. Every partition has upper sum one and lower sum zero. Density here creates the obstruction, not integrability.
