Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Recover three proof moves

This refresher assumes school algebra is comfortable and asks for written proofs, not only correct answers. Plan around eight hours over several sessions: read five lessons, rebuild their arguments from memory, then complete the mixed problem and capstone. The route has two bounded outcomes. You will use a contrapositive and induction deliberately, and you will separate an existence claim from a uniqueness claim. It does not attempt a full logic or number theory course.

Entry recap. An implication “if P, then Q” permits P as a hypothesis and owes Q as a conclusion. The converse reverses the arrow and needs independent justification. An integer is even when it has form 2k and odd when it has form 2k + 1. For a sequence indexed by positive integers, induction requires a true base and a step from an established case to the next. The lessons repair these ideas locally, so this recap is a check rather than a gate.

Module 1: Quick recall

  1. How to read a definition without fear. Spend less time on easy classifications and more on the witness domain. Write down why n/2 is not always an admissible integer witness.
  2. What a proof actually owes. Draw an arrow map for “divisible by 6 implies even,” then cross out the unjustified reverse arrow.

These two articles are the quality check for later proofs. Every method still needs definitions at its endpoints and valid reasons between them. A contrapositive changes which endpoint you start from; it does not remove the obligation to prove an arrow. Induction repeats an arrow over many indices; it also does not excuse a missing base. If either recap is unexpectedly hard, write its exercises before continuing.

Module 2: Proof engines

  1. Proving backward by contrapositive. Rebuild the odd-square algebra without looking. State both the original claim and its contrapositive; do not call the converse a contrapositive.
  2. Induction is a chain with a base. Reproduce the sum proof with the hypothesis at n explicitly marked and the target at n + 1 left unassumed.
  3. Existence and uniqueness are two jobs. Read the general uniqueness proof slowly. Underline where the remainder bound is used and identify the general existence step that the article only sketches.

The bridge among these methods is the shape of the obligation. Contrapositive is useful when negating the conclusion gives a tractable input form. Induction is useful when the claim is indexed by every positive integer and the next case depends on an earlier case. Uniqueness starts by supposing two proposed answers already exist and proving they coincide; that argument alone never manufactures an answer. Choose a method only after stating the precise theorem. A polished proof with the wrong direction or missing existence half does not meet its target.

Worked mixed practice: odd squares and a remainder

Suppose an integer x satisfies x² = 6q + 3 for some integer q. Show that x is odd. Then find the standard quotient and remainder when the concrete value x² = 81 is divided by 6, and say which part of the division statement your calculation establishes. This mixes a parity implication with a quotient–remainder witness.

Hint

Prove the parity claim by the contrapositive: if x is even, what is the remainder of its square modulo 2? For 81, choose a multiple of 6 just below it.

Worked solution

If x were even, x = 2k and x² = 4k², which is even. But 6q + 3 = 2(3q + 1) + 1 is odd. An integer cannot be both even and odd, so x is odd. Equivalently, this proves the contrapositive “even x makes the displayed form impossible.” For 81, write 81 = 6·13 + 3 with 0 ≤ 3 < 6. This constructs a quotient–remainder pair for this one input. The general uniqueness theorem shows it is the only standard pair for 81 and 6; the one calculation is not a proof that pairs exist for all dividends.

As an induction check, prove a different short claim: 1 + 3 + ··· + (2n − 1) = n². The base n = 1 gives 1 = 1. If the first n odd terms total n², the next term is 2(n + 1) − 1 = 2n + 1, and the new total is n² + 2n + 1 = (n + 1)². This is a complete proof, but write it yourself before using the displayed version as a check.

Capstone: audit two parity arguments

Consider the claim “For every integer n, if n² is odd, then n is odd.” Argument A says: “An odd square has an odd root because odd numbers square to odd numbers.” Argument B says: “If n is even, write n = 2k. Then n² = 2(2k²), even. Therefore, if n² is odd, n cannot be even and must be odd.” Identify the defect, if any, in each argument. Then give an induction proof of 1 + 3 + ··· + (2n − 1) = n² with base and step labelled. Finally, for the concrete square 25, exhibit its standard quotient and remainder upon division by 4 and explain whether your computation proves existence generally.

Model solution

Argument A proves or recalls the direction “odd n ⇒ odd n²,” then reverses it without justification. The desired claim is true, but A’s stated reason is a converse error. Argument B is valid: even n yields even square, which is the contrapositive of odd square ⇒ odd n. The coefficient 2k² is an integer, and parity exhausts the integer possibilities. For induction, the base at 1 is 1 = 1². Assume the sum through 2n − 1 equals n². Add 2n + 1 to get n² + 2n + 1 = (n + 1)², exactly the next case. Thus the identity holds for every positive integer. Finally 25 = 4·6 + 1 with 0 ≤ 1 < 4. This establishes existence for the particular dividend 25 and divisor 4, and the general uniqueness theorem rules out another standard pair. It does not establish general existence.

Self-check rubric. Award two marks for identifying A’s unproved converse and B’s valid contrapositive with its integer witness; two for the induction base and a step that assumes only the earlier case; and two for the correct quotient–remainder pair and honest scope statement. A score of six means the three proof obligations are separately clear. If one element is missing, revisit its linked lesson and rewrite that element rather than rereading the whole course.

Stop after you can repair the argument

A good stopping point is a clean capstone written without the model: two parity directions correctly distinguished, an induction step with no circular assumption, and a remainder calculation described as one existence witness. That is useful recovered proof fluency. The deep route asks for longer arguments about all earlier cases, preserved states, and infinite pairings if you want to continue.
