Build a small piece of Euclidean geometry

This route takes about four hours including drawing, the four linked lessons, exercises, and the capstone. Its destination is modest and concrete: make an exact angle bisector, explain why it works, and use parallel lines and circle arcs to reason about angles. You will finish with a diagram you can justify rather than one you merely measured. The route stays in Euclidean geometry; it does not promise a survey of every geometry.

Entry recap. An angle is formed by two rays at a common vertex. In a triangle, equal side lengths support SSS congruence, meaning the triangles match in all corresponding angles. The length of a displacement (a,b) is √(a²+b²). A line parallel to another does not meet it, and in the Euclidean plane a transversal makes equal alternate interior angles. A circle consists of points at one fixed distance from its centre. If any of these terms feels rusty, read the capsule at the beginning of each linked lesson before continuing.

Use a pencil, straightedge, compass if available, and a separate sheet for reasons. Ideal constructions are exact mathematical objects even when a physical line is thick. Give every claim a named equality or theorem. A compass is helpful for exploration, but the static numerical examples in the lessons let you complete the route without special equipment.

Module 1: lengths and constructions

  1. Why a detour cannot be shorter. Begin with two arrows. Calculate direct and walked distances for at least one pair that ties and one pair that does not. The proof shows why all pairs obey the bound, while the equality case teaches you to identify a geometric condition rather than only a number.
  2. A compass bisects an angle. Now construct two equal-distance points on the rays, then a point equidistant from them. Record the three pairs of equal sides before looking at the finished ray. The length work from the first article makes “equal radius” an exact distance statement; SSS converts it into an angle statement.

The bridge between these readings is the difference between a distance constraint and an angle conclusion. In the first, coordinates prove an inequality about distance. In the second, exact equal distances create two congruent triangles. The drawing helps you find the triangles, but SSS is what certifies the half-angle.

Module 2: angles

  1. Where triangle angle sums come from. Add a parallel through one vertex of a triangle and label which alternate interior angle represents each distant corner. The parallel line is the key Euclidean assumption.
  2. The angle at the circumference. Fix a chord and mark the arc on which the third point lies. The inscribed angle is half its intercepted central arc, which can be the long arc. The arc restriction prevents an attractive but false diagram claim.

This module moves from a straight-angle sum to an arc-angle relation. In both cases, the proof relocates an angle without measuring it: parallel lines relocate triangle corners, while equal radii in isosceles triangles relate a circumference angle to a central one. Keep the reason attached to each relocated angle.

Worked mixed practice before the final construction

Suppose a 70° Euclidean angle is bisected by equal-radius arcs. Each part is 35° by SSS and angle addition. Now imagine that 70° is a central angle AOB in a circle and P lies on the major arc AB. The inscribed angle APB is 35° as well, but for a different reason: P intercepts the 70° minor arc. The matching numbers do not make the two arguments interchangeable. The bisector statement concerns two triangles sharing a side; the circle statement concerns a chord and an arc.

Check the other arc as a failure test. If P moves to the minor arc AB, it intercepts the 290° major arc and APB becomes 145°. A drawing that merely says “half the central angle” would now be wrong. Also, neither calculation by itself proves the Euclidean triangle angle sum; that proof needs a parallel line. When you can tell these three reasons apart, you are ready for the capstone.

Capstone: one bisector and one circle configuration

Construct the bisector of a right angle at O using only equal-radius circles and an ideal straightedge. Label the two triangles whose congruence proves the result. Then, separately, choose a circle with central right angle AOB and a point P on the major arc AB. Find APB and explain which arc it intercepts. Give one sentence on why a point P on the minor arc has a different angle. You may use a coordinate sketch to check your answers.

Construction hint

Place the initial equal-radius marks at X=(2,0) and Y=(0,2). Equal circles centred at X and Y can meet inside the angle at C=(2,2).

Model solution and reasons

Take O=(0,0) and the two rays along the positive coordinate axes. A circle of radius 2 centred at O gives X=(2,0) and Y=(0,2), hence OX=OY. Draw circles of radius 2 centred at X and Y. They meet inside the angle at C=(2,2), so XC=YC=2. Join O to C. Triangles OXC and OYC have OX=OY, XC=YC, and common side OC. By SSS, ∠XOC=∠COY. Since together they form a right angle, each is 45°. This is a construction proof; the coordinates are an optional exact check.

For the circle part take the unit circle centred at O, A=(1,0), B=(0,1), and P=(−1,0). AOB is 90° and P lies on the major arc AB, so it intercepts the 90° minor arc and APB=45°. If P instead lies on the minor arc, it intercepts the 270° major arc and APB=135°. The difference is the chosen intercepted arc, not a change to the chord AB.

Self-review rubric. Give one point each for an unambiguous construction, all three SSS side equalities, the 45° bisector conclusion, the correct major-arc circle calculation, and the minor-arc countercase. Five points show a complete solution; if the angle numbers are right but the equalities or arc are absent, return to the relevant article. This is practice feedback, not a credential.

A satisfying place to stop

You can stop after producing one diagram with a real congruence argument and one circle angle with its arc named. The linked articles also show why a direct route bounds a detour and why a flat triangle's corners total π. If you want to continue, the accelerated route asks what changes when a “line” means a great circle or a disk geodesic. That is a new model question; it does not weaken the Euclidean proofs you have completed here.
