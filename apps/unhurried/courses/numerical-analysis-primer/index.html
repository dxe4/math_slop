Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Trusting an approximate answer

This path teaches three practical questions: what information did rounding discard, can algebra give a safer expression, and can a root estimate carry a location guarantee? Allow about 250 minutes for four lessons, calculations, and the final report. The scope is deliberately bounded: you will not survey numerical linear algebra, integration, or differential equations. A calculator helps with decimals, but every certificate here can be checked through exact fractions or signs.

Entry recap. Scientific notation writes a quantity as a significand times a power of ten. A function f assigns an output f(x) to x. A root satisfies f(x)=0. A continuous function has no jump across its interval. A derivative gives tangent slope; the Newton lesson defines the particular quotient it needs. If derivatives are new, complete the first three lessons and the bracket capstone before treating the Newton comparison as an optional extension.

Use a notebook with three columns for every answer: the computed value, the mathematical claim you can justify, and the assumption that makes the claim true. “My calculator printed it” belongs only in the first column. This habit will make the final report readable to someone who did not watch your work.

Four lessons in three modules

Module 1: Finite arithmetic

Read Why a computer loses a tiny number. Work its decimal toy examples by hand and identify each rounding point. Then try 10.0+0.04 under the stated three-digit rule. This module explains why a small quantity may vanish and why doing several rounded additions differs from rounding one exact sum. Bridge to the next module by asking whether the order and form of the computation can preserve a small answer.

Module 2: Stable formulas

Read Two equal formulas, unequal answers. Verify the rationalizing identity in exact algebra before comparing rounded calculations. An identity guarantees equal real outputs on its domain; the two evaluation routes still have different intermediate values. The bridge to roots is a warning: a plausible number is not yet a certified answer. For a root, we will track an interval rather than only the last decimal printed.

Module 3: Root finding

Read A root you can trap and then Newton’s method can leap the wrong way. Keep the bisection invariant in view: continuity and reliable opposite signs trap a root while width halves. Newton’s tangent step can be faster near a simple root but has no general global guarantee. In a two-column comparison, record bracket width for bisection and both iterate and residual for Newton. Do not call a Newton residual a location bound unless another argument supplies one.

One chain of reasoning

Suppose a model asks for the positive solution of x²−2=0. First ask whether the function values you use have trustworthy signs. With exact rational endpoints 1 and 2, the signs −1 and +2 are certain. Bisection then gives a sequence of certified brackets. A Newton estimate such as 17/12 can be useful inside that bracket, but if it ever leaves the bracket, the bracket itself remains the guarantee. The computation and the certificate play different roles.

The small-decimal lesson matters even here. With machine arithmetic, a midpoint may round to an existing endpoint after many halvings. At that point the width no longer decreases in the stored format, so a program must stop or use a stronger representation. Similarly, a computed function sign near zero may be uncertain. In this course’s hand calculations, exact rational squares make the signs reliable; an actual software implementation must justify them separately.

Worked mixed practice

Find a safe way to evaluate √(1.0004)−1 and then give a certified bracket for the positive root of x²−2 with width at most 1/8. State what each result promises.

Worked answer

For x=0.0004, rationalize: √(1+x)−1=x/(√(1+x)+1). The exact identity holds because the denominator is positive. The value is about 0.00019998; this decimal is an approximation, and its last digits require a specified arithmetic model to certify. For the root, f(1)=−1 and f(2)=2. Midpoints 3/2, 5/4, and 11/8 have signs positive, negative, and negative respectively: f(11/8)=121/64−128/64=−7/64. The retained bracket is [11/8,3/2], width 1/8. Continuity puts a root there; monotonicity on the positive interval makes it the unique positive root. Its midpoint 23/16 is within 1/16 of that root.

The first half of this practice checks a safer expression but does not itself certify a rounded decimal. The second half gives a location certificate without needing a decimal expansion of √2. Notice that the same habit—stating the exact algebra before trusting an approximation—connects both halves.

Capstone: a root report someone else can audit

Use f(x)=x²−3 on [1,2]. Produce a bracket of width at most 1/8, state a midpoint and its certified positional error, and take two unguarded Newton steps from x₀=2. Compare the two outputs. Explain one way Newton could fail for a different function.

Model report

f(1)=−2 and f(2)=1. At 3/2, f=−3/4, so retain [3/2,2]. At 7/4, f=1/16, so retain [3/2,7/4]. At 13/8, f=169/64−192/64=−23/64, so retain [13/8,7/4]. Width is 7/4−13/8=1/8. Its midpoint is 27/16=1.6875, with error at most 1/16 from the root enclosed by continuity. Since x²−3 is strictly increasing on [1,2], this root is unique there.

Newton uses x_{n+1}=(x_n+3/x_n)/2. From 2 it gives 7/4, then 97/56≈1.732143. This second iterate is numerically much closer to √3 than the bracket midpoint, but the bare iteration does not supply the same rigorous location bound. For g(x)=x³−2x+2, starting at 0 cycles between 0 and 1, as the linked lesson calculates. A zero derivative is another explicit failure mode.

A satisfying stopping point

You can stop when you can give one approximate value and one honest statement of what supports it. For this route, that means a finite-precision calculation with declared rules, an algebraic repair for cancellation, and a root bracket with explicit assumptions. Further study can add derivative-based error bounds, interval arithmetic, and implementation details. Completing this short route is evidence of these selected skills, not of general numerical-analysis mastery.
