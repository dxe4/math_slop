Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Two small tests for a big subject

This route gives a first encounter with two kinds of topological reasoning. One is local: is there room around every point of a subset of the real line? The other is a count on a finite drawing: what happens to vertices, edges and faces when you add a line? Allow about three hours for reading, doing both lessons' exercises, the mixed calculation, and the capstone. The goal is a reliable first test for openness and an honest Euler count, not a survey of all topology.

Entry recap. An interval (a,b) omits its endpoints; [a,b] includes them. The distance from x to y on the line is |x−y|. A graph here is a finite collection of vertices joined by edges. A plane drawing has no edge crossings except shared endpoints and divides the plane into faces; the unbounded outside region counts as a face. Those definitions are repeated where each lesson needs them, so you can enter either lesson directly.

Work through the two catalog modules

  1. Local room: Open means room to move. At each included x, choose a radius ε that stays inside the set. Pause at [2,5): make yourself name its bad point and a point just outside every attempted neighborhood. The lesson's half-gap method is more useful than saying an interval “looks open.”
  2. Counting a surface: Why a plane drawing keeps giving two. Record V, E and F at each stage of its graph construction. The article proves a plane-graph formula conditional on a face-splitting lemma. For the sphere examples below, a planar drawing of a polyhedron's skeleton counts one surface face as the outside planar face; this is a bridge for these examples, not a proof about arbitrary surfaces.

These modules ask different questions. Openness tests every point of a set, so one counterexample point defeats the claim. Euler's formula checks a global count after a permitted graph construction. In both, the first useful move is to state what is being counted or quantified: all included points in the first lesson, all faces including the outside in the second. The number 2 alone does not identify a sphere or classify a space.

A worked mixed checkpoint

Consider A=(−1,0)∪(0,2). Is A open in ℝ? Then take a square drawn with one diagonal and count its regions. For the first task, a point x in (−1,0) has a radius half the smaller of x+1 and −x. A point x in (0,2) has radius half the smaller of x and 2−x. Each chosen radius is positive and stays in its component, so A is open. The missing point 0 is irrelevant to openness because the definition tests points in A; its absence makes the two components separate but does not spoil local room.

For the square, V=4, E=5, and F=3: two triangular interiors plus one outside region. Thus V−E+F=4−5+3=2. If someone reports F=2, ask whether the outside was omitted. If they report that the open set A has “two faces,” they have mixed the objects: A is a subset of the line, while the square is a plane graph. The bridge is a habit of precise definitions rather than one formula applying to both.

Test two sets and count two sphere models

Submit a short page with four parts. Decide whether U=(−2,1)∪(3,4) and W=[−2,1) are open in ℝ, giving either a point-dependent radius or a boundary counterexample. Then compute V, E and F for the boundary of a tetrahedron and the boundary of an octahedron, treating each as a closed triangulated sphere. State why an outside face appears in a plane drawing of each skeleton and what the shared Euler value does not prove.

Capstone hint

For U, handle the two components separately. For W, test its included left endpoint. The tetrahedron has four triangular faces; the octahedron has eight. Each edge belongs to two triangular faces.

Model solution

For x∈(−2,1), choose ε=min(x+2,1−x)/2. For x∈(3,4), choose ε=min(x−3,4−x)/2. In either case the neighborhood stays inside U, so U is open. W is not open: −2 belongs to W, and for any ε>0 the point −2−ε/2 lies in its ε-neighborhood but outside W.

A tetrahedron has V=4 vertices, E=6 edges and F=4 triangular faces, giving 4−6+4=2. An octahedron has V=6, E=12 and F=8, giving 6−12+8=2. The edge counts can be checked from triangle incidences: 4·3/2=6 and 8·3/2=12 because each edge is on two faces. For these polyhedral spheres, drawing a connected skeleton in the plane puts one chosen surface face around the outside; it is still one of the F faces. Both counts equal 2. This agreement is an invariant calculation for these triangulations, not a proof that any object with value 2 is homeomorphic to a sphere.

Self-review rubric. A complete answer gives a positive radius for every point of U, a specific excluded neighbor of −2 for every ε, all six labelled polyhedron counts, and a sentence including the outside-face convention. It also avoids turning equal Euler values into a classification theorem. A correct final number without the reason for it is only a partial answer.

A satisfying stopping point

You can stop after you can reproduce the two open-set decisions and both counts without reopening the solutions. The next route adds maps between spaces and global properties. Nothing in this primer establishes a general surface-classification result; it supplies two exact, reusable examples. If the radius choices still feel mysterious, repeat the first module with a new union of intervals before moving on.
