Counting by structure

This is a sustained proof route through three selected counting methods: signed overlap correction, finite generating polynomials, and bijections. Allow about fifteen hours, including each linked article, written proof work, and the capstone. The outcome is a defensible count of a constrained finite choice problem by two methods. This route does not cover the full field of enumerative combinatorics or infinite generating series. Its “deep” label refers to the requirement that you show why each correspondence and correction is exact, including small boundary cases.

Entry recap. You should already be comfortable with C(n,k), factorials, and polynomial multiplication. Recall that C(n,k) counts subsets of size k from n distinct labels; choosing bar positions gives a bijection to nonnegative allocations of n identical items among k named recipients. In a finite generating product, [xʳ]P(x) denotes the coefficient of xʳ. We use formal polynomials: x is bookkeeping and no numerical limit is taken. For inclusion–exclusion, the central question is how many times one object occurs in a signed sum. These local reminders repair notation, but the proofs require you to pause and write your own maps or signed ledgers.

Ordered modules and bridges

Module 1: Overlap

  1. Counting overlaps only once — Count union of two and three finite sets without double counting.
  2. No gift returns to its owner — Count derangements by inclusion–exclusion for small n and derive formula.

First read the union formula and the derangement count as one argument in two settings. In a mailing-list union, an attendee can be in several sets. In a gift exchange, an assignment can violate several “person receives own gift” conditions. In both settings, adding single-event counts and alternating intersections gives each acceptable object weight one and each rejected object weight zero. After the derangements lesson, prove that a permutation fixing exactly t>0 people has net weight zero by pairing subsets of its violations: choose one fixed person and toggle whether that person appears in a selected subset. The two terms differ in sign. This finite pairing is stronger evidence than a table of Dₙ values. Check n=0 and n=1 explicitly before assuming a pattern.

Module 2: Algebra of choices

  1. A polynomial is a counting machine — Read coefficients of a generating function as counts of choices.
  2. Distributing identical treats — Derive weak compositions with bars and stars.

Now move from forbidden-event corrections to algebra that records choices. In a product ∏(1+xwi), a factor chooses “leave” or “take” for one distinct token. Write down the exact function from a subset to one product term, then its reverse. Coefficients add when different subsets have equal total weight. Stars and bars is another bijection, this time from a tuple of nonnegative shares to a string of stars and bars. The two tools solve different problems: generating factors count labeled token selections, while bar patterns count distributions of identical units. A repeated weight does not make tokens identical. For example, (1+x)² has coefficient two at x, but one inventory of two indistinguishable tokens is encoded by 1+x+x². Explain that distinction before calculating any capstone coefficient.

Module 3: Proof by partition

  1. A doorway into Pascal's triangle — Prove Pascal's identity by partitioning subsets.

Pascal's identity is the proof-by-partition bridge tying the route together. Fix one distinguished object and classify subsets by whether they contain it. The include and exclude classes have sizes C(n−1,k−1) and C(n−1,k). Both a generating product and a bar string can also be partitioned by a chosen factor or position, producing recurrences. To practice, split subsets of {1,2,3,4,5} of size two by whether they contain 5. The counts are C(4,2)=6 and C(4,1)=4, totaling 10. Then split binary selections of weights 1,2,3 by whether weight 3 is used. For total 3, the used case contributes one, and the unused case contributes one through 1+2. This is the same case logic acting on different mathematical objects.

Worked mixed practice

As a worked mixed problem, choose a subset of distinct tokens with weights 1,2,2, where the two weight-two tokens carry separate names A and B. The product is (1+x)(1+x²)². To reach weight two, choose A alone or B alone: [x²] is two. To reach weight three, combine the weight-one token with A or with B: [x³] is two. To reach weight four, take both A and B: [x⁴] is one. These coefficients sum with the others to 2³=8 total subsets when x=1. If A and B become indistinguishable copies of one token type, the one-copy choice is unique; the inventory factor for that type becomes 1+x²+x⁴ and total visible choices become 2×3=6. A single algebraic symbol can therefore count different objects depending on the factor interpretation; the proof must state the interpretation.

Capstone

You have four distinct tokens A,B,C,D with respective weights 1,1,2,3. Select a subset whose total weight is exactly 4. Count the valid subsets first by direct cases and then as a coefficient of the finite generating polynomial. Give a reversible explanation of the coefficient interpretation. Next, a tempting shortcut says to distribute four identical units among the four token labels by stars and bars and declare the result to be the number of valid subsets. Refute it with a specific forbidden allocation and explain the model mismatch. Finally, require that at least one weight-one token be chosen. Give an inclusion–exclusion count for the valid weight-four subsets satisfying that requirement, and reconcile it with your direct list. Treat A and B as distinct even though they share a weight.

Model solution

Direct cases: if D of weight three is chosen, one further unit is needed, so choose A or B. This yields {D,A} and {D,B}. If D is absent, C of weight two must be chosen: taking both A and B adds two, giving {C,A,B}. Without C or D, A and B together total only two and cannot reach four. Thus there are three valid subsets. The product is P(x)=(1+x)²(1+x²)(1+x³). One can expand the first two factors to (1+2x+x²)(1+x²)=1+2x+2x²+2x³+x⁴. Multiplying by (1+x³), the x⁴ coefficient receives one from x⁴·1 and two from 2x·x³, totaling three. These are exactly {C,A,B} and the two D pairs. For the reversible interpretation, choose 1 or x in the A and B factors, 1 or x² in the C factor, and 1 or x³ in the D factor. Each subset makes one unique product-term choice; reading which nonconstant terms were chosen recovers it. Exponent addition gives total weight, so collecting x⁴ terms counts the desired subsets. Stars and bars would count nonnegative quadruples summing to four, C(7,3)=35, such as (4,0,0,0). That tuple gives four identical units to A, while a subset either includes A once or excludes it. It also ignores the tokens' unequal weights; even (0,0,4,0) is not a permissible selection of C. Thus 35 does not count our subset universe. For the extra requirement, let E_A and E_B be weight-four valid subsets containing A and B respectively. From the list, E_A={ {D,A},{C,A,B} } has two members and E_B={ {D,B},{C,A,B} } also has two. Their intersection has the one subset {C,A,B}. Inclusion–exclusion gives |E_A∪E_B|=2+2−1=3, matching every valid subset in the direct list. An equally sound coefficient calculation restricts to outcomes using at least one A/B factor, replacing (1+x)² by 2x+x² and taking [x⁴](2x+x²)(1+x²)(1+x³)=3.

Assessment rubric

Award two points for an exhaustive direct partition by D and C with all three distinct subsets, two for the correct polynomial and coefficient arithmetic, two for an explicit forward and reverse subset-to-term map, one for a concrete stars-and-bars counterexample with the reason it is forbidden, two for the inclusion–exclusion event counts and overlap, and one for reconciling all three valid subsets. An unexplained answer “three” earns little: the aim is to show why none of the methods omits or duplicates an outcome. If you choose a different case split, check that it is both disjoint and exhaustive. If you expand the entire polynomial, only the x⁴ coefficient needs checking, but display enough terms to verify its sources.

Outcomes and a stopping point

  • Prove derangement formula. Write the signed intersection ledger in the derangements lesson and the fixed-point subset cancellation proof in the first bridge.
  • Interpret generating coefficients. Extract the coefficient of x to the fourth power in the capstone polynomial and explain which three subsets supply it.
  • Use bijections. Give the forward and reverse subset-to-product-term map in the capstone, then explain why the stars-and-bars map counts a different universe.

A satisfying stopping point is a clean written capstone proof that another reader could audit without seeing your computations. It should identify the universe of distinct tokens, show why each subset maps to exactly one product term, and state why each signed inclusion–exclusion contribution survives once. For further work, replace D's weight three by four. Recompute the direct list and coefficient, then decide whether the inclusion–exclusion event counts still match the old ones. They should change because the total-weight constraint changes, even though the general identities remain true. This is a selected proof window into counting by structure, with deeper general generating-function theory left for a later study route.
