Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

A proof dossier from structure to fitting

This is a sustained, selected route for a reader already comfortable with 2×2 matrices, independent vectors, dot products and proof. Its main deliverables are two complete arguments: rank-nullity for a finite-dimensional domain and least-squares orthogonality under full column rank. You will also interpret the singular values of a concrete 2D map. The general spectral theorem is a named dependency for the singular-value construction, not a theorem proved in this route. The result is proof practice in a bounded set of ideas, not a degree-level survey of linear algebra.

Plan about fourteen hours, including approximately three hours of lesson reading, at least six hours of reconstructing arguments without looking, and the remaining time for numerical work and the capstone. A useful entry test is to prove that if independent vectors v₁,v₂ satisfy a₁v₁+a₂v₂=0, then a₁=a₂=0, and to expand ||r−Az||² using dot products. If either move is unfamiliar, first work through the basis and dot-product lessons as refreshers. Throughout, maps are real and finite-dimensional unless a statement explicitly narrows to R².

Two modules with proof checkpoints

Module 1: Finite-dimensional structure

  1. A matrix is a rule for moving space. Reprove that basis images determine a map. Record precisely where additivity and scalar homogeneity enter.
  2. Coordinates depend on a basis. Convert between addresses using P and P⁻¹; explain why a basis must be independent. Verify one example by reconstruction.
  3. Missing dimensions have to go somewhere. Reconstruct the proof: extend a kernel basis and show the images of added vectors both span and are independent.
  4. What determinant actually counts. In two dimensions, link nonzero signed area to invertibility. Keep this special shortcut separate from general rank-nullity, which applies to rectangular maps.

The bridge is the choice of a basis suited to a map. Put kernel directions first: their output is zero. Extend that list to an input basis: the remaining output vectors form an image basis. This turns rank-nullity into a count of an explicitly organized basis. The existence of a basis extension is a standard finite-dimensional theorem used here; the rest of the rank-nullity proof must be supplied in full. Test the limits with a zero map R³→R²: rank zero, nullity three, no determinant. A determinant cannot replace the argument outside square 2D examples.

Module 2: Spectral and fitting ideas

  1. Directions a map does not turn. Derive the 2×2 characteristic equation and distinguish real eigenlines from complex roots. Use a quarter-turn as a counterexample to a real-eigenvector assumption.
  2. The closest answer to an impossible system. Prove that a full-column-rank normal-equation solution has residual orthogonal to every column, then expand a squared norm to certify the unique minimum.
  3. Rotate, stretch, rotate. Derive the 2D factorization from the symmetric spectral theorem for AᵀA. Identify exactly which claim is imported rather than proved.

The bridge is that AᵀA appears for two different reasons. In least squares, Aᵀ(b−Ax)=0 tests whether the residual is perpendicular to every prediction direction. In SVD, vᵀAᵀAv=||Av||² measures the squared stretch of an input direction. Full column rank makes AᵀA positive definite and the least-squares coefficients unique; a zero singular value exposes a kernel and loss of uniqueness. These connections are exact, although computing via normal equations can be numerically less stable than QR or SVD. This course proves an algebraic minimum, not a floating-point algorithm's reliability.

Worked mixed example: a small projection

Fit one constant c to observations b=(1,3,5). Let A=(1,1,1)ᵀ. The normal equation is AᵀAc=Aᵀb, hence 3c=9 and c=3. The residual is r=(−2,0,2), orthogonal to the sole column because −2+0+2=0. For any change t, the new residual is r−tA; its squared norm is ||r||²+t²||A||²=8+3t², so c=3 is uniquely best. The map A:R→R³ has rank one and nullity zero, matching its one-dimensional domain. Its only singular value is √(AᵀA)=√3, a measure of how much the unit input changes prediction length. This example connects rank, projection, normal equations and singular value without claiming that a rectangular A has a 2×2 determinant.

Proof checkpoint: if the column of A were zero instead, AᵀA=0. Every c would predict zero, so the residual could still be orthogonal to the column space while the coefficient was not unique. State which step of the preceding uniqueness proof fails: the equality ||Az||²=0 would no longer force z=0.

Fit a line to inconsistent data

At times t=0,1,2, measurements are 1,2,2. Fit a line a+bt by least squares. Set up A and data column d; compute coefficients a and b exactly, list predictions and residuals, and verify both residual orthogonality equations. Then find the rank and nullity of A and the singular values of A. Attach two general proof paragraphs: one proving rank-nullity by kernel-basis extension, and one proving the least-squares minimum from normal equations under full column rank. Finally name the theorem needed to justify an SVD for an arbitrary real 2×2 matrix.

Model solution, numerical part

The design matrix is A=[[1,0],[1,1],[1,2]], with data column d=(1,2,2)ᵀ. AᵀA=[[3,3],[3,5]] and Aᵀd=(5,6)ᵀ. The equations 3a+3b=5 and 3a+5b=6 give b=1/2 and a=7/6. Predictions are (7/6,5/3,13/6); residual d−A(a,b)ᵀ is (−1/6,1/3,−1/6). Its entries sum to zero, and the time-weighted sum is 0·(−1/6)+1·(1/3)+2·(−1/6)=0. Thus Aᵀr=0. The columns (1,1,1)ᵀ and (0,1,2)ᵀ are independent because the first coordinate forces any zero combination's first coefficient to vanish, and the second then forces the other to vanish. Hence rank A=2, nullity A=0. The eigenvalues of AᵀA solve (3−λ)(5−λ)−9=λ²−8λ+6=0, giving λ=4±√10; both are positive. Singular values are √(4+√10) and √(4−√10). They measure lengths along orthogonal principal input directions, not the line's slope and intercept themselves.

Model solution, proof part

For rank-nullity, let k₁,…,kr be a basis of ker T and extend it to k₁,…,kr,v₁,…,vs for the finite-dimensional domain. Each T(x) is a combination of T(vⱼ), since T(kᵢ)=0. If ∑cⱼT(vⱼ)=0, then ∑cⱼvⱼ lies in ker T, hence equals a combination of the kᵢ. Independence of the extended basis forces all cⱼ=0. Thus T(v₁),…,T(vs) is an image basis and dim ker T+dim im T=r+s=dim domain.

For least squares with full-column-rank A, AᵀA is invertible: zᵀAᵀAz=||Az||²=0 implies z=0. Let x̂ solve AᵀAx̂=Aᵀd and put r=d−Ax̂. Then r·Az=zᵀAᵀr=0 for every z. Any other coefficient x̂+z has squared residual ||r−Az||²=||r||²+||Az||², greater unless z=0. That proves the unique global minimum. To justify the 2D SVD construction generally, import the real symmetric spectral theorem for AᵀA; the linked lesson derives the remaining steps, including zero singular values.

Rubric. Two points for exact normal equations and coefficients; two for predictions, residual and both orthogonality checks; one for rank/nullity with independent-column reasoning; one for exact singular values; two for a complete basis-extension proof including independence of image vectors; two for the orthogonality/Pythagorean minimum proof with full-rank hypothesis. A clear statement of the spectral-theorem dependency is required for full credit. Arithmetic alone cannot substitute for the two proofs.

Stop with two arguments you can reproduce

A satisfying stopping point is the ability to write both proofs without consulting the model solution and to explain why zero singular values correspond to lost directions. The route does not prove the symmetric spectral theorem, extend SVD to arbitrary dimension, or provide a numerically robust fitting implementation. Those are valid next studies, but the two complete proofs and one exact fit already form a substantial, checkable piece of linear algebra.
