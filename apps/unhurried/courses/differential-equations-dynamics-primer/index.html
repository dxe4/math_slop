Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Powers and logarithms

For a positive integer n, aⁿ means a multiplied by itself n times. For example, 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. For a nonzero base, a⁰=1 and a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. A negative exponent describes a reciprocal.

Worked example. With base 3, the logarithm asks which exponent produces a positive input. Since 3⁴=81, log₃(81)=4. More generally, for a positive base b different from 1, log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with x positive.

34=81log3(81)=4

Multiplying powers with the same base adds their exponents: 2³×2²=2⁵. For positive inputs, this becomes the logarithm rule log₂(uv)=log₂(u)+log₂(v). It is a rule about a product.

Try one. Find log₂(1/8) and explain the negative sign.

Worked answer

Because 2⁻³=1/2³=1/8, the logarithm is −3. For a base larger than 1, inputs between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms.

Watch the operation. Logarithms do not generally turn a sum into a sum: log₂(2+2)=2, while log₂(2)+log₂(2)=2 happens to agree in this case. Using 2+4 instead gives log₂(6), which differs from 1+2=3 because 2³=8.

Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Start with a rate, end with a prediction

This route is for a reader who knows a derivative as a rate and has seen exponentials and basic graphs. Its outcome is modest and useful: turn two simple changing-system rules into checked predictions, read which way a state moves without solving first, and distinguish settling from oscillating in a linear spring. Allow about five hours including the linked lessons, six short model-to-equation attempts, the mixed problem and the capstone. Stop between modules; the estimates are invitations to work through answers, not a speed test.

Entry recap. If y is a state measured at time t, y′ is its change per unit time. A rule y′=f(y) supplies a velocity for each current state. A constant state c is an equilibrium if f(c)=0. The function e−kt, k>0, starts at one and shrinks toward zero; its derivative is −ke−kt. A second derivative x″ measures how velocity changes. These facts are enough to begin, and each lesson repairs the specific notation it uses. This route does not build a general existence theory or solve arbitrary differential equations.

Three ordered modules

Rules and solutions

Read The mathematics of a cooling cup. Before its formula, translate “cools faster when farther above the room” into T′=−k(T−Ta). Notice why the difference from ambient, rather than the absolute temperature, is the state that decays. Check the solution at t=0 and by differentiating it. As a first model-to-equation attempt, change the cup to one initially below room temperature: keep the same rule and predict the sign of T′.

Equilibria

Read Read a differential equation without solving it. The bridge is that the cup's ambient temperature becomes the field's equilibrium level. For y′=1−y, draw a three-row sign table for y<1, y=1 and y>1 before looking at its exact solution. Then use the exact formula to check that the arrows describe the whole forward curve. As a second attempt, mark slopes at y=0,1,2. As a third, explain in one sentence why a coarse Euler step is evidence about a numerical method rather than proof about the equation.

Oscillation

This module joins two contrasting kinds of change. Read Growth that runs out of room first: its population moves toward a capacity without oscillating, under positive initial conditions. For a fourth attempt, write the logistic rate as “population × remaining fraction of room” and locate its two equilibria. Then read Three ways a spring can settle: velocity is now a second state, so a spring can pass through equilibrium while losing energy. For a fifth attempt, decide from c²−4mk whether m=k=1,c=1 oscillates. For a sixth, explain why c=0 gives periodic motion but no decay. The comparison, not a claim that logistic growth itself oscillates, is the purpose of the module.

Make the four lessons talk to each other

Every model chooses a state and a rule. Cooling uses one state T and a fixed target Ta; logistic growth uses population P and a density-dependent rate; the spring needs both displacement x and velocity x′ to determine what happens next. That difference matters when interpreting initial data: T(0) is enough for the first model, P(0) for the second, but a spring also needs x′(0). Write those required starting measurements beside each equation before solving anything.

“Stable” has a precise local meaning: a small displacement from an equilibrium stays small for future time. The cooling formula proves more, since the displacement shrinks by e−kt. The logistic sign chart and formula show positive populations near K return toward K. A spring with positive damping has decreasing mechanical energy, but its position may cross zero several times. A graph that wiggles is therefore not a sign that the equilibrium is repelling.

Mixed practice: two quantities, two meanings

A room is at 18°C. A cup starts at 58°C and follows T′=−0.2(T−18), with time in minutes. At the same time, a scaled population follows P′=0.5P(1−P/100), starting at P=20, with time in days. Find each initial rate, its target equilibrium and the direction of motion. At one cooling time constant, what is the cup temperature? For the population, at what P is the growth rate greatest?

Hint

Use the gap 58−18, then inspect the quadratic P(1−P/100). Keep the time units separate.

Worked answer

The cup has initial rate −0.2·40=−8°C per minute and target 18°C. Its time constant is 1/0.2=5 minutes, so T(5)=18+40/e≈32.7°C. The population has initial rate 0.5·20·0.8=8 individuals per day and target 100; it initially grows. Complete the square or differentiate 0.5P−0.005P²: the rate is greatest at P=50, where it is 12.5 individuals per day. The cup temperature approaches its target from above; the positive population starting below capacity approaches its target from below. No temperature–population unit conversion is meaningful.

Capstone: decide what each model can say

Write a short report about two proposed systems. System A is a 70°C drink in a 20°C room with T′=−0.1(T−20), time in minutes. System B is a population with P′=0.2P(1−P/500), time in years, starting at 100. For each, give the initial rate, equilibrium, long-time model prediction and one assumption that could fail. Explain why a population at 600 behaves differently from one at 100. Then classify a separate spring x″+2x′+x=0 by its discriminant and say what initial measurements it needs. The spring comparison tests whether you choose a state before applying a formula.

Model solution

For A the initial rate is −0.1·50=−5°C per minute, equilibrium 20°C, and T(t)=20+50e−0.1t tends to 20 from above. A room whose temperature changes invalidates this constant-ambient formula. For B the initial rate is 0.2·100·0.8=16 individuals per year. The positive solution starting at 100 rises toward the equilibrium K=500. If P starts at 600, its rate is 0.2·600·(1−1.2)=−24 individuals per year, so it falls toward 500. A fixed capacity assumes resources and conditions stay sufficiently stable; migration or changing resources can break it. For the spring, m=c/2=k=1 gives discriminant c²−4mk=4−4=0, so the characteristic root is repeated and negative: critical damping. The equation needs both x(0) and x′(0) to select a solution. None of these long-time model statements is guaranteed for the physical system after its assumptions fail.
