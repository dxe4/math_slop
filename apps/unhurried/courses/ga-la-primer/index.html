Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Move a grid, then recover a point

This route begins with walks on a coordinate grid and ends with a complete two-step linear transformation. You will add displacements, use the images of two unit arrows to move any point, and solve a pair of equations to recover an input. The destination is modest and concrete: read a real 2×2 matrix as an action on the plane, then distinguish a transformation that can be reversed from one that loses information.

Allow about three and a half hours: roughly one hour for the three linked lessons, another hour for examples and transfer problems, and the rest for the construction and self-check below. The only entry needs are signed numbers, coordinates and simple linear equations. No prior matrix course is assumed. If an arrow begins at a point p and ends at q, its displacement is q−p. A linear map T satisfies T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v) and T(cu)=cT(u). These two rules are enough to reconstruct a map from its unit-arrow images.

Entry check. From (2,−1), moving by (−3,4) reaches (−1,3). If that subtraction is unfamiliar, do it on paper before opening the first lesson. Treat a point as a location and a vector as a change of location; they may share a coordinate pair while answering different questions.

Two modules, with a bridge at each step

Module 1: Vectors and maps

  1. A walk is a vector. Check the exact component rule by starting at a point other than the origin. Its transfer task is to say which part of a route survives when only net displacement is recorded.
  2. A matrix is a rule for moving space. Now every input is a combination of two standard unit vectors. A linear map sends that combination to the same combination of two output arrows. Write its columns from those arrows, then check T(0,0)=(0,0).

The bridge is deliberate: vector addition describes how to build an input arrow from simpler arrows; linearity says the output obeys the same recipe. If T(e₁)=(2,0) and T(e₂)=(1,1), then T(3,−2)=3(2,0)−2(1,1)=(4,−2). The first lesson supplies the combination; the second explains why it carries through a map.

Module 2: Meeting constraints

  1. When two equations meet. Reverse the question: given an output (p,q), which input (x,y) produced it? Each output coordinate is one equation. Elimination decides whether there is one input, a whole line of inputs, or none.

For the sample map T(x,y)=(2x+y,y), recovering output (4,−2) means solving 2x+y=4 and y=−2. This gives x=3, restoring the earlier input. Recovery works here because the two columns point in independent directions. Later routes express this condition with determinant and rank; this primer only asks you to test it by actual equations.

A worked mixed problem

Suppose F(e₁)=(1,1) and F(e₂)=(2,0). Find F(2,−1), then decide whether the output (5,1) is attained. Solution: F(x,y)=(x+2y,x). Therefore F(2,−1)=(0,2). To reach (5,1), solve x+2y=5 and x=1. The second equation gives x=1; the first then gives 2y=4, so y=2. Checking F(1,2)=(5,1) completes the solution. This links vector combinations, a matrix's columns, and a two-equation system.

Try three transfer problems without looking back: (1) With G(e₁)=(0,2) and G(e₂)=(1,1), compute G(−1,3). (2) For H(x,y)=(x+y,x+y), list two distinct inputs with output (2,2). (3) Explain why K(x,y)=(x+1,y) is not linear. Checks: (1) (3,1), because −(0,2)+3(1,1)=(3,1); (2) (2,0) and (0,2) both work; (3) K(0,0)=(1,0), while any linear map sends zero to zero. The second task shows how a map can lose information.

Construct two moves of a grid

Make a shear T(x,y)=(x+y,y), then a horizontal stretch S(x,y)=(2x,y). Apply T first and S second. State the composite rule, its two basis images, and the images of the unit square's four corners. Find the output of (1,2), then recover the input that gives output (6,2). Finally explain in words what happens to a vertical unit arrow and why order matters.

Model solution

Applying T then S gives (S∘T)(x,y)=S(x+y,y)=(2x+2y,y). Its basis images are (2,0) and (2,1), the matrix columns. The corners (0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(0,1) go to (0,0),(2,0),(4,1),(2,1). Thus a square becomes a slanted parallelogram; the vertical unit arrow moves to (2,1), slanting right while retaining a vertical change of one. Input (1,2) goes to (6,2). To recover (6,2), solve 2x+2y=6 and y=2; then x=1. Reversing order gives (T∘S)(x,y)=(2x+y,y), which sends e₂ to (1,1), so the operations do not generally commute.

Rubric. Give one point each for the composite rule from the stated order; two correct basis images; four correctly transformed corners; the forward and reverse calculations with substitution checks; and a written comparison of e₂ under both orders. Five points show the route's bounded goal; a missing order comparison suggests revisiting the map lesson. Alternate coordinate notation is fine if the arithmetic and explanation agree.

A complete first stop

Stop when you can move a point from the two column arrows and solve for a possible input without depending on a picture. You have studied real planar linear maps and two-variable systems, not all matrix theory. If you want the next layer, the determinant measures area and detects collapse, while the accelerated route connects that calculation to rank and eigenlines.
