Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Selected proofs about groups and rings

This route asks you to produce proofs, not just calculate examples. Its named targets are finite cyclic subgroups, Lagrange's theorem, the normal-subgroup criterion for quotient multiplication, classification of ideals of Z, and the prime-modulus field criterion. You will also state and apply first Sylow accurately while leaving its proof outside this course. Plan about fifteen hours across several sessions: eight for close reading and rewriting arguments, four for exercises and counterexamples, and three for the proof dossier and capstone.

Entry recap. A group has associative operation, identity and inverses. A left coset aH is {ah:h∈H}. A normal subgroup N satisfies gNg−1=N for every g. An ideal of a commutative ring is an additive subgroup stable under multiplication by any ring element. A quotient must have operations independent of the representative chosen for each class. If that phrase is unfamiliar, start with the modular-clock and ideal examples before tackling the general proof. This is a selected proof course; it does not confer a degree-level command of all abstract algebra.

Module 1: Finite group structure

  1. How long until a symmetry returns?. Rewrite the finite-return argument without looking: repetition gives gi=gj, then cancellation gives a positive return. Show the first m powers are distinct and form ⟨g⟩.
  2. Why subgroup sizes divide. Treat disjoint-or-equal cosets as the key lemma. Prove equal size with an explicit bijection, then count blocks. Record the one-way direction of the theorem.
  3. A map that respects the operation. Show kernels are subgroups and that equal outputs differ by a kernel element. This prepares the idea that a map's fibers are cosets.
  4. Collapse a normal subgroup. Prove representative independence for (aN)(bN)=abN. Then prove normality is necessary if this product rule is supposed to work on all left cosets. Use S₃/H with H={e,(12)} as the counterexample.
  5. A theorem larger than its picture. State the finite-group factorization |G|=pnm with p∤m and the guaranteed subgroup order pn. Work a group-order consequence, but do not present a list of examples as a proof of Sylow.

The module's logical spine is clear: cyclic powers give a specific subgroup; cosets count any subgroup; kernels relate a homomorphism to cosets; normality licenses multiplication on cosets. First Sylow goes in a different direction by forcing certain subgroups to exist. Lagrange restricts possible orders, so it cannot replace first Sylow's existence result. Before moving on, prove that an index-two subgroup is normal: the two left cosets are H and its complement, and the same holds for right cosets, so gH=Hg for every g.

Module 2: Ring structure

  1. Polynomials are numbers with a variable. Use coefficient arrays to prove distributivity and inspect the difference between formal polynomials and polynomial functions over F₂. A few evaluations never establish formal equality there.
  2. Remainders as quotient rings. Prove the classification of ideals of Z, treating {0} separately. Then check multiplication of quotient classes with a′=a+i and b′=b+j; the difference a′b′−ab lies in the ideal.
  3. Why division sometimes fails modulo n. Prove that gcd(a,n)=1 exactly when [a] is a unit, using Bézout in one direction and a linear combination in the other. Deduce the prime-modulus field criterion and name the n=1 exclusion.

The bridge from groups to rings is subtle. A normal subgroup makes coset multiplication of a group well-defined. An ideal makes both addition and multiplication of ring cosets well-defined. An additive subgroup of a ring need not be an ideal: Z is an additive subgroup of Q, but (1/2)·1 is not in Z. By contrast 2Z is an ideal of Z because multiplication by any integer keeps even integers even. Keep the operation and the needed closure condition visible in each proof.

Worked proof practice: two kinds of collapse

First collapse the additive group Z by 6Z. If a′=a+6u and b′=b+6v, then a′+b′−(a+b)=6(u+v), so addition of classes is independent of choices. Since Z is abelian, 6Z is normal and this is a group quotient. To retain multiplication as well, compute a′b′−ab=6(av+bu+6uv), again a multiple of six. This extra computation uses the ideal property and produces the quotient ring Z/(6). Both constructions have the same six underlying cosets but make different promises: the group quotient talks only about addition; the ring quotient also talks about products.

Now inspect a general group quotient. If a′=an₁ and b′=bn₂ with n₁,n₂∈N, then a′b′=ab(b−1n₁b)n₂. The middle conjugate must belong to N; this is exactly where normality enters. Without that condition, the class of a′b′ can depend on representatives. Do not replace this algebraic step with a diagram of blocks. The computation proves the universal rule, and S₃ supplies a failed case.

Proof dossier: two quotients, two operations

Construct (1) the quotient group S₃/A₃ and (2) the quotient ring Z/(6). For each, list its classes, state which relation is being collapsed, and prove the advertised operations are independent of representatives. Compute one product of nonidentity classes in S₃/A₃ and one nonzero zero-divisor product in Z/(6). Explain why the second quotient is not a field. Finish with a separate paragraph that states a correct Sylow consequence for a group of order 18 and labels the proof boundary.

Hint

A₃={e,(123),(132)} is the even-permutation subgroup. Its two cosets are even and odd permutations. The subgroup has index two, hence is normal. For Z/(6), use differences in 6Z and multiplication expansion.

Model solution

A₃ has three elements and is an index-two subgroup of S₃, which has six elements. The cosets are E=A₃={e,(123),(132)} and O=(12)A₃={(12),(13),(23)}. The collapsed relation identifies two permutations when their quotient lies in A₃, equivalently when they have the same parity. Index two makes A₃ normal: any element outside A₃ has both left and right coset equal to the complement O. If a′=an₁ and b′=bn₂ with n₁,n₂∈A₃, then a′b′=ab(b−1n₁b)n₂. Normality places the final two factors in A₃, so a′b′A₃=abA₃. This proves the quotient product is independent of representatives. E is identity and O·O=E because the product of two odd permutations is even; specifically (12)(13)=(132)∈A₃. The quotient group therefore has the two-element multiplication table.

For the ring, 6Z={6k:k∈Z} is an ideal: sums and additive inverses remain multiples of six, and an arbitrary integer times 6k is 6 times an integer. The six classes are [0],[1],[2],[3],[4],[5]. The collapsed relation is a≡b when 6 divides a−b. If a′=a+6u and b′=b+6v, then a′+b′−(a+b)=6(u+v), while a′b′−ab=6(av+bu+6uv). Both differences lie in 6Z, so both class operations are well-defined. The nonzero classes [2] and [3] multiply to [6]=[0]. They are zero divisors, so the ring cannot be a field: if [2] had an inverse, multiplying [2][3]=[0] by it would force [3]=[0]. This ring has six elements, while S₃/A₃ has two; the two quotient constructions also preserve different operations.

For |G|=18=3²·2, first Sylow guarantees a subgroup of order 9. It also guarantees a subgroup of order 2 because 18=2·9. These are theorem applications, not proofs of first Sylow. The theorem's general proof is deferred; the calculations here prove only the quotient and field claims explicitly written above.

Rubric. A complete dossier lists exactly two and six classes, identifies the two collapsed relations, proves representative independence with normality and ideal absorption in the right places, computes O²=E and [2][3]=[0], and gives the field contradiction. The Sylow paragraph must name p, maximal p-power and finite-group hypothesis while accurately separating theorem use from proof. A correct example without the two independence proofs is partial work; a generic claim that “quotients are well-defined” omits the main task.
