Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Recover the working language

This selected core is an eight-and-a-half-hour route for readers comfortable with functions and short proofs. You will verify a functor and a natural transformation, then prove a Set product and pullback universal property. The work is not a survey of arbitrary limits: every construction is explicit, and the capstone asks for both a finite calculation and a general mediator argument. Before starting, refresh the two proof moves used repeatedly here: equality of functions by an arbitrary input, and uniqueness by showing every candidate has forced coordinates.

Write arrows with their types while reading. For f:X→Z and g:Y→Z, the pullback projections leave the matching-pair object, while a compatible cone from T yields a mediator entering it. Reversing that direction changes the problem. The course's calculated examples are small enough to enumerate; the proofs are not based on enumeration and apply to general sets.

Module 1: Mappings between categories

  1. Relationships before ingredients supplies the category axioms using literal paths. Use its identity and associativity proof as your baseline, then ask which equations a functor must preserve. Do not mistake a graph's edge list for all arrows of its path category.
  2. A structure-preserving translation moves paths into Set. Recompute the composite image table without looking at the answer. The bridge to the next lesson is that two such translations can themselves be compared at each object.
  3. A translation between translations checks the naturality square on a generating edge and explains extension to paths. Write both sides as maps F(A)→G(B) before comparing outputs. Functoriality lets a component family commute with composites once it commutes with the generating edges in a free path category.

As a mixed check, let the source have a:A→B. Set F(A)={0,1}, F(B)={u,v}, F(a)(0)=u, F(a)(1)=v. Set G(A)={p,q}, G(B)={red,blue}, G(a)(p)=red, G(a)(q)=blue. Define ηA(0)=p, ηA(1)=q and ηB(u)=red, ηB(v)=blue. Both routes at 0 give red, and at 1 give blue; identities commute automatically. Thus η is natural. This same arbitrary-input style will appear in the Set product proof, though its unique mediator is a different kind of object.

Module 2: Universal objects

  1. Pairing as a universal property proves that maps f:T→X and g:T→Y factor uniquely through X×Y. After reading, reproduce the uniqueness proof from the two projection equations. This gives the independent pairing step used to construct a pullback.
  2. A pullback matches shared data restricts X×Y to pairs satisfying f(x)=g(y). The compatibility equation is the extra condition that makes a cone land in this subset. Prove both that the matching set's own square commutes and that every compatible cone has exactly one mediator. The database analogy is optional; the Set proof is the assessed result.

One useful bridge is to compare the mediators: a product accepts any two arrows from T, whereas a pullback accepts only pairs of arrows whose composites into Z agree. The mediator has the same pointwise form t↦(u(t),v(t)) in both cases, but for a pullback you must first show that this pair belongs to the matching subset. Omitting that membership check is a proof gap.

Worked mixed practice

Take X={a,b}, Y={r,s}, Z={0,1} with f(a)=0, f(b)=1, g(r)=0 and g(s)=1. The product has four pairs; the pullback P has only (a,r) and (b,s). Let T={t0,t1} with u(t0)=a, u(t1)=b and v(t0)=r, v(t1)=s. The functions f∘u and g∘v both have table (0,1), so the cone is compatible. The mediator is m(t0)=(a,r), m(t1)=(b,s). The projections recover u and v exactly. If v(t1) were changed to r, compatibility would fail at t1; a mediator with those projections could not exist.

Capstone: a pullback with a proof

Task. Let X={a,b,c}, Y={p,q}, Z={red,blue}, with f(a)=f(c)=red, f(b)=blue, g(p)=red and g(q)=blue. Construct P, state its projection maps, verify the square and prove the universal property for any compatible u:T→X and v:T→Y. Give one incompatible cone.

Model solution. The matching set is P={(a,p),(c,p),(b,q)}. Define pX(x,y)=x and pY(x,y)=y. For each displayed pair, its two key values are the same, so f∘pX=g∘pY:P→Z. Now let T be any set and let u:T→X, v:T→Y satisfy f∘u=g∘v. For every t, (u(t),v(t)) lies in P by this equality. Hence m(t)=(u(t),v(t)) defines a function T→P with pX∘m=u and pY∘m=v. If n:T→P has those same two equations, n(t)'s first coordinate is u(t) and its second is v(t). Equality of ordered pairs gives n(t)=m(t) for every t, so n=m. As an incompatible cone, take T={∗}, u(∗)=a and v(∗)=q; red≠blue, so no mediator with those projections exists.

Rubric. Award two points for exactly the three matching pairs and correct projection types, two for the commuting square, two for well-defined mediator existence, two for pointwise uniqueness and two for an explicit incompatible cone. A list of pairs without an arbitrary-T argument does not establish the universal property.
