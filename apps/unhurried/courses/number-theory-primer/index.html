Make a repeating rota predictable

This primer studies three questions about integers: when one count fits exactly into another, how to find the greatest size that fits two counts, and when a repeated step around a clock returns to its start. You need multiplication, division with remainder, and comfort with positive and negative whole numbers. Each linked lesson repairs its own notation. The result is a usable small toolkit for finite repeating schedules, not a claim to cover number theory as a whole.

Allow roughly seven hours including reading, a handwritten remainder ledger, the mixed practice, and the final rota design. The pages themselves are shorter; the time estimate leaves room to pause over the proofs and check examples without a calculator. You can stop after any module. The satisfying end point is an exact prediction for a rota you choose and a reason that covers every turn, not just its first few entries.

Quick entry recap

A whole-number quotient is an integer. If 24 objects go into six identical boxes with none left over, 24=6·4. If 26 objects do not fit, division gives 26=6·4+2. The 2 is a remainder. On a clock with m positions numbered 0 through m−1, totals differing by a multiple of m land at the same position. Negative totals are allowed: −1 lands at position m−1. Keep these concrete readings beside the symbols as you work.

Module 1: Divides and remainders

  1. Divisibility has a witness. Read with a pencil: whenever a divides b, exhibit the integer k in b=ak. The theorem about a∣b and b∣c works because the two witnesses multiply. The counterexamples show why a familiar sounding converse may fail.
  2. Euclid's remainder ledger. This turns the search for a common divisor into a finite process. Track not just the numbers but the invariant: replacing (a,b) by (b,r) preserves every common divisor when a=qb+r. The last positive remainder is therefore the largest common divisor.

The bridge between these lessons is the difference a−qb. A number that divides both a and b also divides that difference; a number that divides b and the difference also divides a. You do not need to remember a tiling picture once you can reconstruct those two implications. For a practical checkpoint, compute gcd(40,28): 40=1·28+12, 28=2·12+4, 12=3·4+0, so the gcd is 4. It fits both original counts: 40=10·4 and 28=7·4.

Module 2: Clock arithmetic

  1. When does a clock step return? The existing lesson studies repeated addition and proves that a step s on an n-position clock first returns after n/gcd(n,s) moves. After t moves the unwrapped total is ts, so the return condition is n∣ts. The variable t is the unknown number of moves.

The bridge from gcd to a rota is direct. The Euclidean algorithm finds d=gcd(n,s), while the clock theorem converts that one number into a return time n/d. The orbit's positions are distinct before the first return, so n/d is also the number of visited positions. A picture or a few observations can test a prediction, but only the divisibility argument establishes it for all positive n and s. You need no modular inverse or prime-number theorem for this route.

Work a mixed example

A circular display has 15 positions. A marker starts at 0 and advances 6 each turn. First, show that 3 is a common divisor of the position count and the step: 15=3·5 and 6=3·2. Then use Euclid to show it is the greatest one: 15=2·6+3, 6=2·3+0. The clock rule predicts 15/3=5 moves before the first return. The visible positions are 0,6,12,3,9,0. The four positions after the start and before the return are new, so the orbit has five distinct positions including 0.

Why does a step of 6 miss ten positions? Every unwrapped total is a multiple of 3, so every landing position is congruent to 0 modulo 3. The positions 0,3,6,9,12 are all five such residues modulo 15. That explains the pattern in a second way. It would be incorrect to divide 15 by 6 and round the result: the return condition is about a multiple of 15, not a comparison of physical lengths. The divisor witness, Euclid ledger, and clock theorem play different roles in the same answer.

For independent mixed practice, change the step to 4. Because 15=3·4+3, 4=1·3+1, and 3=3·1, the gcd is 1 and the first return takes all 15 moves. List only the first four positions, 0,4,8,12, then explain why you do not need to list the remaining eleven to know every position will eventually appear. Check your reason against the distinct-position argument in the clock lesson.

Capstone: design a maintenance rota

A circular control panel has 18 numbered positions, 0 through 17. A technician starts at 0 and services the position reached after each jump of 8. Determine the first return time, list the full orbit, and say which positions never receive service. Then propose a new positive step that visits every position and justify the choice. Include a remainder ledger, a divisibility explanation for the return, and a check that the proposed new step has the property you claim.

Model solution

Euclid gives 18=2·8+2 and 8=4·2+0, so gcd(18,8)=2. The return theorem gives 18/2=9 jumps. Adding 8 modulo 18 produces 0,8,16,6,14,4,12,2,10,0. The nine distinct visited positions are precisely the even positions; 1,3,5,7,9,11,13,15,17 are missed. Each jump preserves even parity, which explains why the odd positions cannot appear. For a new step, choose 5. Euclid gives 18=3·5+3, 5=1·3+2, 3=1·2+1, and 2=2·1+0; hence gcd(18,5)=1. The rule yields 18/1=18 moves, visiting all 18 positions before returning. Equivalently, if 18 divides 5t, coprimality forces 18 to divide t, so no earlier positive t works.

Self-review rubric: a complete capstone has two correct gcd ledgers, the nine-jump prediction, the exact orbit, the missed odd positions, and a coprime replacement step with justification. A correct list without the return argument is useful evidence but not yet the intended explanation. If the number of jumps is off by one, count the move that lands back at 0. If the new step is claimed to visit everything only because it “looks different,” check its gcd with 18.

Stop with one justified rota

You can now go from a divisibility witness to a gcd ledger to a proven cycle length. A step need not be smaller than the clock: reducing it modulo the position count preserves the route. If you want a second route, the accelerated course asks when multiplication, rather than addition, can be reversed modulo a number. The arithmetic developed here already stands as a complete finite result.
