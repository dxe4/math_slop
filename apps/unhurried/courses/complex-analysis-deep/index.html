Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

A selected proof route through complex analysis

This route develops a restricted but complete chain from coordinate differentiation to a disk version of Cauchy's formula, then uses that formula for a derivative bound. It assumes real-line limits, Green's theorem for a disk or annulus, parametrized line integrals, uniform convergence on compact sets, and comfort writing proofs. Plan roughly ten focused hours: about four for the eight linked articles, four for the connective proof and twelve checkpoints, and two for revision and the capstone. It is a selected proof study, not a credential or a survey of Riemann mapping and analytic continuation.

Entry diagnostic. If f=u+iv and f has a complex derivative, compute the real-axis and imaginary-axis quotient limits. They are ux+ivx and vy−iuy; equality yields the Cauchy–Riemann equations. Also parametrize |z|=r counterclockwise and recover ∮dz/z=2πi. If either calculation needs repair, do it in the first two modules before using Green's theorem. Our proof below assumes continuous first partials on a neighborhood of the closed disk. General Cauchy–Goursat removes that extra regularity, but its proof is beyond this route; none of our restricted conclusions should be silently advertised as the full theorem.

Module links and proof bridges

1. Local structure

Read A derivative must agree from every direction and Two equations behind rigid behavior. The first proves a full limit for z² and disproves one for conjugation. The second translates a complex derivative into coordinate equalities and explains why pointwise equalities without real total differentiability cannot be reversed. Write the linear approximation f(z+h)=f(z)+f′(z)h+o(|h|) before using the equations. This makes the next bridge legitimate: Green's theorem applies to real C¹ coordinate functions, and the Cauchy–Riemann equations cancel its area terms.

2. Cauchy theory on disks

Read Why the logarithm needs a cut, A loop that counts turns, and The boundary knows every interior value. The cut and loop show why a puncture cannot be ignored. The reconstruction article states the general disk formula. Here is a proof of a restricted version using a named real-variable theorem, with every regularity assumption displayed.

Restricted Cauchy theorem. Suppose f=u+iv is holomorphic on an open neighborhood of a closed disk and u,v have continuous first partial derivatives there. Write dz=dx+i dy. Then f dz=(u dx−v dy)+i(v dx+u dy). By Green's theorem, its counterclockwise boundary integral is the area integral of (−vx−uy)+i(ux−vy). Both components vanish by Cauchy–Riemann. Thus ∮f dz=0. The same computation applies to a disk with a small closed disk removed; the inner boundary is clockwise in the positive orientation of that annulus.

Restricted Cauchy formula. Fix an interior a and put g(z)=f(z)/(z−a). On the annulus outside |z−a|=ε, g has continuous first partial derivatives and is holomorphic, so its outer boundary integral equals the counterclockwise inner-circle integral. Split g=f(a)/(z−a)+[f(z)−f(a)]/(z−a). The first inner integral is 2πi f(a). The second has modulus at most 2π sup|z−a|=ε|f(z)−f(a)|, which tends to zero by continuity. Let ε shrink to obtain ∮f(z)/(z−a)dz=2πi f(a). This proof uses Green's theorem and C¹ regularity. It does not prove the holomorphic-only Cauchy–Goursat theorem.

3. Consequences and singularities

Read A real integral solved by a complex detour, Why the biggest value lives on the edge, and A distant singularity limits a local series. The residue theorem extends a boundary integral to isolated interior poles; the worked real integral needs an arc estimate in addition. The maximum principle uses Cauchy's circle average. The geometric-series calculations give exact radii for rational functions. To move from our restricted formula to a derivative estimate, differentiate the kernel with respect to a on a smaller disk; the contour stays a positive distance from a, so the difference quotients converge uniformly along it. The result is f′(a)=(1/2πi)∮f(z)/(z−a)²dz under our neighborhood assumptions.

Twelve proof checkpoints

Worked mixed practice. Take F(z)=1/(2−z). On the closed unit disk, find the maximum boundary modulus, compute F′(0), compare it with Cauchy's derivative bound, and find the exact radius of the Taylor series at zero. Explain why all these conclusions can coexist with a pole elsewhere.

Hint for mixed practice

On |z|=1, use |2−z|≥2−|z|. Rewrite F as a geometric series in z/2.

Worked mixed solution

For |z|=1, |2−z|≥1, with equality at z=1. Thus |F(z)|≤1 and the boundary maximum is exactly M=1. Direct differentiation gives F′(z)=1/(2−z)², so F′(0)=1/4. Cauchy's unit-disk estimate gives |F′(0)|≤M/1=1, and 1/4 satisfies it. Also F(z)=(1/2)∑n≥0(z/2)n for |z|<2; the exact radius is 2 because terms cease to tend to zero on or outside |z|=2. The only pole is z=2, outside the unit disk but on the boundary of this series's larger convergence disk. The same function can therefore obey a unit-disk bound while its Taylor series extends farther.

Write each argument before opening its key. These are short proofs or repairs, not a claim that the whole subject has been covered. Four concern local structure, four the disk proof, and four consequences.

  1. Prove from the definition that f(z)=z³ has derivative 3z².
    Key

    Expand (z+h)³; after dividing by h the error is 3zh+h², whose modulus tends to zero.

  2. Disprove complex differentiability of z̄ at any point.
    Key

    The quotient is h̄/h, equal to 1 on real h and −1 on imaginary h.

  3. Derive both Cauchy–Riemann equations from axis steps.
    Key

    Equate ux+ivx with vy−iuy.

  4. Repair the claim “Cauchy–Riemann at a point implies a derivative.”
    Key

    Add real total differentiability there; continuous first partials in a neighborhood are a sufficient condition for it.

  5. Show ∮|z|=rdz/z=2πi for r>0.
    Key

    Use z=reit, dz/z=i dt, 0≤t≤2π.

  6. Prove the restricted zero-integral statement for a disk.
    Key

    Apply Green's theorem to the two real components of f dz; Cauchy–Riemann makes both curls zero.

  7. Bound the small-circle remainder in the restricted Cauchy formula.
    Key

    Its absolute integral is at most 2π sup|z−a|=ε|f(z)−f(a)|→0.

  8. Explain why Green's theorem cannot be applied to f(z)=1/z on the full disk centered at zero.
    Key

    It is undefined at zero; its coordinate partials are not continuous on that disk.

  9. Show that the upper semicircle integral of 1/(z²+1) tends to zero.
    Key

    For R>1, bound it by πR/(R²−1), which tends to zero.

  10. Prove a mean-value inequality |f(a)|≤max|z−a|=ρ|f(z)| under Cauchy's formula.
    Key

    Parameterize the circle so f(a) is its average, then apply the triangle inequality to the average.

  11. Find the exact radius of ∑n≥0(z/2)n.
    Key

    The finite geometric identity gives convergence for |z|<2; terms fail to tend to zero for |z|≥2.

  12. Repair “the principal Log obeys Log(zw)=Log z+Log w for all inputs.”
    Key

    Require z,w,zw in the slit domain and their chosen angles to add without crossing the interval (−π,π); otherwise the sides can differ by 2πi or one side is undefined.

A successful proof states what theorem is being used. In particular, Checkpoint 6 depends on Green's theorem, and Checkpoint 10 depends on Cauchy's formula. If a step uses a larger theorem than this restricted route proved, name it. That practice is more informative than presenting a correct numerical answer with a hidden hypothesis.

Capstone: a disk estimate and a missing hypothesis

Let f be holomorphic on an open neighborhood of |z|≤1, with |f(z)|≤M on |z|=1. Prove |f′(0)|≤M from Cauchy's formula. Then construct a function with the same boundary bound and a derivative at zero that violates this estimate when holomorphy throughout the disk is removed. State precisely which hypothesis fails. Finally, explain how the restricted Green's-theorem route above justifies the formula if f additionally has continuous real first partials near the closed disk.

Model solution

Cauchy's derivative formula gives f′(0)=(1/2πi)∮|z|=1f(z)/z² dz. On the unit circle |z²|=1 and the path length is 2π, so the contour length estimate yields |f′(0)|≤(1/2π)(2πM)=M. For a counterexample take g(z)=1/(z−1/2). On |z|=1, |z−1/2|≥1/2, so |g(z)|≤2; thus M=2 is a valid boundary bound. Yet g′(0)=−1/(−1/2)²=−4 and |g′(0)|=4>2. The pole at 1/2 lies inside the disk, so g is not holomorphic throughout it. For a C¹ holomorphic f near the closed disk, Green's theorem gives the zero integral on punctured annuli; shrinking the inner circle proves Cauchy's formula as above. Uniform separation of z on the boundary from a near zero permits differentiating that formula, giving the derivative formula used in the first sentence. The counterexample shows why boundary control alone cannot authorize that chain.

Rubric. A complete capstone derives the derivative formula or names the justified Cauchy consequence; computes the bound with the circle length and denominator modulus; verifies the counterexample's boundary bound and derivative; locates the interior pole; and states where C¹ regularity was used in our restricted proof. A numerical value alone does not meet the proof outcome. You can stop with a valid restricted disk theory and a clear map of its dependencies. General Cauchy–Goursat, arbitrary domains, Riemann mapping and analytic continuation remain separate studies.
