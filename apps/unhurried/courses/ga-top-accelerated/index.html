Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

Make each hypothesis earn its place

This route refreshes selected point-set topology for a reader who knows sets, functions, metric balls and short contradiction proofs. Plan roughly eight hours: two for close reading, three for article exercises, and three for the mixed task and capstone. The task is to prove specific statements and diagnose when a tempting converse fails. It does not attempt to classify all spaces or develop a full general-topology course.

Entry recap. A metric ball B(x,r) contains points within distance r of x. An open set gives every included point some ball that stays inside. For a map f:X→Y, f−1(V) is the set of inputs that land in V, whether or not f has an inverse function. A subspace A⊆X uses intersections A∩U with ambient open U as its open sets. An equivalence relation groups points into classes for a quotient. Write these meanings at the top of your scratch page; the route will use each one in a different role.

Three catalog modules, seven lessons

  1. Spaces and maps. Start with open sets and write the quantifiers in their given order. In continuity, prove the forward and reverse directions for metric spaces, checking that the preimage is open in the domain's own topology. In homeomorphisms, test both f and f−1; a continuous bijection alone is too weak. The connection is that neighborhoods give a local test for a map, while a homeomorphism makes that test reversible.
  2. Global properties. Work through connectedness and then compactness. For the first, pull back an alleged separation. For the second, start with an arbitrary open cover and push the finite-covered endpoint to a supremum. These proofs have different inputs: the connected-image theorem uses continuity and open preimages, while interval compactness additionally uses completeness of ℝ.
  3. Identifications. In quotient topology, look at the full saturated preimage of a set of classes. Finish with the existing Euler count, which treats a finite connected plane drawing and explicitly includes the outside face. The cylinder seam is a quotient example; the plane-graph count is a finite invariant example. Neither licenses an unproved surface-classification claim.

At each transition, ask a precise question: Does the assertion concern points, maps, covers, decompositions, identifications or drawings? For example, the cylinder quotient is built from a continuous map q, but counting its square's edges before identification would count the wrong object. Conversely, an Euler value alone will not tell you whether a map has a continuous inverse.

Worked mixed problem: one map, two properties

Let f:[0,1]→ℝ be f(t)=2t−1. Show it is continuous using the metric condition, identify its image, and say why the image is connected and compact. Then consider the quotient that identifies 0 with 1 in [0,1]. Does f define a map on those quotient classes?

For s,t∈[0,1], |f(s)−f(t)|=2|s−t|, so δ=ε/2 proves continuity at every t. The image is [−1,1], since f is increasing and sends the endpoints to −1 and 1. The domain [0,1] is connected and compact. The connected-image proof carries connectedness to [−1,1]; pulling an open cover of the image back through f and taking a finite subcover carries compactness. These facts can also be checked directly because [−1,1] is a closed interval. The quotient question has a different answer: f(0)=−1 while f(1)=1, so f assigns different outputs to equivalent points. It is not well-defined on the quotient. To define a map from equivalence classes, first require equal outputs on each class; continuity comes only after well-definedness.

The exercise mixes local continuity, two global preservation statements and a quotient well-definedness check. A tempting mistake is to say that the quotient map is continuous so every formula on the interval descends. Continuity cannot repair a formula that disagrees on identified endpoints.

Compare a circle with a closed interval

Write a proof that S¹ and [0,1] are not homeomorphic. Define a cut point, show an interior point of [0,1] is one, show no circle point is one, and explain why a homeomorphism would preserve the property. Justify the connectedness facts you invoke. Add one counterexample to the false converse “a continuous map with connected image has connected domain,” and explain why Euler counting is not the chosen test here.

Capstone hint

Remove 1/2 from the interval. Parametrize the circle without a chosen point by an open real interval, after rotating that point to (1,0). For the counterexample, a constant function is useful.

Model proof

A cut point p of a connected space X makes X∖{p} disconnected. The interval [0,1] is connected by the standard least-upper-bound argument. Removing 1/2 leaves [0,1/2) and (1/2,1], disjoint nonempty sets open relative to the punctured interval and covering it, so 1/2 is a cut point. Rotate any chosen point p∈S¹ to (1,0). Rotation is a homeomorphism. The map t↦(cos t,sin t) sends (0,2π) continuously onto S¹∖{(1,0)}. An interval is connected; by the connected-image theorem, that punctured circle is connected. Thus no p on S¹ is a cut point.

If h:[0,1]→S¹ were a homeomorphism, its restriction from [0,1]∖{1/2} to S¹∖{h(1/2)} would be a homeomorphism. Open separations are carried across a homeomorphism, so both punctured spaces would have the same connectedness status. They do not; contradiction. The false converse fails for a constant continuous map from [0,1]∪[2,3] to one point: its image is connected while its domain is separated. Euler's plane-graph theorem concerns a specified finite drawing and face convention; no drawing or triangulation of these two spaces was given, and the cut-point property already supplies the needed distinction.

Self-review rubric. A complete dossier has two full short proofs you can reproduce without prose gaps: the connected-image theorem and the interval compactness argument. Its capstone gives a separation after deleting an interior interval point, a reason every punctured circle stays connected, and a statement of preservation under homeomorphism. Give credit for naming the least-upper-bound property as the interval theorem's input. An example alone, or a claim that a picture makes the circle “one piece,” is incomplete.

Where to stop

Stop when you can distinguish continuity, homeomorphism, compactness and connectedness by their quantifiers and can finish the cut-point proof unaided. The next route studies loops with a clearly marked covering-space lemma. If the capstone stalls, return to the connected-image proof rather than memorizing that an interval and circle are different; the proof method transfers to new spaces.
