Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

What survives when a point set forgets an equation?

This proof-based route is a selected window into affine algebraic geometry, with a projective intersection audit at the end. Allow about sixteen hours: seven articles, their exercises, the proofs and the capstone below. You should already be comfortable with polynomial rings, ideals, quotient rings, complex numbers, projective coordinates and reading proofs. The main independent proof target is a concrete coordinate-ring isomorphism and the prime-ideal calculation for two small quotient rings. The Nullstellensatz and general Bézout are stated with exact conditions and tested in examples; their general proofs and scheme sheaves are outside this route.

Entry recap. An ideal I of a polynomial ring is closed under sums and multiplication by arbitrary polynomials. In A/I, elements differing by something in I become equal. The zero set Vk(I) contains coordinate tuples over k on which every element of I vanishes. Its vanishing ideal I(S) contains all polynomials zero on the set S. A prime ideal p is proper and satisfies ab∈p ⇒ a∈p or b∈p. The radical √I consists of f whose positive power lies in I. A nilpotent is an element whose positive power is zero. Keep these definitions distinct throughout the route.

Module 1: algebra of curves

  1. Draw the solutions of a polynomial. Start with exact membership tests. The real empty-locus example warns that the field of coordinates is part of every geometric claim.
  2. Tangents from derivatives of equations. Use the gradient at a smooth zero, then inspect the squared-equation failure case. A vanishing gradient of a repeated equation need not mean the underlying point set has a cusp.
  3. Turn geometry into a ring. Prove the kernel of substitution y↦x² is (y−x²), giving ℝ[x,y]/(y−x²)≅ℝ[x]. Reproduce the factorization, not just the final isomorphism.

The module moves from points to local behavior to functions constrained by an equation. On the parabola, y and x² evaluate equally at every point; the quotient makes them the same class. The proof of the isomorphism also tells you why one matching value at (0,0) is insufficient to equate two functions: equality in the quotient requires a polynomial identity modulo the whole ideal. This is a useful threshold between testing examples and proving a ring statement.

Proof checkpoint. For P(x,y)=y³+xy, subtract P(x,x²)=x⁶+x³. The identity y³−x⁶=(y−x²)(y²+x²y+x⁴), while xy−x³=x(y−x²). Hence P−P(x,x²) is a multiple of y−x². Explain why the same termwise argument works for every polynomial; that is the kernel proof in miniature.

Module 2: beyond visible points

  1. Which polynomial sets share the same points? Over an algebraically closed field, I(Vk(I))=√I. Prove the easy containment yourself, then state clearly that the reverse containment uses the Nullstellensatz.
  2. Why a ring can describe more than visible points. Compute the unique prime of k[x]/(x²), and identify its nonzero nilpotent. The underlying spectrum is one point; the affine scheme retains the ring through its structure sheaf.
  3. Where parallel lines meet. Compute intersections in homogeneous coordinates. This is a deliberate change of setting from affine rings to projective points, useful for counting all intersections.
  4. Why intersection counting needs hypotheses. Distinguish a proved line–conic restriction calculation from the stated general degree-product theorem. List algebraic closure, projectivity, no shared component and multiplicity before applying it.

The bridge from coordinate rings to radicals is a controlled loss of information. Passing from (x²) to its classical zero set forgets the repeated factor; passing to √(x²)=(x) formalizes what the points can still recover over ℂ. Spec of k[x]/(x²) has one prime because every prime contains the nilpotent class of x, yet the ring itself retains that class. Projective completion repairs a different information loss: an affine chart omits points with Z=0. These are distinct mechanisms, and neither alone proves a general theorem about curves.

Theorem-scope checkpoint. The Nullstellensatz statement belongs to ideals in k[x1,…,xn] with k algebraically closed and n finite. Over ℝ, (x²+1) has no real zero and defeats the same formula with ordinary radicals. Bézout belongs to two complex, or more generally algebraically closed-field, projective plane curves of degrees m and n with no common component, and counts local intersection multiplicity. A real affine picture can omit complex or infinite points; a distinct-point count can omit tangency multiplicity. Neither theorem follows from one computed example.

Worked mixed practice: point sets, rings and counts

Compare J=(x²) and K=(x) in ℂ[x]. Their classical zero sets both equal {0}. Their radicals both equal (x), because a polynomial has a positive power divisible by x² exactly when it is divisible by x. Their quotients differ: in ℂ[x]/(x), the class of x is zero; in ℂ[x]/(x²), ε=[x] is nonzero and ε²=0. Every prime of the latter contains ε, and its quotient by (ε) is ℂ, so (ε) is the sole prime. Thus the two spectra have the same one-point topology but different global ring arithmetic.

Now compare the line y=1 with the conic x²+y²=1 over ℂ. Their one visible affine point (0,1) is a tangent meeting. The line–conic restriction yields x²=0, a repeated root of order two. Over projective coordinates Y−Z=0 and X²+Y²−Z²=0, setting Z=0 forces X=Y=0, so no projective point lies at infinity. The total multiplicity is two. This is independent of the nilpotent quotient example above, though both teach that a bare list of distinct points can lose algebraic information. One should not identify intersection multiplicity with arbitrary quotient nilpotence without the local definitions.

For an additional proof check, show that (x) is prime in B=ℂ[x]/(x²) only after translating notation carefully: the ideal is really generated by the class ε, and quotienting B by it yields ℂ. The zero ideal in B itself is not prime since ε·ε=0 while ε≠0. This avoids silently treating ideals of the polynomial ring and ideals of its quotient as the same objects.

Capstone: two one-point affine examples

Let k=ℂ, A=k[x]/(x) and B=k[x]/(x²). Compare their classical k-points, their complete ideal lists, their prime spectra and Zariski topologies, and their nilpotents. Show that the rings are not isomorphic. Relate the comparison to I(V(I))=√I in k[x], stating what the theorem supplies and what you have calculated directly. Finish with one sentence explaining why this capstone does not construct a general scheme.

Hint

Use A≅k. Write every element of B as a+bε with ε²=0. An element with a≠0 is a unit: solve (a+bε)(c+dε)=1. Nonunits all lie in (ε).

Model solution

Both defining ideals vanish at the sole classical coordinate x=0, so both classical point sets are {0}. A≅k, whose ideals are (0) and A. Division by x² leaves a unique remainder a+bx, so every element of B has the unique form a+bε with ε=[x]. If a≠0, its inverse is a−1−ba−2ε, since ε²=0. An ideal containing such an element is all of B. Every proper ideal therefore lies in (ε); because (ε) is one-dimensional as a k-vector space, its only subideals are (0) and (ε). Thus B has exactly three ideals: (0), (ε), B.

The only prime of A is (0), since A is a field. In B, (0) is not prime because ε²=0 with ε≠0, while (ε) is prime because B/(ε)≅k. Thus each Spec has one point; its only open sets are ∅ and the whole space. A has exactly one nilpotent, 0. In B, every bε is nilpotent since (bε)²=0, and an element a+bε with a≠0 is a unit, so cannot be nilpotent in a nonzero ring. Hence the full nilpotent set of B is {bε:b∈k}. A ring isomorphism preserves nilpotents, so A and B are not isomorphic. In k[x], √(x²)=(x), and both ideals have V={0}, illustrating I(V((x²)))=(x). That equality is also an instance of the Nullstellensatz over algebraically closed k; here we checked it directly in one variable. The exercise computes rings and their prime spaces, but it does not construct structure sheaves or prove general scheme results.

Where this route ends

Stop when you can reproduce the parabola quotient proof and the capstone without relying on the articles' solutions. You have then gained concrete control over a few foundational examples and the hypotheses of two major theorems. Full Nullstellensatz, general intersection theory, structure sheaves, and the many kinds of schemes remain beyond this selected route; the cited specialist references show the sustained mathematics needed there.
