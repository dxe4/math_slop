Carry curve calculations onto a surface

This route is for a reader who remembers multivariable calculus but wants to reconnect its derivatives to geometry. It first measures and frames regular curves, then uses two tangent directions to measure routes on a sphere. Allow about nine hours, including all six lessons, written proofs, a mixed practice calculation and the capstone. The result is working fluency with selected curve and surface calculations, not a general theory of manifolds or a guarantee that every geodesic is globally shortest.

Entry recap. For a vector function γ(t), differentiate coordinate by coordinate. The norm √(v·v) is speed, and integrating speed gives path length. For a map X(u,v) into space, Xᵤ and Xᵥ are the two coordinate tangent vectors. The chain rule turns a coordinate route (u(t),v(t)) into spatial velocity Xᵤu′+Xᵥv′. A cross product Xᵤ×Xᵥ is nonzero exactly when those directions are independent. These facts let you check a patch before trusting its metric.

Keep two kinds of statements apart while working: a direct calculation from a given parametrization, and a general theorem that needs hypotheses. The route proves some local formulas and verifies great circles as examples. It only sketches the wider variational theory of geodesics.

Curve toolkit: speed becomes turning

  1. Arc length rebuilds distance from speed. Recalculate the quarter-circle example with your own radius and compare it with its chord. This is the quantity a surface metric must eventually reproduce for every route in space.
  2. Plane curvature asks how tangent direction changes per unit of that length. Check why the denominator is speed cubed and why zero speed blocks the quotient. Length alone cannot tell a tight circle from a straight segment of equal length.
  3. The moving frame turns signed curvature into T′=κN and N′=−κT for unit-speed plane curves. Prove the first equation by differentiating T·T=1, then the second by rotating. These equations complete the plane-curve toolkit before the surface coordinates begin.

Surface toolkit: two tangent directions

  1. Regular patches introduces X(u,v) and the rank check Xᵤ×Xᵥ≠0. The sphere's latitude-longitude grid works away from poles; at a pole, a coordinate derivative vanishes although the sphere stays smooth. Carry that domain restriction forward.
  2. The first fundamental form takes dot products of those same derivative vectors. The bridge is direct: independence makes a valid tangent basis; E,F,G measure lengths in that basis. Derive the expression from ||Xᵤu′+Xᵥv′||² before substituting memorized sphere coefficients.
  3. Geodesics asks which paths are locally straight according to that surface geometry. Verify a great circle by showing its unit-speed ambient acceleration points normal to the sphere. A path can pass this local test and still have a shorter competitor between distant endpoints.

For each module, write down the interval or chart where the calculation is valid. In the first three, check nonzero velocity when a unit tangent or curvature is used. In the last three, check the regular patch domain and whether a claim concerns local stationarity or global distance. This habit is part of the outcome, not bookkeeping.

Mixed practice: a route that spirals around a cylinder

Take the cylinder X(u,v)=(3cos u,3sin u,v) and coordinate path c(t)=(t,2t), 0≤t≤π. Compute the patch's metric and the route's length. Xᵤ=(-3sin u,3cos u,0), Xᵥ=(0,0,1), and their cross product has norm 3, so the patch is regular on any suitable open angular strip. Dot products give E=9, F=0, G=1. Since u′=1,v′=2, squared speed is 9+4=13 and the route's length is π√13.

The same answer comes directly from γ(t)=X(t,2t)=(3cos t,3sin t,2t): γ′=(-3sin t,3cos t,2) has norm √13. This double calculation is a useful audit of the metric formula. The path is not a plane curve, so the plane Frenet pair from the earlier module should not be copied onto it unchanged. It also should not be declared a global shortest path from the length calculation alone.

Capstone: two routes on a radius-two sphere

Use X(θ,λ)=(2sinθ cosλ,2sinθ sinλ,2cosθ). Start at A with (θ,λ)=(π/3,0) and finish at B with (π/3,π/2). Route P follows the latitude θ=π/3. Route Q goes from A along a meridian to the north pole and then along longitude π/2 to B. Compute the metric away from poles and the length of each route. Say which of these two is shorter, why the pole needs special coordinate handling, and whether your comparison proves a globally shortest path.

Hint

The sphere metric is 4dθ²+4sin²θ dλ². Split Q into two meridian pieces.

Model solution

Derivative dot products give E=4,F=0,G=4sin²θ for 0<θ<π. Along P, θ is fixed at π/3, so speed per unit longitude is 2sin(π/3)=√3 and L(P)=√3·π/2. Along either meridian piece of Q, longitude is fixed and speed per unit θ is 2; each moves through π/3 radians, so each length is 2π/3. Thus L(Q)=4π/3, which is greater than L(P)=π√3/2.

At the pole all longitudes label the same point and Xλ=0. The latitude-longitude map fails the rank test there; Q's two segments can still meet at that genuine sphere point, and each segment length is computed by approaching it from a regular chart or directly in space. Q has a corner in its tangent direction at the pole for these longitudes, so it is not a smooth geodesic through the pole. We compared only two candidates. A shorter great-circle route may exist; the calculation does not prove P is globally minimal.

Rubric. Check the two derivative vectors and rank domain; all three metric coefficients; both route lengths with proper interval factors; an explicit numerical or symbolic comparison; and a statement separating the coordinate singularity from the physical surface. A fully correct answer also limits its conclusion to the two routes given.

Where to stop

Stop when you can derive the sphere metric, compute both route lengths and explain why a chart failure at a pole does not make the sphere singular. That is a substantial foundation for local differential geometry. For a proof-oriented continuation, study Gaussian curvature; it asks what surface bending can be extracted from local derivatives, while later global results require further theorems.
