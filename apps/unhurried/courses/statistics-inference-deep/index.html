Enter the route

This sustained route uses all ten statistics and inference articles, including the existing Simpson’s paradox lesson. It begins with fractions, graph reading, elementary probability and the ability to manipulate a finite sum. The local lesson capsules restore the essential notation. The route is deep in the specific sense of proving elementary identities under named assumptions and auditing a complete toy analysis. It is not a credential in statistics, and it does not cover the wider theory of estimation, generalized linear models or causal identification. Plan roughly seventeen hours for lessons, proof reconstruction, numerical checks and a written capstone.

Keep a three-column notebook headed “identity,” “sampling-model claim,” and “interpretation.” Put histogram count conservation and least-squares residual equations in the first column. Put Bernoulli standard errors, coverage and exact null tails in the second, each with assumptions. Put claims about who the result concerns and what it might cause in the third. This discipline prevents a valid algebraic identity from being mistaken for a valid population or causal conclusion. Report observed effect sizes alongside uncertainty or p-values.

Modules and bridges

Foundations

Foundations: the frame article makes the target explicit. The histogram article demonstrates that displays can change without changing records; the mean and median article demonstrates that summaries can change when a single record changes. Reconstruct both calculations from raw values. The bridge is that a summary is a function of data, so different data or different selection can change it.

Mechanisms and examples

Mechanisms and examples: sampling variation derives Var(p-hat)=p(1−p)/n from independent zero-one trials. Coverage then treats an interval as a function of random data, while the null-tail calculation treats the test statistic under a specified null. Work through the logical order: design, statistic, sampling distribution, procedure, observed result. Do not replace a repeated-sampling probability by a posterior statement about a realized interval.

Limits and deeper questions

Limits and deeper questions: multiple testing adds a reporting-selection mechanism, correlation and residuals test the adequacy of a one-number or one-line summary, and Simpson’s paradox shows how unequal subgroup weights change an aggregate. Reproduce the independent-uniform familywise formula and then name why discrete or dependent p-values do not satisfy its equality. These lessons are complementary limits on interpretation, not competing universal corrections.

Worked mixed practice

Proof practice: Let X_1,...,X_n be independent Bernoulli(p) indicators and p-hat their mean. Since Var(X_i)=p(1−p) and cross-covariances vanish, Var(p-hat)=n p(1−p)/n²=p(1−p)/n. For p=0.4 and n=100 the standard error is sqrt(0.24/100)≈0.0490. This is a spread across repeated samples under that model. Now take m=20 independent exact continuous Uniform null p-values. The event that their minimum exceeds 0.05 requires all twenty to exceed 0.05, with probability 0.95^20; therefore the chance of at least one p≤0.05 is about 0.6415. If those p-values are discrete, equality generally fails; if dependent, multiplication fails. Under valid p-values, the union bound still gives at most mα for a per-test threshold α.

Capstone and model solution

Capstone audit. Draft an invented study of a puzzle club with a target of all 100 members but an initial frame of 40 Saturday attendees. Of those attendees, 30 prefer route A; among the 60 absent members, 12 prefer A. Separately, a fair spinner is tested with ten independent prespecified spins and eight blue results. Build a written report that gives the target and frame rates, derives an iid Bernoulli standard error at p=0.4 for n=100, states an approximate 95% interval construction and its coverage interpretation, calculates exact Wald coverage at n=10 and p=0.5, computes the exact upper-tail p-value for the spinner, and explains what twenty independent exploratory tests would do to a 5% per-test error rate. Add the Simpson puzzle counts from that lesson as a constructed confounding example, and use the residual sequence 2,−1,−2,−1,2 to critique a line. State which facts are descriptive and which require a sampling model.

Show a complete model solution

Model solution: the Saturday rate is 30/40=75%, while the full-club count is 30+12=42 and the rate 42/100=42%; the first cannot stand for the second without a selection assumption. For iid Bernoulli draws, averaging independent indicators yields SE=sqrt(p(1−p)/n)=sqrt(0.24/100)≈0.0490 at p=0.4. An illustrative Wald interval uses p-hat±1.96 sqrt(p-hat(1−p-hat)/n); its roughly 95% coverage is a procedure-level approximation under the iid model, not a probability for fixed observed endpoints, and it can fail near boundaries. For an exact coverage check of this Wald rule when n=10 and p=0.5, enumerate the binomial counts X=0,...,10. The interval contains 0.5 only for X=3,4,5,6,7. Their binomial weights are 120,210,252,210,120 out of 1024 equally likely sequences, so the true coverage is 912/1024=0.890625, below the nominal 0.95. The observed spinner blue rate is 8/10=0.8, an effect of 0.8−0.5=0.30 above the fair null. The fair-spin upper tail is [C(10,8)+C(10,9)+C(10,10)]/2^10=56/1024≈0.0547. For twenty independent exact uniform null p-values, the chance of a minimum at most 0.05 is 1−0.95^20≈0.6415; this exact equality does not apply to discrete binomial p-values. In the Simpson table A leads 90% to 80% on easy boards and 40% to 30% on hard boards, yet trails 45% to 75% pooled because A received mostly hard boards. The residual sequence is U-shaped, so a line misses curvature even though least-squares residuals with an intercept sum to zero. None of these calculations proves that a route causes success.

How to check your work

Outcome to practice: variance derivation checks the sampling mechanism; the interval and spinner tasks check two distinct inferential probabilities; the twenty-test calculation checks selection; the frame, Simpson and residual tasks check interpretation limits. The capstone is intentionally broad within these ten lessons, but each result is a finite calculation or a bounded model statement.

Rubric, twenty points: three for target, frame and denominators; three for deriving the variance and standard error with independence visible; three for interval formula and procedure coverage; three for exact spinner tail and effect-size separation; three for multiplicity assumptions and discrete qualification; three for correct Simpson arithmetic; two for residual interpretation and causal restraint. A satisfying stopping point is a coherent audit in which another reader can reproduce every number and see exactly where the mathematical claim stops.
