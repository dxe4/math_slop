Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

From first-step equations to time-window models

This route connects seven lessons in about eight hours of reading, algebra and a solved investigation. It is for a reader comfortable with fractions and conditional probability who wants to calculate more than a few sample paths. You will solve a finite hitting problem, iterate a transition matrix, distinguish a stationary row from convergence, and derive a waiting probability from a Poisson zero count. Queueing and branching are outside this route's stopping point.

Entry recap. For disjoint alternatives, add their probabilities; along independent steps, multiply them. A conditional probability P(A|B) is defined when P(B)>0. A row vector (a,b) represents current state chances, and multiplication by a transition matrix weights each possible current state by its next-state row. The exponential e⁻ˣ decreases with positive x. A rate λ measured per hour must meet a time measured in hours. These ideas recur in different forms, and each lesson gives its own local refresher.

Keep a one-page assumption ledger. For each calculation, write the state, time scale, independence claim, and boundary or rate units. Many apparent paradoxes in this subject come from switching one of those silently.

Module 1: Refresh core ideas

  1. A random walk on a tiny map. Work the four-step return-to-zero exercise, then explain why a position is not the same as a path.
  2. How long until the token reaches an edge? Solve the first-step recurrence before reading its answer. The bridge from the free walk is an absorbing boundary: once reached, future path enumeration is irrelevant to “which first?”
  3. Memory lives in the state. Decide whether position alone suffices for the edge question or whether an “already hit” flag is needed for a fixed-time question about prior visits.

For first-hit probabilities the token can be stopped at the edge, making its current state sufficient. The boundary values h₀=0 and hᴺ=1 encode the event, not just a picture. Fairness makes neighboring differences equal; if the move probabilities change, keep the recurrence and revise its solution.

Module 2: Work across examples

  1. A playlist that chooses its next mood. Use row convention consistently and check every total.
  2. What a Markov chain settles into. Solve πP=π, then give a periodic counterexample to the claim that every start converges.
  3. Random arrivals at an imaginary museum. Build the count from λt, and articulate independent increments.
  4. The next chime has no appointment. Use “no arrival by t” to derive survival, then form a conditional survival ratio.

The matrix bridge concerns discrete chimes; the arrival bridge concerns continuous time. A stationary row is an invariant distribution for repeated matrix steps, whereas an exponential wait is a distribution for the first event in a constant-rate Poisson process. The models share probability rules but answer different questions. Do not read “memoryless” in the exponential lesson as “all events are independent”; the next interval is independent of earlier Poisson increments, while the total count across nested intervals is plainly dependent.

Worked cross-topic check

On a fair board with absorbing edges 0 and 5, begin at 2. Separately, take a two-state chain A→(3/4,1/4), B→(1/2,1/2), starting at A. Finally, suppose arrivals follow a Poisson process at rate three per hour. Find the chance of reaching 5 first, the A probability after two steps, and the chance of waiting more than 20 minutes.

Worked answer

The fair hitting formula gives h₂=2/5. For the chain, the first row is (3/4,1/4). On the second step P(A)=(3/4)(3/4)+(1/4)(1/2)=9/16+2/16=11/16; P(B)=5/16. For arrivals, 20 minutes is 1/3 hour, so λt=1 and P(T>20 minutes)=P(N(1/3)=0)=e⁻¹≈0.3679. These answers should not be numerically compared as if they were estimates of one event: they come from three separately stated models.

Now audit the chain: c/4=b/2 implies stationary (2/3,1/3). From A, cₜ₊₁=1/2+cₜ/4, so cₜ−2/3=(1/4)ᵗ(1−2/3). This direct recurrence proves convergence in this example. It is stronger than merely seeing the first two iterates approach 2/3.

Capstone: one walk, one chain, one clock

Construct a fair walk on positions 0 through 7 with absorbing edges and start at 2. Find its chance of reaching 7 first using a recurrence and boundary values. Use the two-state chain A→(2/3,1/3), B→(1/4,3/4); start at A, calculate two steps, solve a stationary row, and justify convergence for this particular chain. For a Poisson arrival process at rate three per hour, find P(N(1/2)=0) and P(T>1/2), explain why they match, and find P(T>2/3 | T>1/2). State the assumptions that make each result valid.

Model solution

For the walk, h₀=0, h₇=1 and hᵢ=(hᵢ₋₁+hᵢ₊₁)/2. Consecutive differences are equal, so hᵢ=i/7 and h₂=2/7. Independent fair steps and absorption at first edge are the assumptions. For the chain, after one step from A the row is (2/3,1/3). After two, its A entry is (2/3)(2/3)+(1/3)(1/4)=4/9+1/12=19/36, so the row is (19/36,17/36). Stationary flow gives c/3=b/4; with c+b=1, π=(3/7,4/7). The A error recurrence cₜ₊₁−3/7=(5/12)(cₜ−3/7) proves convergence from every starting row in this particular chain. It also has positive self-loops and communicates both ways, satisfying finite irreducible aperiodic conditions.

For the clock, λt=(3/hour)(1/2 hour)=3/2, so both P(N(1/2)=0) and P(T>1/2) equal e^(−3/2). They name the same event: no arrival during the first half hour. Conditional survival for another sixth of an hour is e^(−2)/e^(−3/2)=e^(−1/2). Constant rate, independent increments and the Poisson zero-count law justify the clock calculations. A time-varying arrival rate would alter the last result.

Self-review rubric. A complete answer states both walk boundaries, produces h₂=2/7 by the recurrence, normalizes every chain row, obtains (19/36,17/36), checks πP=π, gives a convergence reason beyond the fixed-point equation, and distinguishes the two Poisson questions while computing e^(−3/2) and e^(−1/2). The assumption ledger should mention independence, time homogeneity and rate units. Stop when you can defend these steps without looking at the model solution; the deep route adds queue and branching limits.
