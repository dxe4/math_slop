Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

A first taste of change and accumulation

This route asks two connected questions: how can a changing graph have a rate at one instant, and how can a changing rate build a total? Algebra with fractions and the idea of a graph are enough to start. A slope is rise divided by run; an area can be approached by rectangles. The articles provide the main lessons. Allow about three and a half hours for careful reading, the exercises below, revision, and the final journey. This is a selected first encounter with calculus, not a complete differentiation or integration course.

Before reading, check that you can find the slope from (1,1) to (2,4): it is (4−1)/(2−1)=3. If the numerator and denominator are both small, keep the denominator nonzero while simplifying. That single habit will matter in the derivative lesson.

Three modules, four lessons

Rates and corners

Begin with A slope at an instant. Its calculation of (a+h)² creates the derivative rather than assuming one. Record the secant formula 2a+h and explain why h can approach zero but cannot be set to zero in the original quotient. This module's endpoint is one defensible local-rate calculation. Try a negative a so the rate is signed; a negative velocity means motion in the negative direction, while speed is its absolute value.

Accumulation

Next read A corner that defeats the derivative. The catalog places this lesson here; use it as a hinge. It shows that continuous motion of a graph need not have a single instantaneous slope. Check both positive and negative secants before using rate language. The lesson's x|x| example also shows that an absolute-value symbol does not automatically create a corner. The aim is a reliable test, not recognition by appearance.

The bridge

Read Trap a curved area between rectangles and then Why accumulation undoes differentiation. The first computes a particular accumulated area by squeezing lower and upper sums. The second changes the upper endpoint into a variable and asks how fast that total changes. After each lesson, write one sentence distinguishing a finite rectangle estimate from an exact limit. A continuous input f makes the short new strip approximately f(x)h; the error divided by h tends to zero. That is why the accumulated total has derivative f(x).

The route deliberately stops with continuous inputs and simple areas. It does not teach every integration technique. The four lessons have different jobs: define a rate, expose a failure of that definition, define accumulation, and prove their local connection.

Mixed practice with worked feedback

Check 1: secant. For q(t)=t², find the average change per unit time from t=2 to t=2+h, then its limiting value. Solution: [(2+h)²−4]/h=(4h+h²)/h=4+h for h≠0, so the limit is 4. A finite step h=0.1 has slope 4.1, not exactly 4.

Check 2: corner. At t=0, can |t| have velocity zero because its position is zero? Solution: Position and velocity are different quantities. For h>0 the quotient |h|/h=1; for h<0 it is −1. There is no single two-sided velocity at zero.

Check 3: bounds. On [0,1], split the graph f(t)=t into two equal rectangles. Find lower and upper sums. Solution: Width is 1/2. Lower heights are 0 and 1/2, giving L=1/4. Upper heights are 1/2 and 1, giving U=3/4. The exact area 1/2 sits between them; doubling the number of rectangles shrinks the gap.

Check 4: bridge. If A(x)=∫₀ˣ(1+t²)dt, what is A′(1)? Solution: The input is continuous, so the fundamental theorem gives A′(1)=1+1²=2. Directly A(x)=x+x³/3, whose derivative is 1+x².

Final investigation: a piecewise journey

Task. A traveller's position in metres is s(t)=t² for 0≤t≤1 and s(t)=2t−1 for 1≤t≤2. Give the position at each endpoint, the velocity on each open piece, and the total displacement both by endpoint subtraction and by velocity accumulation. Decide whether velocity exists at t=1. Finally, say whether the total distance equals the displacement.

Model solution. The positions are s(0)=0, s(1)=1 from either formula, and s(2)=3. For 0<t<1, s′(t)=2t. For 1<t<2, s′(t)=2. At the joining time the left derivative of t² is 2 and the right derivative of 2t−1 is also 2. Thus s is differentiable at t=1 with velocity 2; a piecewise definition does not necessarily make a corner. The displacement is s(2)−s(0)=3 metres. Integrating velocity gives ∫₀¹2t dt+∫₁²2dt=1+2=3 metres. Velocity is nonnegative throughout, so total distance is also 3 metres. If a negative-velocity segment were present, distance would instead integrate |s′| and could exceed displacement.

Rubric, 10 points. Award two points for correct endpoint positions and continuity at the join; two for the two interior velocity formulas; two for checking both one-sided derivatives at t=1; two for a correct integral calculation; and two for distinguishing displacement from distance with a reason. A correct numerical answer without the join check earns at most eight. The mathematical stopping point is reached when you can explain each score item without consulting a picture.

What you can now connect

The outcome “read a local rate” is checked by the secant, corner, and journey-velocity tasks. The outcome “read accumulated area” is checked by rectangle bounds and the velocity integral. The outcome “explain the bridge” is checked by A′(1) and the agreement between endpoint displacement and accumulated velocity. If these are secure, this primer has done its job; substitution, optimisation, and broader integration methods are separate next routes.
