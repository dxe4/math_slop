Prove what the optimization certificate really says

This selected proof route studies convexity, quadratic descent, equality multipliers, weak duality, KKT sufficiency, subgradients and Newton's quadratic model. Its destination is an explicit certificate for a convex toy problem followed by an analysis of a stationary point where the same conclusion would be invalid. The work is proof-based, but it is not a general theory of nonlinear programming or a degree credential.

Entry recap and diagnostic. You should already be able to differentiate a multivariable quadratic, solve Hp=b for a positive-definite H, and use eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix. Recall that an infimum need not be attained and that a feasible point obeys every inequality. Before reading, prove or disprove these three claims on paper: (i) f′(x*)=0 implies a global minimum; (ii) if 0<λ<2/L, a fixed gradient step converges for every differentiable function with L-Lipschitz gradient; (iii) any nonnegative multiplier produces a Lagrangian lower bound for a minimization problem with ≤0 constraints. For (i), x³ is stationary at zero without a minimum. For (ii), f(x)=−x² has 2-Lipschitz gradient; with α=1/2 and x₀=1, gradient descent doubles x at every step. Claim (iii) is weak duality after the infimum is taken. If the meaning of “positive definite” or “infimum” is hazy, repair it before proceeding.

Budget about 720 minutes: 210 for seven linked articles, 360 for twelve proof tasks at roughly 30 minutes each, 110 for the capstone and 40 for correction and comparison. The estimate is a work plan, not a measured completion time. Stop once you can reconstruct the proofs of local-to-global, quadratic contraction, weak duality and convex KKT sufficiency, and can reject a false certificate by naming its failed hypothesis.

Read in the catalog order

1. Convex structure

Read convex local-to-global, then quadratic gradient descent. The first proof uses a segment from a supposed local minimum to a better distant point; the second decomposes error into eigen-directions. Keep these distinct: geometry certifies which point is best, while the contraction calculation says whether a particular iteration reaches it. After each, write the assumptions above the conclusion, then find the first line of the proof that would fail without one assumption.

2. Duality and optimality

Read regular equality multipliers and weak duality. The multiplier lesson derives a necessary condition on a smooth constraint curve with nonzero gradient. The dual lesson starts with a pointwise inequality valid for any feasible x and nonnegative λ. Do not confuse their roles: a multiplier equation can generate a candidate, while a dual value can certify a lower bound even when convexity is absent. Strong duality, by contrast, is not guaranteed by this elementary argument.

3. Algorithm guarantees

Read convex KKT sufficiency, subgradients, then Newton's quadratic model. KKT joins a global Lagrangian lower bound to a feasible point through complementarity. Subgradients supply a global supporting inequality even where a derivative fails. Newton minimizes a local quadratic model if its Hessian is positive definite; that model can differ from the true objective far from the current point. This route proves sufficiency of a supplied convex KKT pair and the exact positive-quadratic Newton result. General KKT necessity under Slater's condition and nonlinear Newton convergence are named boundaries, not proved here.

Twelve proof tasks

Use the linked articles as lemmas only after you can restate their assumptions. Tasks 1–4 are about structure: (1) prove convex local-to-global by a segment contradiction; (2) prove strict convexity gives at most one minimizer and give an open-domain nonexistence example; (3) show the square function's chord gap is t(1−t)(x−y)²; (4) prove 0∈∂f(x*) is equivalent to global minimality for the convex subgradient definition. Tasks 5–8 are about algorithms and constraints: (5) derive eₖ₊₁=(I−αQ)eₖ; (6) prove the strict spectral step interval and exhibit a boundary cycle; (7) derive the regular equality multiplier condition from tangent directions in the plane; (8) give a singular-constraint counterexample. Tasks 9–12 are certificates: (9) prove weak duality without using convexity; (10) show a feasible candidate and finite dual value bound its error by their gap; (11) prove convex KKT sufficiency from a stationary global minimum of L; (12) derive Newton's positive-quadratic one-step result and give a negative-curvature failure.

Write a proof of each in four parts: hypotheses, main inequality or identity, conclusion, and one failure mode. The short complete models below show the expected level. For task 4, the definition at g=0 is f(y)≥f(x*) for all y, exactly the global minimum statement; no differentiability is needed. For task 10, weak duality gives g(λ)≤p*≤f₀(x̄) for dual-feasible λ and primal-feasible x̄, hence 0≤f₀(x̄)−p*≤f₀(x̄)−g(λ). The last inequality bounds the candidate's unknown loss by a known gap. If either feasibility check fails, that chain cannot be written.

Complete model for task 6

Let Q be symmetric positive definite with eigenvalues 0<λ₁≤⋯≤λₙ. Expand e₀ in its orthonormal eigenbasis: e₀=Σᵢaᵢvᵢ. The recurrence gives eₖ=Σᵢaᵢ(1−αλᵢ)ᵏvᵢ. If 0<α<2/λₙ, each factor has magnitude below one, so ‖eₖ‖≤ρᵏ‖e₀‖ with ρ=maxᵢ|1−αλᵢ|<1. At α=2/λₙ, starting at x*+vₙ makes the error alternate as (−1)ᵏvₙ. Thus the strict upper bound matters for a guarantee from every start. The proof uses symmetry for an orthonormal basis and positive definiteness for positive eigenvalues.

Complete model for task 11

Assume f₀ and fᵢ are convex differentiable, x* is feasible, λ≥0, λᵢfᵢ(x*)=0 and ∇ₓL(x*,λ)=0. Nonnegative weighting makes L(·,λ) convex. Its supporting inequality says L(y,λ)≥L(x*,λ) for all y. Complementarity changes L(x*,λ) to f₀(x*). For feasible y, all λᵢfᵢ(y)≤0, so L(y,λ)≤f₀(y). Chain the statements to obtain f₀(x*)≤f₀(y). This proves sufficiency. It does not assert that every optimum has a KKT multiplier; that necessity direction requires further hypotheses.

Capstone: certify, then reject an imitation

Part A: minimize f(x,y)=½(x²+y²) over x,y≥0 with x+y≥2. Give a primal optimum and its value, write all constraints in ≤0 form, calculate a dual lower bound through a Lagrangian infimum, and check KKT. State whether strict feasibility exists and whether your proof actually needs a strong-duality theorem. Part B: consider the unconstrained nonconvex objective q(t)=−t². A colleague points to q′(0)=0 and calls t=0 optimal. Write the exact failed inference and exhibit lower nearby values.

Model solution

Write h₁=2−x−y, h₂=−x and h₃=−y. The point (1,1) is feasible and has f=1. Choose dual multipliers (1,0,0). The Lagrangian is ½(x²+y²)+(2−x−y)=½[(x−1)²+(y−1)²]+1, so its infimum over ℝ² is g(1,0,0)=1. Nonnegative multipliers and weak duality give p*≥1; the feasible point gives p*≤1. Hence p*=1 and the gap is zero.

At (1,1), residuals are h₁=0, h₂=−1, h₃=−1. Multipliers are nonnegative and all complementary products vanish. The gradient of the objective is (1,1); the weighted constraint gradient is (−1,−1), giving stationarity. Convexity of f and affine constraints makes the supplied KKT pair sufficient. A strictly feasible point exists, for example (2,2): h₁=−2 and h₂=h₃=−2. Yet our displayed matching bounds already prove optimality; no unproved strong-duality implication was used.

For Part B, q′(t)=−2t, so q′(0)=0. But q(ε)=−ε²<0=q(0) for every nonzero ε. Thus zero is a strict local maximum, and q is unbounded below on ℝ. Stationarity was only necessary for an interior differentiable local minimum, and convex KKT sufficiency cannot be applied because q is concave, not convex. A zero gradient by itself is no global certificate.

Rubric. Award one point each for correct constraint signs and primal feasibility; an infimum calculation over the whole base domain; dual feasibility and a numerical zero gap; all four KKT checks; the direct role of weak duality; and a nonconvex counterexample with actual lower values. Six points indicate a complete certificate and a sound boundary analysis. If the dual value was obtained by plugging in the primal point alone, redo the infimum. If the nonconvex response says only “not convex,” supply explicit nearby values as evidence.

Where the proof route ends

The strongest result established here is a global certificate for a supplied convex candidate, backed by a computed lower bound, and exact convergence or one-step claims for stated positive quadratics. Beyond this stopping point lie separation-based proofs of strong duality, constraint qualifications in general spaces, nonsmooth algorithms and globalized Newton methods. The articles and proofs here prepare those questions without claiming to settle them.
