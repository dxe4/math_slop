Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

A geometric first look at complex functions

This route starts with a number acting as a turn, follows the square map across a plane, and ends by asking why some apparently natural operations cannot be differentiated or given a continuous angle everywhere. It is a selected first look. Its four linked lessons and activities fit about four hours of careful reading and pencil work. Contour theorems are outside this route.

Entry recap. A complex number x+iy is a point (x,y). Its length is √(x²+y²), and a nonzero point also has an angle, defined only up to complete turns. The rule i²=−1 turns distributive multiplication into a coordinate calculation. You need ordinary algebra and the sine/cosine interpretation of a point on a circle. If a formula is unfamiliar, the first article supplies the local definitions; here you can test readiness by calculating (1+i)²=2i and checking |1+i|=√2.

Three modules, three questions

1. Turns and scales

Read Multiplication that turns the plane. Explain two descriptions of (a+bi)(c+di): a coordinate rule and a turn plus stretch. Compute (2+i)i=−1+2i, then say why no angle is assigned to zero. This distinction is the bridge to the next module: if multiplying by z turns a point, what happens when z itself supplies the multiplier?

2. Mapping grids

Read What squaring does to a square grid. Follow the image of z and −z, and use (x+iy)²=x²−y²+2xyi to map points without a picture. A vertical input line becomes a parabola except at the special axis. Sketch three paired points and label the origin as a critical point. The square map is smooth there, yet its first-order scale becomes zero; that observation motivates a more exact definition of derivative.

3. Derivatives and branches

Read A derivative must agree from every direction and Why the logarithm needs a cut. Test z² with an arbitrary small h; then see conjugation fail along two directions. The logarithm problem is different: locally an angle can be chosen, but a single continuous choice cannot survive a complete loop around zero. Both lessons reward asking precisely what domain a claim covers. No contour integral theorem is required to use them.

Worked mixed practice

Let z=1+i. First compute z². Then compare the changes in z² produced by small real and imaginary steps h, and finally locate z² relative to the principal logarithm cut. The answer should link geometry, a difference quotient and a branch domain.

Hint

Use (z+h)²−z²=2zh+h². The principal logarithm domain removes the nonpositive real ray.

Worked solution

z²=2i: modulus changes from √2 to 2 and angle from π/4 to π/2. For every nonzero complex h the quotient is 2z+h=2+2i+h, so both real and imaginary approaches tend to 2+2i. This is stronger than merely observing agreement on those two paths because the exact error has magnitude |h| for all paths. The output 2i lies in the principal Log domain, and Log(2i)=ln 2+iπ/2. Squaring the opposite input −1−i also gives 2i; a logarithm of the output cannot tell which of that pair was used.

For a second quick check, try z=−1+iε and z=−1−iε with ε>0 small. Their principal angles approach π and −π. The jump describes the chosen cut, while both points themselves remain nonzero. Saying “Log is discontinuous everywhere near −1” would be too broad: a different branch can be chosen locally near −1.

Capstone: narrate one map and one branch

Make a small table for the inputs 1+i, −1−i, 1−i, and 0. Record each square, describe which inputs collide, and state where a local angle-preservation argument fails. Then choose the principal branch Log on the plane with the nonpositive real ray removed. Say which nonzero outputs from your table have a principal Log, compute those values, and explain why removing a ray helps without making zero harmless. Include a sentence distinguishing a failure of differentiability from a failure of a global logarithm choice.

Model solution

The squares are 2i, 2i, −2i, and 0. The first two inputs are negatives, so the collision follows from (−z)²=z²; the nonzero output 2i has exactly those two square roots. At zero the derivative of z² is 2z=0, so the nonzero-rotation argument for local angle preservation fails there. Both 2i and −2i lie off the removed ray. Their principal logarithms are ln 2+iπ/2 and ln 2−iπ/2. Zero has no logarithm because ew never equals zero, and zero is excluded from the branch domain. Squaring is differentiable even at zero, while conjugation lacks a complex derivative. The logarithm obstruction instead concerns a continuous single-valued choice around a full loop; it is a global domain problem.
