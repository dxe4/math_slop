Recover a lost number from modular data

This route is for a reader comfortable with algebra who wants to recover proof fluency. It connects two uses of Euclid: remainders calculate a gcd, while reversing those equations gives coefficients that can undo multiplication modulo a chosen number. A proof that primes do not run out supplies a different example of exact reasoning. Repeated squaring then makes large modular powers calculable. The final task is an affine toy cipher, a reversible arithmetic map on 26 residues, not a secure communications system.

Plan roughly ten hours including the six linked lessons, proof reconstruction, mixed practice, and capstone. You can read the lessons independently; this wrapper asks you to join their claims with hypotheses intact. The desired stopping point is a complete encode/decode calculation backed by a Bézout certificate, plus a clear explanation of why a noncoprime multiplier fails. For readiness, recall that gcd(a,b)=1 means no positive integer greater than 1 divides both. A congruence u ≡ v (mod m) means m divides u−v. The linked pages refresh both notions locally.

Module 1: Euclid twice

  1. Euclid's remainder ledger. Reconstruct the invariant in both directions: a common divisor of a and b divides a−qb, and a common divisor of b and a−qb divides a. Note exactly where a decreasing nonnegative remainder proves termination.
  2. A gcd as a signed combination. Keep the quotients from Euclid's ledger. Back-substitution turns the final gcd into ax+by, a certificate you can multiply back out. The coefficient pair need not be unique.
  3. Why the primes never run out. This reviewed pilot uses a contradiction. Distinguish the number one greater than a product of listed primes from a prime divisor of that number. The product-plus-one number can itself be composite.

The bridge between the first two pages is constructive: every remainder is an integer combination of the starting inputs, and the last positive remainder is their gcd. The bridge to the prime proof is methodological. A finite list of examples suggests a pattern, but a proof must address an arbitrary finite list. Euclid's prime argument does that by creating a number not divisible by any listed prime and then using a prime divisor of it. The name Euclid appears in both ideas, yet the arguments solve different problems.

Module 2: Congruences

  1. When does a clock step return? This reviewed pilot concerns repeated addition. Its n/gcd(n,s) rule is a reminder that gcd controls which residues a step reaches, though it is not itself a rule for multiplicative order.
  2. When clock multiplication can be undone. The Bézout equation ax+my=1 becomes ax ≡ 1 (mod m). Conversely, any inverse yields such a linear combination. Practice stating both directions instead of memorising a one-way recipe.
  3. Huge powers from a short binary ledger. Use exponent bits to multiply only selected squares. Rebuild the invariant result·baseremaining ≡ ae before using the procedure as a black box.

The bridge from additive clocks to inverses is a change of operation. Repeatedly adding s creates an orbit; multiplying by a acts on every residue at once. Both involve gcd, but their questions differ. If gcd(a,m)=1, multiplication by a permutes the residues, allowing cancellation. If gcd(a,m)>1, two distinct inputs collide. Repeated squaring composes modular multiplications quickly; it does not create an inverse when one is absent.

Mixed practice: from a certificate to a decoding rule

Suppose a code sends x to y ≡ 7x+3 (mod 20). Find its decoding rule. Euclid gives 20=2·7+6 and 7=1·6+1. Reverse: 1=7−6=7−(20−2·7)=3·7−20. Thus 3 is the inverse of 7 modulo 20. Subtract 3 from y and multiply by 3: x ≡ 3(y−3) (mod 20). Test x=4. Encoding gives y ≡ 31 ≡ 11; decoding gives 3(11−3)=24 ≡ 4. The test catches arithmetic errors, while the Bézout certificate proves every residue is recovered.

Now change the multiplier from 7 to 6. The formula y ≡ 6x+3 (mod 20) is not reversible, because gcd(6,20)=2. Indeed x=0 and x=10 both encode to 3: 6·10+3=63 ≡ 3. This collision is an exact counterexample to a decoder for all 20 symbols. Do not infer from a handful of successfully decoded symbols that the whole map is invertible. The inverse criterion answers the global question.

A proof checkpoint from the other module: if you list primes 2,3,5,7,11,13, their product plus one is 30031=59·509, not prime. Nevertheless none of the six listed primes divides 30031, because each leaves remainder 1. A prime factor of 30031 must be outside the list. This same distinction between a checked instance and a general argument is useful in cipher work: the modular inverse theorem certifies all residues, while a single round-trip certifies only its input.

Capstone: decode an affine toy cipher

Represent A through Z by residues 0 through 25. A toy encoder publishes y ≡ 5x+8 (mod 26). First establish that this is reversible by finding a Bézout certificate for 5 and 26. Encode the letter H, whose index is 7. Decode the resulting number using a formula that works for every residue. Finally explain what goes wrong if the multiplier is 13 instead. Write the inverse condition and one explicit collision; do not make claims about secrecy.

Model solution

Euclid gives 26=5·5+1, so 1=26−5·5. Thus 5·(−5)+26·1=1, meaning −5 ≡ 21 is an inverse of 5 modulo 26. In general, subtract 8 from y, then multiply by 21: x ≡ 21(y−8) (mod 26). For H, x=7 gives y=5·7+8=43 ≡ 17, the index R. Decode: 21(17−8)=189=7·26+7, so the result is 7, H. Every residue decodes because 21·5 ≡ 1 (mod 26); the worked letter is a check rather than the proof of reversibility.

If the multiplier were 13, gcd(13,26)=13, so no inverse exists. The inputs 0 and 2 collide under y ≡ 13x+8: both give y ≡ 8 (mod 26), because 13·2=26. A decoder receiving 8 could not distinguish them. This is a failure of injectivity, not just difficulty finding a formula.

Stop at justified reversibility

You have linked gcd, Bézout coefficients, and modular cancellation to a reversible finite map. You have also used a contradiction proof whose conclusion is about every finite prime list. Neither exercise gives a secure cipher. If you continue to the deep route, the same tools construct simultaneous congruence solutions and prove a carefully scoped toy RSA round-trip.
