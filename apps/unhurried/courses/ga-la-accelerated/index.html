Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Refresh one map from several viewpoints

This route is for someone who has seen algebra and coordinates before and wants working fluency with real 2×2 maps. You will translate between vectors, basis coordinates, equations and matrices; diagnose invertibility through determinant, kernel and rank; and identify genuine eigenlines. It is a selected core, not a survey of all linear algebra. A short route through eight lessons cannot cover general diagonalization, complex vector spaces or numerical algorithms.

Budget about eight hours. The linked lessons account for roughly three hours of focused reading; allow another five for the computations, explanations and capstone. Bring school algebra, coordinates and basic trigonometry. Recall that the matrix [[a,b],[c,d]] sends (x,y) to (ax+by,cx+dy). The column vectors are the images of e₁=(1,0) and e₂=(0,1). A system Ax=b asks whether and how the output b is reached. The determinant ad−bc measures signed area scaling in the plane. Each lesson repairs its own notation, so enter anywhere when one concept needs attention.

Diagnostic warm-up. If A=[[1,2],[0,1]], then A(3,−1)=(1,−1). Its first column is (1,0), second (2,1). If either calculation feels opaque, spend extra time in the first two modules before attempting eigenvectors.

Three modules, three connecting questions

Module 1: Coordinates

  1. A walk is a vector: add displacements and keep points distinct from changes.
  2. Why the dot product measures alignment: decompose a vector into parallel and perpendicular parts, checking that the projected-on direction is nonzero.
  3. Coordinates depend on a basis: place basis vectors in matrix columns and translate coordinate addresses with an inverse.

The bridge is that each numerical description answers a different question. Components depend on a basis; dot products measure Euclidean length and angle; a basis conversion changes the coordinate column while leaving the underlying vector fixed. Check this explicitly for B=((1,1),(1,−1)): the vector (4,2) has B-coordinates (3,1), while its Euclidean squared length remains 20, not 3²+1²=10 because B is not an orthonormal basis.

Module 2: Maps and equations

  1. A matrix is a rule for moving space: reconstruct outputs from column images.
  2. When two equations meet: solve Ax=b using reversible row operations and classify inconsistent or free-variable cases.
  3. What determinant actually counts: compute signed area and distinguish its sign from ordinary area.
  4. Missing dimensions have to go somewhere: find kernel and image bases, then account for the input dimension.

Use one map to connect all four: M(x,y)=(2x+y,0). Its columns are (2,0),(1,0), so the unit square collapses to a segment and det M=0. The equation M(x,y)=(4,0) has infinitely many solutions, all with 2x+y=4. Its kernel is the line spanned by (1,−2); its image is the horizontal line. Rank one plus nullity one equals the input dimension two. The determinant and rank statements are two views of this exact collapse in dimension two.

Module 3: Stable directions

  1. Directions a map does not turn: solve det(A−λI)=0 and then solve for nonzero eigenvectors. Do not assume every real map supplies two real eigenlines.

The bridge from rank is useful: λ=0 is an eigenvalue exactly when the kernel contains a nonzero vector, so a singular map has a zero eigenvalue. That does not mean every eigenvector lies in the kernel; another eigenvalue may describe a surviving direction.

Worked mixed diagnosis

Take N=[[2,1],[0,0]]. For b=(4,0), solve Nx=b; then find the kernel, image, determinant and eigenpairs. The equation is 2x+y=4, with no second restriction. Setting x=t gives y=4−2t, so there are infinitely many inputs. The kernel solves 2x+y=0 and is spanned by (1,−2). The image is spanned by (1,0), since the columns (2,0),(1,0) point along the same line. The determinant is zero; rank and nullity are each one. The characteristic polynomial is det(N−λI)=λ(λ−2), giving λ=0 and λ=2. An eigenvector for zero is (1,−2); one for two is (1,0). Direct multiplication checks both. This single map connects every diagnosis without relying on a drawing.

As a counterexample to “every matrix has a real eigenvector,” inspect the quarter-turn R(x,y)=(−y,x). Its characteristic polynomial is λ²+1, with no real root. It is invertible and preserves area, yet has no real eigenline. That is why determinant alone cannot predict eigenvector existence.

Audit an invertible map and its directions

For A=[[2,1],[0,3]], do the following on paper. Find A(1,2) and solve Ax=(5,6). Compute determinant, kernel, image, rank and nullity. Find both real eigenvalues and an eigenvector for each. Explain whether A is invertible and whether the claim “every invertible real 2×2 map has two real eigenvectors” follows from this example. In a chosen basis B=((1,0),(1,1)), give the B-coordinate column of (1,2). This last item checks that a coordinate address is not the vector itself.

Model solution

A(1,2)=(4,6). Solving Ax=(5,6) gives 3y=6, hence y=2; then 2x+2=5 and x=3/2. The determinant is 2·3−0·1=6, so A is invertible. Its kernel is {0}, with empty basis; its image is all of R², with basis {(2,0),(1,3)}, the two columns of A. Rank two plus nullity zero equals two. The characteristic equation (2−λ)(3−λ)=0 gives eigenvalues 2 and 3. For λ=2, (A−2I)v=0 forces y=0, so (1,0) works. For λ=3, the equation −x+y=0 gives (1,1). Direct checks are A(1,0)=2(1,0) and A(1,1)=3(1,1). In basis B, (1,2)=−1(1,0)+2(1,1), so [(1,2)]B=(−1,2)ᵀ. This example cannot prove a universal claim; the invertible quarter-turn R has determinant one and no real eigenvector.

A useful stopping point

Stop when you can explain one map with columns, equations, area, kernel, image and eigenlines, while keeping their hypotheses distinct. The deep route goes further into proof and data fitting. Passing this route's self-check records practice, not a credential; proof of the general spectral theorem and stable numerical methods lie beyond it.
