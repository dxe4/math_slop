Selected proofs of numerical reliability

This proof route is for readers comfortable with Taylor remainders, elementary matrix norms, orthogonality, integrals, and scalar ODEs. Allow about 1,200 minutes over multiple sessions: nine linked lessons and their eighteen exercises, the twelve derivations below, two reproducible tables, and a capstone proof audit. “Deep” here means sustained work on named results: bisection’s invariant, Newton’s local estimate, a trapezoidal bound, scalar Euler stability, and QR’s least-squares reduction. It does not cover large-scale solvers, numerical PDEs, or every convergence theory.

Entry recap. An error theorem has a target, hypotheses, an error measure, and a conclusion. Differentiability at a point differs from a bounded derivative on an interval. A residual |f(x)| differs from a location error |x−r|. For matrices, orthogonality preserves Euclidean length; full column rank makes the thin QR triangular factor invertible. If these distinctions are unfamiliar, use the relevant background capsules before attempting the proof workshop. Keep exact arithmetic separate from any floating-point model you invoke.

Three proof modules

Module 1: Error models

Read cancellation, conditioning versus algorithm, and bisection. Prove the rationalizing identity on its real domain; derive the local relative condition number from a derivative; then prove the bracket invariant by induction. These are three different kinds of claim. The identity is exact algebra. The condition formula is local asymptotic sensitivity. The bracket is a finite certificate under continuity and reliable signs. Bridge forward by asking when a fast method can supplement, rather than silently replace, the bracket.

Module 2: Convergence and approximation

Read Newton’s method, finite-difference derivatives, and trapezoidal quadrature. Reconstruct Newton’s quadratic local error estimate from Taylor’s theorem and identify the denominator that requires a simple root. For finite differences, write truncation and evaluation error as separate inequalities before balancing them. For quadrature, derive one-panel interpolation error and sum it. The bridge to the last module is that a small local error need not make a repeated process safe; propagation and geometry matter.

Module 3: Dynamical and algebraic stability

Read Runge interpolation, Euler stability, and QR least squares. Reproduce the n=4 interpolation values before generalizing; a finite plot is not a convergence proof. For Euler, derive the geometric update factor and its sharp scalar decay interval. For QR, prove the orthogonal residual decomposition and the condition-number identity. These examples show distinct mechanisms: node geometry, discrete amplification, and algebraic conditioning. No single “stable method” label covers all three.

Twelve derivations to make the route active

Write a short proof or counterexample for each prompt. Use exact fractions where possible, and state when a claim changes from exact arithmetic to finite precision. In the first module: (1) prove the rationalizing identity including x=−1; (2) derive κ for x² at x≠0; (3) derive κ for 1/(x−1) and state where it is undefined; (4) prove the bisection width by induction. In the second: (5) derive Newton’s step from a tangent equation; (6) derive its local quadratic error with an explicit bound away from f′=0; (7) derive the forward-difference M|h|/2 bound; (8) integrate the one-panel trapezoid interpolation bound. In the third: (9) derive the even quadratic interpolant for Runge’s three shared nodes; (10) derive 0<hλ<2 for scalar Euler decay; (11) prove that QR’s perpendicular residual term does not depend on x; (12) prove κ₂(AᵀA)=κ₂(A)² for full-rank A using singular values.

Checks for the twelve derivations

The central results should be x/(√(1+x)+1); κ=2; κ=|x/(x−1)|; width (b₀−a₀)/2ⁿ; x−f/f′; an error factor bounded by max|f″|/(2 min|f′|) times |e|²; M|h|/2; Mh³/12 per panel; 1−25x²/26; 0<hλ<2; ||Rx−Qᵀb||²+||(I−QQᵀ)b||²; and (σmax/σmin)². These are checks, not replacements for the hypotheses and proof steps.

Make two compact tables from the derivations: one with exact and rounded cancellation results for two small x values under a declared toy precision, and one with Euler factors for hλ=1/2, 3/2, 2, and 5/2. Both tables must include the reference quantity and the rule used to produce each entry. A table is reproducible only when another reader can recompute it without guessing arithmetic conventions.

Worked mixed proof practice

Suppose a continuous f satisfies f(1)<0<f(2). After five exact bisections, a colleague reports a midpoint r̂ and a small residual, |f(r̂)|<10⁻⁶. What location error can be certified without a derivative bound? If f is also differentiable on the final bracket with f′(x)≥2 throughout, what extra bound follows from the residual?

Worked solution

The bracket invariant proves at least one root r remains in the final interval. Its width is (2−1)/2⁵=1/32, so its midpoint r̂ is within 1/64 of that enclosed root. This conclusion uses continuity and signs, not the residual. Under the additional derivative lower bound, f is strictly increasing, so the root is unique on the bracket. The mean value theorem gives |f(r̂)−f(r)|=|f′(ξ)||r̂−r|≥2|r̂−r|. Since f(r)=0, the residual gives |r̂−r|<5×10⁻⁷. We may take the smaller of 1/64 and 5×10⁻⁷. Without the derivative hypothesis, the residual alone need not imply that position bound.

This practice crosses three lessons: the bisection certificate, derivative-based sensitivity, and the danger of reading residual as error. It also shows why a theorem can improve an existing certificate rather than overturn it.

Capstone: bound, observation, and their gap

Approximate I=∫₀¹x⁴dx with composite trapezoids for n=2 and n=4. Give exact rule values and actual errors using an independent antiderivative. Prove a C² upper bound for each, compare the observed error ratio with the bound’s h² ratio, and explain why a finite pair of ratios does not prove an asymptotic order for an arbitrary function. Treat samples and arithmetic as exact for the main calculation; then state how uncertain samples would change the certificate.

Model solution

I=[x⁵/5]₀¹=1/5. For n=2, the heights at 0,1/2,1 are 0,1/16,1, so T₂=(1/4)(0+2/16+1)=9/32. The error is 9/32−1/5=13/160≈0.08125. For n=4, interior fourth powers sum to (1+16+81)/256=49/128. Thus T₄=(1/8)(2·49/128+1)=113/512, and T₄−1/5=53/2560≈0.020703125.

Here f″(x)=12x², so M=12 on [0,1]. The trapezoidal theorem gives |E|≤Mh²/12=h²: at n=2 the bound is 1/4, and at n=4 it is 1/16. Actual errors are below their bounds. The actual error ratio E₄/E₂=(53/2560)/(13/160)=53/208≈0.2548, close to the bound ratio (1/16)/(1/4)=1/4. The theorem proves an O(h²) upper bound for this C² function, while two observed points do not establish an asymptotic equality or apply to an arbitrary nonsmooth integrand. If each sampled f value has independent unknown absolute error at most δ, positive trapezoidal weights summing to one add at most δ to each integral estimate. Arithmetic rounding would require another term unless shown negligible.

Rubric. A complete capstone has two reproducible exact trapezoid sums, the independent integral, correct actual errors, a valid maximum-curvature bound, a careful observed-versus-proved comparison, and one explicit statement about sample and arithmetic error. Across the course, each derivation should name its hypotheses. A table without its arithmetic rules or a proof that silently invokes a missing smoothness condition needs revision.

Where this proof route ends

Stop when you can explain why a particular bound applies to a particular computation and can identify one nearby case where it does not. You have established selected one-dimensional and finite-dimensional results, not a general guarantee for arbitrary software or data. Natural continuations include interval arithmetic for sign certification, backward-error analysis of implemented QR, and higher-order ODE methods, each with new assumptions that must be written down.
