Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Refresh the calculation, then audit the certificate

This route is for someone who has already met derivatives, gradients and linear inequalities but wants their hypotheses back in focus. Work a diagnostic first: for f(x)=x³, explain why f′(0)=0 does not give a minimum; for f(x)=x² on x≥1, state the best feasible x; and for a proposed inequality multiplier under the convention h(x)≤0, state its required sign. The answers are, respectively, values below zero just left of zero, x=1, and λ≥0. If any answer is unfamiliar, use the linked local or convex article as a full lesson rather than skimming it.

The bounded outcome is to analyze simple smooth updates and constrained candidates, then produce a valid primal/dual certificate on a convex problem. We do not promise a general nonconvex solver. Module sequence and bridges

1. Geometry and descent

Read local versus global minima and convexity's promise. Write the logical arrows: interior differentiable local optimum implies zero derivative; convex local optimum implies global optimum; strict convexity implies at most one optimum. Stationarity alone does not imply a minimum, and uniqueness does not imply strict convexity. Global minimality does imply local minimality by definition. Test them with x³, |x| and x² on (0,1). This module gives the logical checklist used in every later certificate.

2. Constraints

Read gradient descent on a positive quadratic, equality multipliers, and linear-program vertices, in that order. The first separates a descent direction from a safe finite step. The second shows how a smooth fence changes stationarity when its gradient is nonzero. The third gives a finite global method for bounded polygonal feasibility. For each article, write one line saying exactly which object is optimized and over which set: an unconstrained quadratic, a regular curve, or a polygon.

3. Certificates

Read Lagrangian lower bounds, KKT complementarity, and nonsmooth absolute loss. The bridge from vertices to duality is an inequality: combining resource limits with nonnegative weights can bound every feasible objective. KKT packages such weights with stationarity and complementary slackness. The last lesson shows that a convex support inequality can replace an ordinary derivative at a corner. Keep the sign convention L=f₀+λh for h≤0 visible throughout.

For ten calculations, choose three derivative/candidate classifications, two gradient-descent steps and safe intervals, two constraint multiplier systems, one polygon intersection and two dual-function completions of the square. For three repairs, deliberately inspect a negative multiplier, an infeasible primal candidate and a nonzero multiplier on a slack constraint. Write the failed condition, a corrected candidate or multiplier and the resulting bound. The article solutions give models for each type.

Worked mixed practice: a fence and a lower bound

Minimize f(x)=(x−4)² with x≤2. First solve geometrically, then construct a Lagrangian dual bound with h(x)=x−2≤0. This joins the candidate logic, convexity and KKT modules.

Hint

The closest feasible point to 4 is the right endpoint. For the dual function, minimize (x−4)²+λ(x−2) over all real x.

Worked solution

The feasible point nearest 4 is x*=2, with objective 4. For λ≥0, stationarity of the Lagrangian in x gives 2(x−4)+λ=0, so its infimum occurs at x=4−λ/2. Completing the square or substituting yields g(λ)=2λ−λ²/4. At λ=4, g(4)=8−4=4. Therefore g(4)≤p*≤f(2)=4, and p*=4. KKT checks agree: h(2)=0, λ=4≥0, λh=0 and 2(2−4)+4=0. The convex objective makes this pair globally sufficient. If λ=−4 were used, the claimed dual bound would have no weak-duality guarantee because dual feasibility fails.

A small perturbation clarifies activity: change the fence to x≤5. The unconstrained minimum x=4 becomes feasible, so λ=0 and x=4 satisfy KKT, with objective zero. Keeping λ=4 would violate stationarity at x=4; a formerly active fence need not remain active after the model changes.

Capstone: one constrained convex quadratic

Minimize f(x,y)=(x−3)²+(y−1)² subject to x+y≤2, x≥0, y≥0. Provide a feasible candidate, a valid dual lower bound, the objective gap and all KKT residuals. Explain which constraints matter. Then assess the false claim “the unconstrained stationary point proves the constrained answer.”

Model solution

Set h₁=x+y−2, h₂=−x and h₃=−y, all required ≤0. The unconstrained point (3,1) violates h₁≤0. Geometrically, project it onto the line x+y=2: subtract the same amount from both coordinates, giving (2,0). It is feasible and has objective (2−3)²+(0−1)²=2.

For λ=(2,0,0), the Lagrangian is L=(x−3)²+(y−1)²+2(x+y−2). Complete the squares: (x−2)²+y²+2. Its infimum over ℝ² is 2 at (2,0), hence g(2,0,0)=2. Weak duality gives p*≥2, while feasible (2,0) gives p*≤2, so the gap is zero. Primal residuals are h₁=0, h₂=−2, h₃=0. Dual multipliers are nonnegative. Complementary products are 0,0,0. Stationarity is ∇f(2,0)+(2,2)=(−2,−2)+(2,2)=(0,0). Although h₃ is active, its multiplier is zero; active does not mean a constraint must carry positive weight.

The unconstrained stationary point (3,1) is not a feasible answer because 3+1>2. A stationary equation must be solved in the feasible problem, with boundary conditions. The example is convex and differentiable, so the valid KKT pair is sufficient; the equality of explicit lower and upper bounds already proves global optimality without appealing to the necessity direction of any theorem.

Rubric. Award one credit each for correct constraint signs and primal residuals, a genuinely feasible candidate, a computed dual infimum rather than one Lagrangian value, nonnegative multipliers with all KKT checks, a numerical zero gap, and a clear rejection of the infeasible unconstrained point. A solution with only a stationary system is incomplete. Repair any sign mistake before using weak duality.
