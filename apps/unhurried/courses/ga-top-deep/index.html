Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

A proof dossier from neighborhoods to loops

This sustained route selects two strands of topology. The first proves facts about open sets, connectedness and compactness with hypotheses visible at every step. The second studies based loops and an integer obstruction, reaching the circle fundamental group conditional on the path-and-homotopy lifting lemma. Allow about fifteen hours across reading, reconstruction of proofs, exercise work and the final dossier. It assumes comfort with epsilon–delta continuity, the least-upper-bound property of ℝ, elementary groups and writing proofs. This is a selected proof course, not a degree-level survey or an unqualified proof of every covering-space theorem.

Entry recap. A topological space is a set with a collection of open subsets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections, containing ∅ and the whole set. In a metric space, balls supply those opens. A map is continuous when every open target preimage is open in the source. A based loop is a continuous path beginning and ending at a fixed point; a based homotopy changes loops while holding that point fixed. The least-upper-bound property says every nonempty bounded-above real set has a supremum. Keep the ambient space named: an interval can be open relative to a subspace without being open in ℝ.

Two catalog modules, eight proof stops

  1. Point-set proofs. Begin with open sets; write “for every point, there exists a radius” and construct that radius for an interval. In continuity, reproduce both directions of the metric open-preimage theorem, recording which ball comes from target openness and which comes from source continuity. In connectedness, pull back an alleged separation and check both preimages are nonempty. In compactness, make the finite-covered-endpoint set S and justify every use of its supremum. End with quotient topology, where openness is defined through the full saturated preimage. The proof connection is a repeated move from a target assertion back to a source where a definition is easier to use.
  2. Loop invariants. Read based homotopy and verify the four boundary conditions for the disk contraction. Then compute angle increments in winding number, separating the elementary polygonal construction from the general angle-lifting theorem. Finish with the circle fundamental group; trace where path lifting assigns an integer, homotopy lifting protects it, and interpolation of equal-endpoint lifts proves injectivity. The group law is an endpoint calculation, while existence of lifts is a named external lemma.

After each proof stop, mark statements as “proved here,” “used from an earlier lesson,” or “named external input.” For instance, real completeness is used by the interval compactness proof, but the connected-image theorem itself works in arbitrary topological spaces. Similarly, polygonal winding can be computed edge by edge, but invariance under every continuous homotopy needs lifting. Keeping these boundaries visible is one of the course outcomes.

Worked bridge: a quotient seam and a winding lift

Take Q=[0,1]/(0∼1), where only the two endpoints are paired. Define F:Q→S¹ by F([t])=e2πit. This is well-defined because 0 and 1 have the same image; no other distinct representatives need checking. Its composite with the quotient map q is the continuous formula t↦e2πit. The quotient rule therefore makes F continuous. It is bijective: equal points on the circle have parameters differing by an integer, which within [0,1] means either the same parameter or the identified endpoint pair. The compact-to-Hausdorff theorem then makes F a homeomorphism; this last theorem is an explicit external input, not a consequence of bijectivity alone.

Now let γ(t)=e4πit be a based loop in that circle. The covering map p(u)=e2πiu lets γ lift to u(t)=2t, whose endpoint is 2. Concatenating γ with the reverse once gives endpoints 2+(−2)=0, hence the identity class under the lifting-based classification. The quotient construction explains why a circle can be made by joining interval endpoints; the lift explains why a path may cross that seam several times and still retain an integer record. These are related constructions, but they answer different questions: F compares spaces, while the lift classifies based loops.

Notice a possible false shortcut. Because Q is homeomorphic to S¹, one might declare all loops contractible by lifting them to [0,1], an interval. A lift of γ with winding 2 starts at 0 and ends at 2, so it is not a loop in a single copy of [0,1]. The endpoint difference is exactly the obstruction that shortcut loses.

Proof preparation before the final dossier

Reconstruct three implications without reading the solutions: (i) a continuous image of a connected space is connected; (ii) a continuous image of a compact space is compact; (iii) a based homotopy of circle loops has constant lift endpoint, assuming homotopy lifting. Their surface similarity hides different mechanisms. The first pulls back a pair of relatively open sets; the second pulls back an arbitrary open cover and selects finitely many; the third obtains a continuous integer-valued endpoint function and uses connectedness of the parameter interval.

Checks for the reconstruction

For (i), if A,B separate f(X), then f−1(A) and f−1(B) are nonempty, disjoint, open and exhaustive in X. For (ii), if {Vi} covers f(K), then {f−1(Vi)} covers K; a finite selection maps forward to a cover of f(K). For (iii), choose lifts continuously over the homotopy square. Each final value lies in p−1(1)=ℤ and depends continuously on the homotopy parameter, so it is constant. Here (iii) is conditional on the stated lifting lemma, while (i) and (ii) follow from topology definitions.

Submit a two-part proof dossier

Part A, point set. Prove [0,1] compact using arbitrary open covers and the supremum of finite-covered endpoints. Then use the result to show that f([0,1]) is compact for every continuous f:[0,1]→Y. State where completeness of ℝ enters and where it does not.

Part B, loops. For based loops γ,η:[0,1]→S¹ at 1, assume the path-and-homotopy lifting lemma for p(u)=e2πiu. Prove that their lift endpoints give a homomorphism to ℤ, that every integer occurs, and that equal endpoints yield a based homotopy. Identify exactly which sentence would lack justification without the lemma. Apply your argument to γ(t)=e−6πit and η(t)=e10πit.

Capstone hint

For A, define S={x∈[0,1]:[0,x] has a finite subcover}. For B, lift both loops from 0, concatenate by translating the second lift by an integer, and interpolate real lifts with equal endpoints.

Model solution for Part A

Let 𝒰 be any open cover of [0,1], with its relative topology. Let S be the set of x∈[0,1] for which [0,x] has a finite subcover. Some member contains 0, so 0∈S. Since S⊆[0,1], c=sup S exists by real completeness. Select U∈𝒰 containing c and r>0 with (c−r,c+r)∩[0,1]⊆U. Choose x∈S with x>c−r/2 if c>0; if c=0 take x=0. A finite subfamily covers [0,x], and adding U covers through c, so c∈S. If c<1, choose y with c<y≤1 and y<c+r/2. The same finite family plus U covers [0,y], contradicting c as upper bound. Thus c=1 and 𝒰 has a finite subcover. Completeness entered only to form c.

Now let f:[0,1]→Y be continuous and let {Vi} be an arbitrary open cover of f([0,1]). Their preimages under f are open in [0,1] and cover it. Compactness provides finitely many preimages covering the domain. Their corresponding Vi cover every output, so f([0,1]) is compact. This image argument uses no least-upper-bound property; it uses only continuity and compactness already established.

Model solution for Part B

By path lifting, each based loop γ has a unique lift u starting at 0. Since p(u(1))=1, its endpoint n(γ)=u(1) is an integer. By homotopy lifting, a based homotopy produces continuously varying lift endpoints. A continuous map from the parameter interval to ℤ is constant, so n depends only on the based-homotopy class. If γ lifts from 0 to m and η from 0 to n, the concatenation lift follows γ to m, then follows the η lift translated by m, ending at m+n. Hence n([γ]*[η])=n([γ])+n([η]). The loops t↦e2πikt realize every k∈ℤ. If two lifts u,v share endpoints 0 and k, H(s,t)=e2πi((1−s)u(t)+s v(t)) is a based homotopy, proving injectivity. Existence and homotopy invariance of lifts are the steps unavailable without the lemma; endpoint addition and linear interpolation follow once lifts exist.

For γ(t)=e−6πit, the lift −3t ends at −3. For η(t)=e10πit, the lift 5t ends at 5. Their concatenation represents −3+5=2. The computation is about based classes in S¹; it does not assert a lifting theorem has been proved here.
