Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Numerical methods with an error budget

This is a compact return to selected numerical ideas for a reader who has met derivatives, integrals, matrices and elementary ODEs before. Allow about 650 minutes across several sessions: roughly four hours for the nine linked lessons, three hours for their exercises, two hours for the cross-method checks here, and the remaining time for the capstone and revision. The route does not survey every numerical field or teach programming. Its goal is to make each numerical result carry a relevant error statement.

Entry diagnostic. Before reading, explain in a sentence why a small |f(x)| need not mean x is close to a root. Then derive the trapezoid area h[f(a)+f(b)]/2 and the Euler update y_{n+1}=y_n+hf(t_n,y_n). If any step is hard, use the linked lesson’s background capsule and solve its first exercise. The course assumes comfort with algebraic manipulation, Taylor expansion for some bounds, and matrix dot products for QR. It repairs the local notation but cannot replace a full calculus or linear algebra course.

Three modules with explicit bridges

Module 1: Error and sensitivity

Read finite arithmetic, stable formulas, and conditioning versus algorithm in that order. The first describes a rounding model; the second changes a formula to protect a small result; the third asks whether the exact problem itself amplifies input changes. Make a two-row diagnostic for √(1+x)−1 at small x and 1/(x−1) near x=1. Label which difficulty a better expression can fix and which needs better input data. This distinction travels into every later module.

Module 2: Roots and quadrature

Read bisection, Newton’s method, and finite-difference derivatives. A bracket bounds root location under continuity and reliable signs. Newton can approach a simple root rapidly under local smoothness, but a residual is not a position certificate. A derivative estimated by subtracting nearby function values can itself be damaged by rounding; this matters if a Newton implementation substitutes a finite difference for f′. The quadrature error bound follows in the next module.

Module 3: ODEs and least squares

Read trapezoidal quadrature, Euler stability, and QR least squares. These methods answer different mathematical questions, yet share a discipline: identify an exact target, state a model and a norm of error, then check assumptions. The trapezoidal theorem needs a bound on |f″|. Euler’s decay threshold concerns a repeated numerical update, not exact finite-time accuracy. QR avoids the squaring of a matrix condition number by normal equations, but cannot make nearly dependent data informative. After each lesson, write one sentence naming the error source that its theorem does not cover.

From method choice to an error statement

For an integral, a rule may have discretization error and data-value error. For an ODE, a stable step may still have finite-time discretization error. For a least-squares fit, a tiny residual can coexist with large coefficient sensitivity. None of these statements can be substituted for another. The common workflow is to fix the desired output, define an error measure, prove or bound what can be bounded, and name unbounded sources openly.

Use refinement as a check, not as a proof by itself. If halving a step changes an estimate little, both estimates might share a bias, measurement error, or arithmetic failure. A theorem or independent exact case gives the comparison teeth. The following mixed practice makes that distinction concrete for a quadratic, where the true integral is known.

Worked mixed practice: one integral, several errors

Approximate ∫₀¹x²dx with four equal trapezoids. Report the value, the smooth-function bound, and the consequence if every sampled value is uncertain by at most 10⁻⁴. Then compare the bound with the actual error.

Worked answer

The nodes are 0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1. Their squared heights are 0, 1/16, 1/4, 9/16, 1. The composite rule gives T₄=(1/8)[0+2(1/16+1/4+9/16)+1]=11/32=0.34375. Since f″=2, the discretization error is bounded by (1)(1/4)²(2)/12=1/96≈0.010417. Independently, ∫₀¹x²dx=1/3, and T₄−1/3=1/96, so the bound is exact here. The trapezoid weights are nonnegative and sum to 1, so each sample error ≤10⁻⁴ adds at most 10⁻⁴ to the final value. A conservative total bound is 1/96+10⁻⁴, provided arithmetic rounding beyond these sample errors is negligible or separately bounded.

Now imagine using a finite difference to estimate f″ instead of knowing it exactly. A noisy estimate of 2 is not automatically a valid upper bound on |f″| over the entire interval. This is where the derivative-step lesson connects to quadrature: observed derivatives can inform a model, but a certificate requires a justified global M.

Capstone: a reproducible quadrature error budget

Use f(x)=x² on [0,1] with n=4 and n=8 trapezoids. Provide both numerical estimates, their difference, an exact integral check, a C² bound for each, and a total bound at n=8 if each sampled value has error at most 10⁻⁴. Explain why the refinement difference alone would not certify a general unknown integral.

Model solution

For the quadratic, T_n=1/3+1/(6n²); this follows from summing the trapezoid weights or from the one-panel quadratic error. Thus T₄=11/32=0.34375 and T₈=43/128=0.3359375. Their difference is 1/128=0.0078125. The independent antiderivative check gives I=1/3, so actual errors are 1/96 and 1/384 respectively. Since f″=2, the theorem gives the same bounds: (1)(1/n²)(2)/12=1/(6n²). At n=8, uncertain sample values contribute at most 10⁻⁴ because the weights sum to 1; total error is at most 1/384+10⁻⁴≈0.00270417, apart from any unbudgeted arithmetic error. The refinement difference equals three times the n=8 error in this special quadratic case, but two nearby estimates for a general function could both be wrong in the same direction. Without a smoothness theorem, exact comparator, or other enclosure, their difference is diagnostic evidence, not a certificate.

Rubric. Full credit needs correct node arithmetic, both estimates, exact error and certified C² bounds, a justified sample-error contribution, and a clear statement about the limitation of refinement. If the QR and Euler lessons feel detached from this capstone, rephrase their main results as “target, method, guarantee, missing error source”; this is the transferable outcome of the route.

Stop with an auditable answer

You are ready to stop when you can defend which part of an approximation is bounded and which part remains an assumption. The linked lessons provide selected methods, not a universal numerical toolbox. A useful next project is to implement one method with explicit input validation and compare a known case against its mathematical bound; no package installation or particular language is required for the course as written.
