Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

A path, a clock, and a turning direction

This route studies three questions about a smooth plane path: how far does it travel, does changing its clock change that distance, and how fast does its direction turn per unit distance? You will leave with a complete calculation for circular arcs and a reliable test for when a curvature formula may be used. Allow about four and a half hours, including the lesson exercises, the mixed problem and the capstone. The scope is selected regular plane curves; surfaces and general space curves are outside this route.

Entry recap. A curve is a vector function γ(t)=(x(t),y(t)). Differentiate its coordinates to get velocity γ′=(x′,y′). The length of a vector (a,b) is √(a²+b²). A definite integral adds a changing rate over time. For vectors v=(a,b), w=(c,d), the determinant det(v,w)=ad−bc is positive when w lies to the left of v, negative when it lies to the right. These facts are enough to start. Revisit the chain rule if differentiating γ(φ(t)) feels uncertain.

Keep one drawing or coordinate table beside every formula. A picture helps predict whether a path turns left or right, but the sign and value must come from a calculation with the chosen orientation. The route is complete when you can explain your answer without relying on how curved a sketch happens to look.

Three lessons, one developing question

  1. A curve has a speedometer. Start with the distinction between endpoint displacement and travel. Work the straight-path exercise first, then the doubling-back example. The bridge to the next lesson is that speed depends on the clock: if the same path is walked faster, the integrand changes. What should happen to the integral?
  2. Different clocks, same curve. Follow the chain rule and substitution in the proof, paying special attention to the positive derivative and endpoint conditions. Test the twice-around-circle failure case. The bridge to curvature is that a geometric measurement of turning should also ignore an increasing change of clock.
  3. How sharply does a curve turn? Learn the determinant formula for signed curvature and its regularity requirement. Calculate a circle in both directions. The earlier length integral measures accumulated movement; curvature measures change of tangent direction per unit of that movement. Together they distinguish a slower walker from a gentler bend.

After each lesson, write a one-sentence answer to its central question before opening the practice solutions. For arc length, name the integrand. For a clock change, name both endpoint coverage and orientation. For curvature, name where velocity is nonzero. These checks catch the most common wrong substitutions.

A mixed calculation before the final task

Consider γ(u)=(2cos u,2sin u) for 0≤u≤π/2, then change clock to β(t)=γ(t²) for 0≤t≤√(π/2). Find the length and signed curvature away from the new clock's start. The original curve has speed 2, so L(γ)=∫₀^{π/2}2du=π. The new curve has β′(t)=γ′(t²)2t, hence speed 4t for t>0; integrating gives ∫₀^{√(π/2)}4t dt=2(π/2)=π. This agrees with length invariance, even though the new speed is not constant.

For γ, the circle formula gives κ=1/2 because the traversal is counterclockwise and the radius is 2. At t>0, β is an increasing regular reparameterization locally, so its signed curvature is also 1/2. At t=0, β′=0. Do not substitute there into the curvature quotient: the denominator is zero. A limiting value of 1/2 exists, but the formula under its regularity hypothesis is not defined at that parameter. This one problem uses all three lessons and illustrates why a stopped clock can preserve length while blocking a tangent-based calculation at one instant.

Capstone: two clocks for a quarter circle

Take a circle of radius 4. Path A is α(u)=(4cos u,4sin u), 0≤u≤π/2. Path B is β(t)=(4cos(πt/2),4sin(πt/2)), 0≤t≤1. Show that they traverse the same oriented quarter circle exactly once. Compute each speed, both lengths, and signed curvature. Explain which hypotheses make length invariance valid and give one change of clock that would fail the same-once claim.

Hint

Use φ(t)=πt/2, check φ′ and endpoints, then use det(α′,α″)/||α′||³.

Model solution

φ maps [0,1] increasingly and bijectively to [0,π/2], with derivative π/2>0. Since β=α∘φ, both paths have the same ordered trace once. α′=(-4sin u,4cos u) has speed 4, so L(α)=4(π/2)=2π. By the chain rule β′ has speed 4(π/2)=2π, and L(β)=∫₀¹2πdt=2π. The determinant for α is 16 and its speed cubed is 64, giving κ=1/4; the positive clock change keeps κ=1/4 for β. All lengths use the chosen coordinate unit; curvature has inverse-length units.

A failure example is δ(t)=(4cos(πt),4sin(πt)) on 0≤t≤1. It travels a half circle rather than the assigned quarter circle; its length is 4π. A plot might contain the quarter circle but endpoint coverage is different. Reversing the original interval would still preserve length but change the signed curvature, so orientation belongs in a complete comparison.

Rubric. A complete response earns one check each for the parameter interval and orientation, two correctly integrated lengths, the curvature determinant and denominator, and a failure example whose endpoint coverage is actually different. If your lengths agree but you cannot explain why, return to the chain-rule proof. If the sign is wrong, check whether the path runs counterclockwise.
