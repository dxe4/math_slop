Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Audit the geometry behind a familiar theorem

This eight-hour route is for a reader comfortable with Euclidean triangles, ratios, coordinates and informal limits. Basic logarithms and integration help with the disk distance derivation; if they are new, use the exact distance formula supplied in that lesson and treat its integral as an optional extension. It connects three Euclidean tools to two alternative line models. The target is an assumption audit: when a triangle claim changes, you should be able to name the underlying metric, geodesics and parallel-line fact. You will calculate in specific models and explain why the same school proof does or does not transfer. This is a selected model comparison, not a construction of non-Euclidean geometry from axioms.

Entry recap. A Euclidean triangle's interior angles are formed by ordinary straight segments. Corresponding sides of similar triangles have one common ratio, but this needs a theorem such as AA. A circle's chord joins two boundary points; an inscribed angle intercepts the arc opposite its vertex. A sphere's lines in our comparison are great circles. In the Poincaré disk, hyperbolic lines are boundary-orthogonal arcs or diameters, and distance uses a position-dependent scale. Read each local article capsule even if the vocabulary is familiar: the exact meaning of “line” is the subject of this route.

Keep a three-column notebook headed Euclidean, spherical, hyperbolic. Record each theorem with its hypotheses, not merely its numerical answer. When a diagram suggests a pattern, ask whether it is a proof, a calculation in one instance, or an application of a stated model theorem.

Module 1: a Euclidean toolkit

  1. Where triangle angle sums come from. Reproduce the parallel-through-a-vertex proof without looking. Underline the alternate-interior-angle step, because it is the hinge the other models will challenge.
  2. Why scale preserves shape. Use nested parallel triangles to derive one shared side ratio. Notice how the proof relies on the same parallel-angle machinery and on Euclidean area ratios.
  3. The angle at the circumference. Track the intercepted arc explicitly. Its isosceles-triangle proof uses Euclidean radii and angle arithmetic; a point crossing the chord changes which arc is seen.

The bridge across this module is that a theorem does more than report a pattern. A triangle-angle sum, a similarity ratio, and an inscribed half-angle each have a different set of construction steps. Make a one-line dependency for each: “parallel transfer,” “parallel intercept and area,” or “equal radii and exterior angle.” This will prevent carrying a result to a new surface merely because its picture resembles the old one.

Module 2: changing the model

  1. One parallel axiom, different worlds. Compare the number of disjoint lines through an exterior point. Include the caveat that spherical great circles meet, and that the sphere's incidence model has its own antipodal detail.
  2. Three right angles on a sphere. Verify the octant triangle with coordinate tangent directions. This is a complete counterexample to the claim that all geodesic triangles have Euclidean angle sum.
  3. Straight lines inside a curved disk. Calculate radial distance at two Euclidean radii, then inspect the stated geodesic classification. Be precise: the radial integral is derived locally; the general classification is a model theorem used here.

These readings bridge a logical and a metric change. On the sphere, great circles replace plane lines, and the missing Euclidean parallel stops the angle-transfer proof. In the disk, arcs can be geodesics because distance is weighted by 2/(1−r²). Its boundary is excluded and infinitely distant; a drawn contact at the rim is not a meeting point of hyperbolic lines. Spend time with the numerical distance table rather than judging by screen shape.

Worked mixed comparison

Take an ordinary Euclidean triangle with angles 60°, 60°, 60°. Its sum is 180° because of the parallel-line theorem, and scaling all sides keeps those angles. Now take the spherical octant with vertices on three positive coordinate axes. Its sides are great-circle quarters; the tangent checks give 90° at every corner, so the sum is 270°. Finally consider a curvature −1 hyperbolic geodesic triangle with angles 60°, 45°, 30°. Its sum is 135°, below 180°. The associated area is π/4 by the hyperbolic defect theorem, which belongs to the deep route; this optional calculation is a conditional use of that theorem, not a proof of it.

The three totals are not measurements of the same drawn triangle after bending paper. They come from three different definitions of distance and line. In the Euclidean case the parallel-line proof is complete. The spherical octant has its own full instance proof. For the general hyperbolic angle-sum inequality, the model theorem lies beyond the module's disk distance calculation. A careful comparison preserves those different proof statuses.

Capstone: one theorem across three models

Write a comparison of the claim “triangle angles add to π.” For each model, specify the lines, give one exact example or calculation, and say which step of the Euclidean proof survives or fails. Include the Euclidean AA similarity consequence and explain why it cannot simply be copied to a fixed-radius sphere. Finish by identifying one claim you have used as a model theorem rather than proved in this route.

Planning hint

Organize your comparison by line definition, example, angle total, and proof status. The octant's tangent directions and the disk's radial integral provide stronger evidence than a sketch alone.

Model response

In the Euclidean plane, lines are ordinary straight lines. For any nondegenerate triangle, draw a unique parallel to one side through the opposite vertex; alternate interior angles transfer the other two corners onto a straight angle. Thus α+β+γ=π. The same parallel mechanism gives AA similarity through nested parallel triangles, so corresponding Euclidean sides have one scale factor.

On the radius-R sphere, choose shorter great-circle arcs as sides. The octant N=(0,0,R), A=(R,0,0), B=(0,R,0) has perpendicular tangent directions at all three vertices, hence α=β=γ=π/2 and sum 3π/2. Great circles always intersect, so no parallel through N to the equatorial side exists; the Euclidean proof's transfer step is unavailable. On one fixed-radius sphere, standard convex spherical triangle angles determine side lengths, so Euclidean-style differently sized AA copies do not arise.

In the curvature −1 Poincaré disk, lines are diameters or arcs orthogonal to the boundary, under metric ds=2|dz|/(1−|z|²). A geodesic triangle with angle triple π/3, π/4, π/6 has sum 3π/4; its positive defect is π/4. This angle triple and the general defect statement rely on hyperbolic model results not proved by the disk radial-distance integral. Multiple disk geodesics through an exterior point can avoid a fixed geodesic, so the Euclidean unique-parallel step is unavailable. The disk boundary is not part of the plane.

Where this route can end

Stop when you can explain why the Euclidean 180° proof is valid, why the spherical octant contradicts its universal extension, and why the disk's curved arcs are hyperbolic lines. That is a useful selected-model fluency. The deep route adds lattice-area reasoning and a conditional Gauss–Bonnet application, but it does not supply a full independence proof or a full hyperbolic metric course.
