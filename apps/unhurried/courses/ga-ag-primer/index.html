Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

From an equation to a verified meeting

This selected gateway asks two manageable questions: which real points make a polynomial zero, and which points satisfy two equations at once? Your end product is a complete analysis of a movable horizontal line meeting a unit circle. Allow about three hours for both articles, the calculations below, and the capstone. You need school polynomial algebra and the coordinate plane; the route does not require a prior algebraic-geometry course.

Entry recap. A coordinate pair (a,b) lies on the graph of y=g(x) when b=g(a). A polynomial zero locus asks the same kind of membership question for an equation F(x,y)=0. If a point must lie on two loci, it must pass both tests. Substitution removes one variable when one equation is already solved for it. You will also use the discriminant of a quadratic ax²+bx+c: Δ=b²−4ac. Positive Δ gives two real roots; zero gives one repeated root; negative gives none. The articles explain these facts through examples.

Equations as loci

  1. Draw the solutions of a polynomial. Verify sample points by substitution, then justify the entire locus of y−x². Pay special attention to x²+y²+1: the equation exists even though it has no real solutions.
  2. Two curves, how many meetings? Reuse the point test twice. The line equation y=t lets you replace y in x²+y²=1, so a geometric meeting problem becomes a one-variable quadratic.

The bridge between the lessons is the word and. A point on Vℝ(f) passes one polynomial test; a point on the intersection of Vℝ(f) and Vℝ(g) passes both. If you solve one equation but forget to restore and check the other, you have not yet found intersection points. At this level, we use plots to decide what to test, then exact arithmetic to decide what is true.

Pause task. Before continuing, determine whether (2,4) lies on y=x² and whether it also lies on y=2x. Both equations hold: 4=2² and 4=2·2, so this point is in their intersection. Try (−2,4) next: it passes y=x² but fails y=2x, whose right side is −4. This tiny comparison is the logical move behind the larger line-circle calculation.

Worked mixed practice

Find all real points common to the parabola y=x² and the line y=x+2. Substitution gives x²=x+2, or x²−x−2=0. Factor: (x−2)(x+1)=0. Thus x=2 or x=−1. Restore y from the line: when x=2, y=4; when x=−1, y=1. Both also satisfy y=x², so the answers are (2,4) and (−1,1). Factoring gave at most two possible x values, and each was checked, so the list is complete.

Now alter the line to y=x−1. The new quadratic x²−x+1=0 has Δ=(−1)²−4(1)(1)=−3. It has no real root, hence no real intersection. A sketch may show that the line stays below the parabola, but the discriminant is the exact certificate. Over complex coordinates the quadratic does have roots; that observation is a glimpse ahead, not needed for the primer outcome.

The two calculations connect the articles in both directions. The zero-locus article tells you what “on the curve” means; the intersection article supplies a complete solving method for these particular pairs. If your answers came only from a graph, redo them as substitutions and checks.

Capstone: slide a line across a circle

For each real t, let Lt be y=t and let C be x²+y²=1. Give all real intersection points in terms of t. Classify the cases with two, one and zero distinct real points. Explain the tangent case by a repeated root, and verify one chosen point directly in both equations. State why a finite picture alone cannot settle all t.

Hint

After replacing y by t, the only remaining equation is x²=1−t². Sort cases by the sign of 1−t² and remember that a point includes both coordinates.

Model solution

Every common point has y=t and x²=1−t². If −1<t<1, the right side is positive and the two distinct points are (√(1−t²),t) and (−√(1−t²),t). At t=1 and t=−1 it is zero, giving exactly (0,1) or (0,−1). The polynomial x² has a repeated root x=0 in these cases, matching tangency. If |t|>1, the right side is negative, so no real x exists. For a direct check choose t=0: (1,0) has y=0 and 1²+0²=1. Every solution was obtained by a reversible substitution; a finite picture cannot examine every real t or distinguish a double root reliably.

A satisfying stopping point

Stop after you can translate a curve picture into an exact zero test and solve a line-circle family for all real parameter values. That is a useful gateway, not a survey of algebraic geometry. Curves of higher degree, singular points, projective coordinates and general intersection theorems require further tools. The tangent lesson is a natural next step if you know derivatives.
