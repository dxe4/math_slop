Prove what survives a change of drawing

This sustained route studies three structural questions about finite graphs: what makes a tree sparse, what a crossing-free embedding does to the vertex–edge–face count, and how to recognise the same network after relabelling. A bipartite matching module adds a different kind of structure: alternating paths that improve an assignment. The five linked articles are the main lessons; this wrapper asks you to connect their proofs, practice the limits of each theorem, and finish with a fully checkable pair of drawings.

Plan roughly twelve hours across several sessions, including proof reconstruction and the capstone. You should know simple graphs, paths, connectedness, and finite induction. A graph is finite here unless stated otherwise. A plane embedding means edges meet only at common endpoints, and its outside face counts. The route is a selected proof-based window into graph theory, not a claim to cover the field or certify expertise.

Module 1: Graphs and trees

  1. Every edge has two ends. Reconstruct the double-count proof using the set of vertex–edge incidences. Ask whether connectedness entered the argument.
  2. Why a tree has one fewer edge than vertices. Reconstruct leaf existence from a longest path, then the induction for |E|=|V|−1.

The bridge is a change in what is counted. The handshaking identity counts each edge twice through its endpoints and holds in any finite simple undirected graph. The tree identity counts the one edge needed for each new vertex, but it requires connectedness and acyclicity. Test the hypotheses against a triangle plus one isolated vertex: its edge count equals V−1 even though it is not a tree. This counterexample explains why a naked equation cannot replace a structural definition. For proof practice, write exactly where the longest-path argument uses finiteness and where the induction uses connectedness of the leaf-deleted remainder.

Module 2: Embeddings and matching

  1. A formula for flat networks. Track V,E,F while cycle edges are deleted from a connected plane embedding. Name the plane-separation fact on which the face step depends.
  2. Pairing people and tasks. Identify an augmenting path, flip its alternating edges, and prove the count rises by one.

The first lesson extends the tree count: after deleting cycle edges, a connected plane graph becomes a spanning tree with one face. Restoring each deleted edge increases both E and F by one. The second lesson uses a different kind of alternating structure. A matching may be enlarged by exchanging k assigned edges for k+1 allowed edges along a path. Neither result follows from a drawing alone; a crossing-free embedding and unmatched endpoints are explicit hypotheses. Keep a proof ledger: for the planar formula, record what stays invariant; for augmentation, record what changes and why no vertex receives two partners.

Module 3: Same structure

  1. Same network, different drawing. Produce a vertex bijection and check adjacency in both directions. Use degree, connectedness, and cycle structure as rejection tests, not as automatic certificates of isomorphism.

This lesson lets you ask whether results from the earlier modules transfer to a differently labelled graph. An isomorphism preserves degrees and cycles, so it preserves the property of being a tree. It also preserves matching size by carrying every selected edge to an edge with distinct endpoints. Planarity is preserved as an abstract property because any crossing-free drawing can be relabelled through the bijection, although the particular coordinates of two displayed drawings can differ. The mapping itself is the proof of sameness. Equal degree lists alone cannot supply one.

Worked mixed practice: one invariant, one operation

Take a connected plane graph with vertices U,V,W,X and edges UV,VW,WU,WX. First compute its degrees and face count. Then remove the edge UV and decide whether the remainder is a tree. Finally, reinterpret edges UV, VW, WX as eligibility edges between left set {U,W} and right set {V,X}; use matching {U–V} and ask whether it can be augmented. The same symbols play different roles in the plane and bipartite questions, so state the model each time.

Hint

The triangle encloses one bounded face. Deleting one triangle edge leaves a connected three-leaf star centered at W. For matching, try W–X first and check whether the endpoints are free.

Worked solution

Degrees in the original graph are deg(U)=2, deg(V)=2, deg(W)=3, deg(X)=1; the sum is eight, twice the four edges. In a plane embedding, the triangle interior and the outside give F=2, and 4−4+2=2. Removing UV leaves edges VW,WU,WX. All four vertices remain connected and there is no cycle, so it is a tree with three edges, matching V−1. In the separate bipartite eligibility graph, {U–V} is a matching of size one. W and X are both unmatched and W–X is an allowed edge, so the length-one augmenting path W–X adds that edge, giving {U–V,W–X} of size two. This example uses only a subset of the original edges as eligibility pairs; the triangle edges together could not form a bipartite graph with left {U,W} and right {V,X}, because WU lies within the left set.

Proof checkpoint. Why does an isomorphism send a matching to a matching? Let f be an adjacency-preserving bijection and let M be a matching. Each {u,v} in M maps to edge {f(u),f(v)}. If two mapped edges shared a vertex, injectivity would make their original edges share a vertex too, contradicting that M is a matching. Hence the mapped set is a matching of equal size. Applying the inverse map shows maximum matching sizes agree. This is a full proof under the stated bijection; it does not require the graphs to be bipartite.

Capstone: two drawings of one plane network

Graph G has vertices A,B,C,D,E and edges AB,BC,CD,DA,AC,CE. Draw A–B–C–D as a square, AC as a diagonal inside, and E as a leaf outside at C. Graph H has vertices 1,2,3,4,5 and edges 24,41,13,32,21,15. Determine whether G and H are isomorphic by an explicit map. Explain why G is planar and compute V−E+F for its stated embedding. Build a spanning tree and use it to give a second proof of the face count. State one false converse about an invariant and refute it with a concrete pair of graphs. Finally, write three short proof paragraphs: a leaf-deletion proof of the tree edge formula, an Euler face-count argument for this graph, and an argument that the exhibited map preserves degrees.

Capstone hint

C is the only degree-four vertex in G, and 1 is the only degree-four vertex in H. E and 5 are leaves. Try A→2, B→4, C→1, D→3, E→5. Check all six edges and all four remaining nonedge pairs, or use bijection plus complete edge coverage.

Model solution and proof paragraphs

The map f(A)=2, f(B)=4, f(C)=1, f(D)=3, f(E)=5 is bijective. It sends AB→24, BC→41, CD→13, DA→32, AC→21, and CE→15, precisely the six edges of H. Distinct input pairs have distinct image pairs under a bijection, so these six images exhaust H's six edges and no nonedge of G maps to an edge of H. Thus f is an isomorphism. The degrees agree under the map: A and 2 have degree three; B and 4 have degree two; C and 1 have degree four; D and 3 have degree two; E and 5 have degree one.

The stated drawing of G is crossing-free, so G is planar. It has V=5 and E=6. Its square interior is split by AC into two bounded faces; CE is a leaf edge and creates no new bounded face. Including the outside gives F=3, and 5−6+3=2. For a second count, choose spanning tree AB,BC,CD,CE. It has five vertices, four edges, and one face. Restore DA and AC inside the stated embedding: each closes a cycle and splits one face, giving six edges and three faces. Therefore V−E+F stays two at every step.

Tree proof. A one-vertex tree has zero edges. Every finite tree with at least two vertices has a leaf, since an endpoint of a longest path cannot have an additional neighbor without extending that path or forming a cycle. Delete a leaf and its unique edge; connectedness and acyclicity remain. Induction on vertices gives n−1 edges for the remainder and n after restoring the edge to a tree of n+1 vertices.

Face proof. In a connected plane graph, deleting an edge on a cycle keeps the graph connected and merges its two adjacent faces, lowering E and F together. Continue to a spanning tree, where E=V−1 and F=1. Hence V−E+F=2 there and also before every deletion. The face-merger step relies on a plane-separation fact; the argument does not establish it from elementary arithmetic alone.

Degree proof. For any graph isomorphism f, a vertex v's neighbors map bijectively to f(v)'s neighbors: adjacency preservation maps them forward and the inverse map retrieves every neighbor. Therefore deg(v)=deg(f(v)). The converse “equal degree lists imply isomorphic graphs” is false: a six-cycle and two disjoint triangles have six degree-two vertices each, but one graph is connected and the other is not.

Give two points for a bijection with all six edge images checked and a nonedge justification; two for a valid crossing-free embedding with the outside face counted; two for a spanning-tree restoration argument; two for the three proof paragraphs with their hypotheses and the plane-separation boundary stated; and two for a concrete false-converse counterexample with the differing invariant identified. A complete, bounded proof route

You can stop when you can reconstruct the tree and plane arguments without a diagram doing the logical work, flip and count an augmenting path, and verify an isomorphism by a concrete map. These methods do not classify all planar graphs or settle all assignment problems. The point of the route is to know precisely what each result guarantees and which assumptions let it be used.
