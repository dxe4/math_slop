Counting choices

Begin by naming one complete outcome. If you choose one of three drinks and then one of two snacks, an outcome is a drink–snack pair. Each drink has two snack choices, so there are 3×2=6 pairs. This counts combinations of choices; it does not require a probability model.

Worked example. Arrange three distinct postcards in a row. There are three choices for the first place, two remaining choices for the second, and one for the last. The result is 3×2×1=6 arrangements, written 3!.

3!=3×2×1=6

For a positive integer n, n! multiplies every integer from 1 through n. The convention 0!=1 counts the single empty arrangement. Factorials count arrangements of distinct objects when each is used exactly once.

Try one. How many two-letter codes can you make from A, B and C when repetition is forbidden?

Worked answer

Choose the first letter in three ways and the second in two ways, giving six codes: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA and CB. If repetition were allowed, the second position would have three choices too, giving nine.

Watch the rule. If different first choices allow different numbers of second choices, count those branches separately and add. A single fixed product needs the stated number of options at every stage.

Counting without double counting

This route is for a reader who remembers finite sets and factorials but wants sharper control over overcounting. Across about ten hours, you will count arrangements with repeated objects, choose subsets by two derivations, correct overlap, build a recurrence, and distribute indistinguishable units. The common task is to state the equivalence relation or case split that justifies a formula. The route does not claim to cover all enumerative combinatorics. Its capstone compares three allocation models whose answers look superficially similar but count different objects.

Entry recap. An ordered permutation of n distinct labels has n! possibilities. A binomial coefficient C(n,k) counts k-element subsets, so C(n,k)=n!/[k!(n−k)!] when 0≤k≤n. A union contains an element appearing in either set, while an intersection contains an element appearing in both. These are the local assumptions needed for the first modules. If factorial cancellation feels rusty, verify C(5,2)=5×4/2 by listing the ten pairs once; that small enumeration is the audit to keep nearby.

Ordered modules and bridges

Module 1: Arrange and choose

  1. Why anagrams need division — Count permutations of a multiset and explain the denominator.
  2. Choosing without caring about order — Derive binomial coefficient from ordered selections.
  3. A doorway into Pascal's triangle — Prove Pascal's identity by partitioning subsets.

Repeated-letter arrangements and unordered groups both use division, yet their denominators come from different questions. In one, temporary labels on identical letters erase to the same visible word. In the other, orders of distinct selected people erase to the same group. Pascal's identity adds a second proof technique: distinguish one person and partition all groups into contain and omit cases. Work one count twice. For example, C(6,2)=15 by a factorial calculation, and also C(5,2)+C(5,1)=10+5 by the distinguished-person partition. Name the map between each case and its smaller subset rather than citing a triangle pattern.

Module 2: Overlap and recurrence

  1. Counting overlaps only once — Count union of two and three finite sets without double counting.
  2. A recurrence remembers its past — Derive Fibonacci recurrence from last-step cases.
  3. Distributing identical treats — Derive weak compositions with bars and stars.

Overlap correction, recurrence, and stars and bars concern three different structures. For two mailing lists, an attendee in both is counted twice until one copy is removed. For one- or two-step climbs, the final move partitions paths into disjoint cases whose counts refer to earlier heights. For identical objects shared by named recipients, bar positions encode a tuple of nonnegative shares. After each lesson, write the object in one line: a distinct attendee, a sequence of moves, or a vector of shares. If you cannot identify the object, a plausible formula may be counting the wrong universe.

Worked mixed practice

A six-letter code has three A tiles, two B tiles, and one C tile. Its visible anagrams number 6!/(3!2!)=60 because every visible word has 3!2! temporary labelings. Separately, six different guests choose a two-person welcome team in C(6,2)=15 ways. If those guests also register for either tea or coffee, and four choose tea, three choose coffee, and two choose both, the number choosing at least one is 4+3−2=5. Those are three unrelated counts; do not multiply them without a stated combined outcome. For a short recurrence check, a₀=a₁=1 and aₙ=aₙ₋₁+aₙ₋₂ give a₄=5. The five stair paths are 1111,112,121,211,22. Every result has a small-case representation that exposes what gets identified or split.

Capstone

A club has three named members and four identical gift vouchers. Compare three rules. Model I distributes the four identical vouchers freely among the members, allowing zero shares. Model II distributes four individually numbered vouchers freely. Model III gives each named member exactly one individually named prize from a collection of three prizes, with no one receiving the prize bearing their own name. Count all three, and explain why one formula cannot answer all three. For Model I also count allocations in which the first member receives at least one voucher by a direct reduction and by subtracting the zero-share cases. Present a tiny two-voucher audit of Model I as a check, and state which changes if zero shares are forbidden.

Model solution

Model I is a nonnegative solution x₁+x₂+x₃=4, so stars and bars gives C(6,2)=15. Model II has four independent labeled vouchers, each with three recipient choices, giving 3⁴=81. Model III is a derangement of three prizes; the two assignments BCA and CAB in recipient order avoid every self-match, so the count is two. That result can also be found by 3!−3·2!+3·1!−1=2. In Model I with x₁≥1, set y₁=x₁−1; then y₁+x₂+x₃=3 has C(5,2)=10 solutions. Alternatively, all 15 allocations minus those with x₁=0; the latter solve x₂+x₃=4 and number C(5,1)=5, again giving ten. With two identical vouchers, the six allocations are (2,0,0),(0,2,0),(0,0,2),(1,1,0),(1,0,1),(0,1,1), which audits the bar encoding. If zero shares are forbidden in Model I, assign one voucher to each member first, then distribute the remaining one in three ways. In Model III each member necessarily receives one prize by definition.

Assessment rubric

Give two points for each model's count with its distinguishability assumptions, two for the two independent derivations of the first-member restriction, one for the complete two-voucher audit, and one for the positive-share change. An answer that writes 3⁴ for identical vouchers has changed Model I into Model II. An answer that applies stars and bars to the named prizes has erased their identities. Treat those as model errors even if later arithmetic is correct.

Outcomes and a stopping point

  • Correct overcounting. Explain the 3!2! denominator for the six-letter code in mixed practice and the model mismatch between labeled and identical vouchers in the capstone.
  • Use inclusion–exclusion. Use the club-overlap calculation in mixed practice and the three-event derangement ledger for Model III in the capstone.
  • Build recurrences. Derive the staircase recurrence in the linked lesson, then reproduce its a4=5 check from the five explicit paths in mixed practice.

Stop when you can explain, without a formula sheet, why 15, 81, and 2 are all legitimate answers to three different gift questions. For extra proof practice, derive C(n,k)=C(n−1,k)+C(n−1,k−1) by an explicit reversible partition, then use the same habit to derive the last-step recurrence. This route's main achievement is reliable model selection and correction of duplicate counts, not speed with factorial arithmetic.
