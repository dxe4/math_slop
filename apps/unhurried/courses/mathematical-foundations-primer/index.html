A short route from a claim to a proof

This course is for someone who can do integer arithmetic but has not written a proof recently. The full route is estimated at around six hours, including exercises, revisions and a capstone. You can begin with one article and one attempt on scrap paper; there is no need to finish a module tonight. The optional small sittings below give you a place to stop and something to recall next time. The bounded destination is a direct proof about even integers. You will also learn how a counterexample defeats a claim about every integer. Finishing the route does not make every proof technique familiar; it gives you a dependable first routine for precise claims.

Entry recap. Integers include negative whole numbers and zero. “For every” has to survive every allowed input; “there exists” needs one allowed witness. An implication has a hypothesis, which you may assume, and a conclusion, which you must establish. If a symbol is new, pause at the first lesson’s local explanation. Keep a scrap sheet with three columns headed “claim,” “reason,” and “what remains.” That small record is the working tool for this route.

One small sitting at a time

These are optional stopping points, not deadlines. Aim for one idea and one attempt each time. The reading estimates on the articles leave out time spent thinking; an exercise can comfortably become its own later sitting. When you return, try the recall question before reopening your notes, then check the explanation. If it has faded, reread that part and continue.

1. Find a witness

Read the definition lesson. On paper, finish “−6 is even because −6 = 2 × ___, and ___ is an integer.” Then explain why using 3.5 would not certify that 7 is even.

Check this sitting

Both blanks are −3. The definition requires an integer witness; 3.5 is not one. Stop when you can explain that restriction in your own words. Before your next visit, leave yourself the question “Does zero have an evenness witness?”

2. Let a pattern fail

First recall the witness for zero without looking: what number doubles to zero? Then read the conjecture lesson. Try its first three inputs yourself. Stop after writing one sentence explaining why the failure matters more than the earlier successes.

Check this sitting

Zero uses witness 0. The prime formula gives 3, 5, then 9 at inputs 0, 1, 2. Since 9 = 3 × 3 is not prime, the claim about every nonnegative input is false. The earlier successes do not remove that counterexample.

3. Give an arrow its reason

Recall why one counterexample is enough to refute a universal claim. Read the proof-map lesson. For an integer divisible by 10, write n = 10k = 5( ___ ). Fill the gap, and say why it proves divisibility by 5. That is enough for this sitting.

Check this sitting

The gap is 2k. It is an integer because k is an integer, so it supplies the multiplier required for divisibility by 5. The converse fails: 5 is divisible by 5 but not by 10. Each direction has its own obligation.

4. Make a new witness

Before reading, recall what you must check about the multiplier in n = 2k. Read the direct-proof lesson, following its four steps. Close the explanation and reconstruct the witness for the sum. If you have energy, fill the guided gap for the difference too.

Check this sitting

The multiplier must be an integer. Starting with x = 2a and y = 2b, the sum has witness a + b and the difference has witness a − b. Both are integers. A remembered equation becomes a proof only when you explain why the new witness is allowed.

5. Try a different claim

Return to the mixed practice and then, in another sitting if needed, the capstone. Attempt each before opening its model. Keep one sentence from your own attempt about what was given and what you needed to show. A mistaken direction is something useful to revise.

Stop after one complete argument in your own words. On a later visit, try reconstructing it without the model. Repeating or splitting a sitting is fine; a saved or explored mark records your choice, not a verdict on your understanding.

Module 1: Claims and examples

  1. How to read a definition without fear. Classify even integers by displaying an integer witness. After reading, decide whether −6 and 7 are even using the definition, rather than a remembered last-digit rule.
  2. One counterexample ends a universal claim. Record the first failing input to the article’s proposed prime formula and say why two earlier successes do not outweigh it.

The bridge between these lessons is the domain of a claim. “There exists an integer k with n = 2k” asks you to produce a witness for one fixed n. “Every nonnegative integer has property P” asks you to cover infinitely many inputs. A witness can confirm the first kind; one failure can refute the second kind. Neither operation proves an untested universal pattern. Before moving on, write the exact domain beside one claim from each lesson.

Module 2: A proof on paper

  1. What a proof actually owes. Label a given fact, the bridge, and the goal. For “divisible by 10 implies divisible by 5,” name the integer multiplier that makes the bridge work.
  2. A first direct proof with even numbers. Start with arbitrary even inputs, give each its own witness, and construct a witness for their sum.

A proof map can reveal where a reason is missing; the direct-proof lesson fills that gap with algebra. The map “even x, even y → x = 2a, y = 2b → x + y = 2(a + b) → even sum” works because a + b is an integer. Try drawing it yourself without the last arrow, then supply the precise reason for that arrow. A diagram never substitutes for that reason.

Worked mixed practice: a plausible false rule

Consider “For all integers a and b, if a + b is even, then both a and b are even.” First test it with odd inputs. Then turn to the related true statement “If both inputs are even, their sum is even” and identify its witness chain. This task uses all four lessons: read the definition, test a universal claim, map the arrows, and prove a repaired direction.

Hint

Try a = 1 and b = 3. For the true direction, write a = 2r and b = 2s.

Worked solution

The original claim fails for 1 and 3: 1 + 3 = 4 = 2·2 is even, while neither 1 nor 3 has an integer half. The related true statement changes the hypothesis. If a = 2r and b = 2s with integers r, s, then a + b = 2(r + s). Because r + s is an integer, the sum is even. The proof map starts at two even inputs and ends at an even output; reversing the arrow would require another argument and is false.

Check yourself by explaining why 100 examples of even-plus-even sums would be weaker than this one symbolic proof. The examples are accurate but each covers only one pair. The symbolic proof starts with arbitrary even integers, so its algebra applies to every such pair.

Capstone: correct a pattern claim

Write a short note to a friend who claims, “Whenever two integers have an even product, both integers are even.” Your note must name the hypothesis and conclusion, give a counterexample, replace the claim with a true statement about an even input and a product, and prove that replacement directly. Label any witnesses and explain why the replacement is general. Try first without opening the model.

Model response

The hypothesis says the product ab is even; the conclusion asserts both a and b are even. Take a = 2 and b = 3. Their product is 6 = 2·3, even, while 3 is odd. Thus the original universal claim is false. A true replacement is: “If a is even and b is any integer, then ab is even.” Let a = 2k for some integer k. Then ab = (2k)b = 2(kb). Since kb is an integer, it witnesses that ab is even. We took arbitrary inputs satisfying the replacement’s hypothesis, so this is a proof for all such inputs rather than another example.

Check your explanation. Does your counterexample satisfy the hypothesis and fail the conclusion? Is the replacement claim stated clearly? Have you built an integer witness for the product? Have you explained why the arbitrary inputs make the proof general? These questions help you revise a particular argument; they are not a score of your mathematical ability. A different valid counterexample works just as well. If the counterexample is right but the proof assumes its desired conclusion, revisit the proof map and try again.

A satisfying stopping point

Stop once you can explain the capstone without reading the model: one example refutes the false universal claim, while a symbolic witness proves the repaired claim. That is a real proof skill, even if it takes several attempts. If you want a next route, the accelerated course treats contrapositive and induction; neither is required to finish this one.
