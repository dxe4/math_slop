Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Powers and logarithms

For a positive integer n, aⁿ means a multiplied by itself n times. For example, 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. For a nonzero base, a⁰=1 and a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. A negative exponent describes a reciprocal.

Worked example. With base 3, the logarithm asks which exponent produces a positive input. Since 3⁴=81, log₃(81)=4. More generally, for a positive base b different from 1, log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with x positive.

34=81log3(81)=4

Multiplying powers with the same base adds their exponents: 2³×2²=2⁵. For positive inputs, this becomes the logarithm rule log₂(uv)=log₂(u)+log₂(v). It is a rule about a product.

Try one. Find log₂(1/8) and explain the negative sign.

Worked answer

Because 2⁻³=1/2³=1/8, the logarithm is −3. For a base larger than 1, inputs between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms.

Watch the operation. Logarithms do not generally turn a sum into a sum: log₂(2+2)=2, while log₂(2)+log₂(2)=2 happens to agree in this case. Using 2+4 instead gives log₂(6), which differs from 1+2=3 because 2³=8.

Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Recover techniques by testing their boundaries

This route assumes prior exposure to first-order equations, characteristic roots and 2×2 matrices. It is a refresher focused on seven selected lessons, not a general differential-equations course. Allow about eight and a half hours: roughly three hours for linked reading and checks, three hours for ten worked problems and corrections, and the remainder for the mixed task and capstone. Work from the diagnostic before rereading familiar material; spend time where an unsupported stability claim or a numerical artifact exposes a gap.

Entry diagnostic. Without notes, solve u′=−2u from u(0)=3, find the roots of λ²+2λ+5=0, and classify the origin of x′=−x,y′=2y. Answers are 3e−2t, −1±2i, and a saddle. If the first is uncertain, use the scalar module carefully. If the second or third is uncertain, refresh quadratics or eigenvectors before the systems module. Derivative, exponential and matrix notation are repaired in the linked lessons. The route does not cover Laplace transforms, partial differential equations or global nonlinear dynamics.

Three modules for a focused refresher

Scalar equations

Revisit The mathematics of a cooling cup and Growth that runs out of room. Bridge them by subtracting the equilibrium. Cooling gives U′=−kU globally and an exponential contraction. Logistic growth near K gives a negative derivative −r at K, but away from K the rate is quadratic and the population cannot be replaced globally by a cooling curve. For two of the ten problems, derive the cooling half-life and locate the logistic inflection by differentiating P′ along a rising solution. Record where you divided by U, P or K−P and restore any equilibrium omitted by that division.

Linear oscillation and systems

Read Three ways a spring can settle and When a small push keeps adding up. The first has no external input; the second adds a sinusoidal force. Characteristic roots describe the homogeneous transient in both. For three more problems, classify one spring in each damping regime, verify the resonant t sin(ω0t) particular solution by substitution, and compute a damped steady amplitude. Do not infer “infinite response” from a denominator that became zero in a formula derived under nonresonance. State whether c=0 or c>0 each time.

Nonlinear local behavior

Read Eigenvalues draw a phase portrait, Where a local approximation stops deciding, and A tiny parameter change can erase equilibrium. The bridge is from exact linear modes to a derivative or Jacobian used only near an equilibrium. For the remaining five problems, classify a node, a saddle and a spiral from explicit matrices; compare ±x³ where linearization is inconclusive; and draw phase arrows for μ−x² at μ positive, zero and negative. A phase portrait is a statement about the specified system and time direction. Label any conclusion from a linear approximation as local, and use signs directly when the eigenvalue or derivative is zero.

One stability checklist across the modules

First find the equilibrium by setting the rate to zero. Second inspect the exact scalar sign or linear eigenvalues. Third ask whether the test has a boundary value: f′=0 for a scalar equilibrium, an eigenvalue with zero real part for a continuous linear system, or a multiplier of magnitude one for an iterated map. Fourth state the conclusion's domain: nearby states, positive states, or all states on a specified interval. A negative derivative at a scalar equilibrium gives local attraction under the smoothness conditions in the lesson; it does not prove every distant start reaches it.

f′(x*)<0suggests local attraction for x′=f(x)

Numerical checking is a separate activity. A small Euler step may follow a direction field, but y′=−ky with Euler factor 1−kh can grow spuriously when kh>2. Conversely a smooth-looking sampled plot does not repair a zero derivative in a proof. In your notebook, keep “exact identity,” “theorem under hypotheses,” and “numerical observation” as separate labels beside every conclusion.

Worked mixed task: the same equilibrium, three tests

Compare u′=−u, v′=−v³ and the Euler update wn+1=wn−3wn, each starting at 0.1. Decide whether zero attracts nearby states in each rule, and give the mechanism.

Hint

The Euler multiplier is 1−3. For v, use a sign chart or solve by differentiating v−2.

Worked answer

For u, u(t)=0.1e−t and zero is exponentially attracting. For v, v(t)=0.1/√(1+0.02t), so it approaches zero more slowly; |v(t)| never exceeds its start, proving stability as well as attraction. For the Euler rule, wn+1=−2wn, so wn=0.1(−2)n and zero is unstable in the discrete update. This last instability belongs to a coarse numerical method applied to u′=−u, not to the underlying continuous equation. The three initial values agree, yet the update laws and conclusions differ.

Capstone: a damped motion and a disappearing rest state

Analyze x″+2x′+2x=0 with x(0)=1, x′(0)=0, alongside z′=μ−z² for μ=1,0,−1. For the oscillator, find an exact solution, state whether it oscillates, and check the sign of mechanical-energy change. For each scalar parameter, list equilibria and stability; at μ=0 explicitly test the linearization. Finally apply one Euler step h=3 to q′=−q from q(0)=1 and explain any misleading result. This capstone joins exact algebra, local phase reasoning and a numerical check.

Model solution

The characteristic roots of λ²+2λ+2 are −1±i, so x(t)=e−t(A cos t+B sin t). x(0)=1 gives A=1; x′(0)=−A+B=0 gives B=1. Thus x=e−t(cos t+sin t), an oscillation with decaying envelope. With m=1,c=2,k=2, E=((x′)²+2x²)/2 obeys E′=−2(x′)²≤0. It need not decrease strictly at every instant of zero velocity.

For μ=1, equilibria are −1 (f′=+2, unstable) and +1 (f′=−2, locally attracting). For μ=0 the only equilibrium is zero, with f′(0)=0. Linearization gives no verdict; f(z)=−z² points left on both sides. Positive starts approach zero, but negative starts move away, so zero is unstable on ℝ. For μ=−1 there are no real equilibria and z′=−1−z²<0. Finally Euler gives q1=1+3(−1)=−2, whereas the exact value q(3)=e−3>0. The sign reversal and larger magnitude are step-size artifacts. This comparison does not claim that every numerical scheme fails at h=3.
