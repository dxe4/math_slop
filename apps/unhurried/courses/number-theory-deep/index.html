From simultaneous clocks to a toy public key

This proof route studies a narrow chain of results: Bézout gives modular inverses; those inverses construct a solution for two coprime clock readings; prime moduli let us permute nonzero residues and reduce exponents; repeated squaring makes the resulting calculations short; and a tiny RSA example combines those ingredients. It is a sustained study of named elementary theorems, not a course in modern cryptography. You will state hypotheses each time: coprime moduli for the Chinese remainder theorem, a prime modulus for Fermat's theorem, and a coprime message for the RSA correctness proof used here.

Budget about fifteen hours for the six linked lessons, reconstruction of two proofs, the mixed challenge and capstone. Prerequisites are Euclid's algorithm, induction or an equivalent comfort with invariants, and basic congruence arithmetic. A reminder: x ≡ a (mod m) means m divides x−a. A modular inverse of u is a residue v with uv ≡ 1. The article pages give local repairs, so you can enter at a module rather than treating the entire route as a gate. The stopping point is a numerical round-trip with a theorem explaining it and a written account of exactly what the theorem does not establish.

Module 1: Modular tools

  1. A gcd as a signed combination. Reconstruct the invariant: every remainder is an integer combination of the original inputs. A final gcd of one is a concrete certificate, not merely an existence claim.
  2. When clock multiplication can be undone. Prove both directions of the inverse criterion. For necessity, an inverse congruence produces au−mv=1, which every common divisor of a and m must divide.
  3. Two clocks, one residue class. Construct x=b+nt for coprime m,n by solving nt ≡ a−b (mod m). Then prove uniqueness modulo mn rather than claiming a unique integer.

The bridge is the coefficient identity. If nu+mv=1, then u undoes multiplication by n modulo m. That same u chooses t in the Chinese remainder construction. For uniqueness, an integer divisible by both coprime moduli is divisible by their product. This is a proof obligation, not something a 15-entry table can establish for arbitrary m and n. Check that you can derive it from Bézout before moving on.

Module 2: Powers and primes

  1. A prime modulus permutes residues. Write down why multiplication by a number not divisible by p maps 1,…,p−1 to the same set in a different order. Explain why their product can be cancelled modulo p.
  2. Huge powers from a short binary ledger. Treat the bit algorithm as a proof exercise: preserve result·baseremaining ≡ ae while remaining is halved. The algorithm works for composite moduli too; Fermat's theorem is not a prerequisite for computing a power.

The bridge here separates an identity from an algorithm. Fermat explains why certain exponents yield residue 1 when the modulus is prime. Repeated squaring evaluates a power under a modulus without asserting a special period. For instance 36 ≡ 1 (mod 7) follows from Fermat, while repeated squaring can check it: 3² ≡ 2, 3⁴ ≡ 4, and 3⁶=3⁴·3² ≡ 4·2 ≡ 1. That check does not replace the permutation proof for every eligible a and p.

Module 3: Toy key

  1. A toy public lock with arithmetic keys. Read the quantified claim carefully. It proves decryption for messages coprime to pq by reducing separately modulo p and q, then combining the two congruences. Its arithmetic examples are intentionally tiny, and its proof says nothing about secrecy.

The final bridge combines the earlier results. The key equation ed=1+k(p−1)(q−1) is a Bézout-style inverse condition modulo the product (p−1)(q−1). Fermat shows med ≡ m separately at p and q for a message coprime to both. The uniqueness idea from the Chinese remainder theorem then converts agreement at both primes into agreement modulo pq. Repeated squaring supplies an economical numerical trace. Each ingredient has one job; removing its hypothesis can invalidate that step.

Worked mixed challenge: two clocks and one exponent

Solve x ≡ 2 (mod 5), x ≡ 3 (mod 7). Write x=3+7t. Modulo 5 this gives 3+2t ≡ 2, or 2t ≡ 4. Since 2·3 ≡ 1 (mod 5), t ≡ 3·4 ≡ 2. Choose t=2, obtaining x=17. All solutions are 17+35k: changing t by 5 changes x by 35, and the coprime uniqueness proof excludes any other class. Verify 17=3·5+2 and 17=2·7+3.

Now calculate 176 modulo 7. Here 17 ≡ 3 (mod 7), and 7 is prime with 7∤3. Fermat gives 3⁶ ≡ 1. The same conclusion can be checked with squares: 3² ≡ 2, 3⁴ ≡ 4, so 3⁶ ≡ 4·2 ≡ 1. The CRT solution did not make Fermat true; it merely produced an integer whose residue modulo 7 is 3. Keeping those two reasons separate prevents a numerical coincidence from being described as a proof.

Try to alter the first clock from 5 to 14 while leaving the second at 7. The conditions x ≡ 2 (mod 14) and x ≡ 3 (mod 7) conflict: the first forces x ≡ 2 (mod 7). The inverse of 7 modulo 14 does not exist, and the two readings disagree modulo their shared factor 7. That failure is more informative than an unsuccessful search through candidates.

Capstone: prove and compute a small-key round-trip

Choose p=7, q=11, n=77 and φ=(p−1)(q−1)=60. Use e=7. Find d with ed ≡ 1 (mod 60), then encode m=2 and decode the resulting residue. Show a repeated-squaring trace for decryption. Prove the round-trip for every message coprime to 77 using Fermat separately at the two primes and the coprime divisibility lemma. State one edge case outside that proof and explain why the small demonstration says nothing about security. Finally, prove either the inverse criterion or the two-modulus CRT uniqueness step in your own words; use the model below to check the logic.

Model solution and proof checks

Since 60=8·7+4, 7=1·4+3, and 4=1·3+1, back-substitution gives 1=4−3=2·4−7=2(60−8·7)−7=2·60−17·7. Thus −17 ≡ 43 (mod 60) is an inverse of 7, so d=43 and ed=301=1+5·60. Encoding m=2 gives c=2⁷=128 ≡ 51 (mod 77).

For decryption, successive squares of 51 modulo 77 have residues 51,60,58,53,37,60 for exponents 1,2,4,8,16,32. Since 43=32+8+2+1, multiply 60·53·60·51 modulo 77. The result is 2; a convenient reduction is 60·53=3180 ≡ 23, then 23·60=1380 ≡ 71, then 71·51=3621 ≡ 2. This is the requested numerical round-trip.

For any m with gcd(m,77)=1, neither 7 nor 11 divides m. Fermat gives m⁶ ≡ 1 (mod 7) and m¹⁰ ≡ 1 (mod 11). Since 300=50·6=30·10, m³⁰¹=m(m³⁰⁰) ≡ m modulo 7 and modulo 11. The two primes are coprime. If both divide m³⁰¹−m, their product 77 divides it, so decryption recovers m modulo 77. The numerical trace tests one m; this argument covers all coprime m.

One proof of the needed uniqueness lemma is: if 7∣z and 11∣z, write z=7k. Because gcd(7,11)=1, Bézout gives 7u+11v=1. Multiplying by k shows k=7uk+11vk, and 11 divides both terms: it divides 7k=z by hypothesis and clearly divides 11vk. Hence 11∣k and 77∣z. An edge message such as m=7 is not coprime to 77, so this particular Fermat-based proof does not apply to it; that observation alone does not assert decryption fails. Tiny factors 7 and 11 are trivial to recover, and no padding or attack model is analysed, so the round-trip is not security evidence.

A theorem chain with visible boundaries

You have a constructive CRT for two coprime moduli, a permutation proof of Fermat's little theorem, and a scoped RSA correctness argument with a small exact trace. The proof exercises can be checked independently of the toy key. A stronger treatment could handle noncoprime CRT data, all RSA message classes, and real cryptographic security; those require additional hypotheses and methods. This route ends with the precise results you have actually established.
