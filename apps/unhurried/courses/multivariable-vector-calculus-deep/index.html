Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

Audit the hypotheses behind familiar formulas

This route is a selected proof workshop on differentiability, circulation, and boundary-to-interior identities. Allow about eighteen hours: eight linked lessons with exercises, the connective proofs and counterexamples here, written attempts at the proof tasks, and a capstone audit. The focus is sustained mathematical argument, not exhaustive coverage of vector calculus or a credential. We prove certain rectangular cases and use the general Green, divergence, and Stokes theorems with their hypotheses stated. Differential forms and a general Stokes proof are outside this route.

Entry recap: a limit in two variables must survive all approaches, not only coordinate lines. A differentiable f at p has one linear map L for which f(p+h)=f(p)+L(h)+o(||h||). A piecewise C¹ path supplies a velocity r′, and an integral of F·r′ is sensitive to orientation. The fundamental theorem of one-variable calculus converts endpoint differences into integrals of derivatives. A field being C¹ on a neighborhood means its first partial derivatives exist and are continuous not just on a boundary curve, but in nearby points on both sides. Keep this neighborhood condition visible when testing a theorem.

Use an assumption ledger for every result: name the region or surface, its orientation, where the field is defined, its regularity, and whether the conclusion is local or global. These checks prevent a compact formula from hiding the actual theorem.

Three modules and their proof bridges

Differentiability

  1. A compass that points uphill. Reconstruct Duf=∇f·u from the linear remainder, then use Cauchy–Schwarz for the largest unit-direction rate. The key question is exactly where differentiability enters. If you remove it, coordinate partial derivatives may still exist, but the common linear approximation need not.
  2. Partial derivatives can miss a cliff. Prove discontinuity along a diagonal and then rule out differentiability. Bridge to later theorem audits: an algebraically correct derivative calculation at points of a punctured domain does not automatically control a missing point. Distinguish a theorem's pointwise computations from its domain-wide premises.

Domains and circulation

  1. Why polar coordinates need an extra r. Compute det DΦ=r and compare it with an annular-sector quotient. The coordinate map degenerates at r=0, but that single exceptional point has zero area for the bounded examples in the lesson. Explain why this observation alone is not a general change-of-variables proof.
  2. Does a round trip cost any work?. Prove the endpoint rule for a gradient using the chain rule on each path piece. Concatenating paths makes intermediate potential values telescope. This proof works on any domain where one globally defined potential exists along the paths.
  3. The hole that curl cannot see. Compute curl zero and circulation 2π for the punctured-plane vortex. Bridge from the endpoint proof: because a closed-loop integral is nonzero, no globally single-valued potential exists on that domain. Zero curl is local information; simple connectivity supplies a sufficient global condition in the standard C¹ theorem.

Boundary-to-interior proofs

  1. A boundary remembers the whole interior. Prove the rectangular case by pairing top/bottom and left/right edges. Note that adjacent rectangular cells cancel their shared edges. The bridge to Stokes is that a planar upward surface recovers Green's scalar-curl formula.
  2. Count the flow through a box. Pair opposite faces and apply the one-variable fundamental theorem in x, y, and z. Interior faces of subdivided boxes cancel by opposite normals, analogous to Green's shared edges. Curved-surface passage is a separate theorem.
  3. The same circulation from different surfaces. Verify the disk example directly, then state why a smooth field allows the same boundary circulation to be calculated on a compatible cap. The bridge among these results is cancellation of oppositely oriented interior pieces, though that picture does not replace all limiting and regularity arguments.

Proof practice with an answer standard

First prove the endpoint rule without assuming the path is a single smooth formula. Suppose r consists of two C¹ pieces meeting at B, running A→B→C, and F=∇φ on an open set containing both. On each piece the chain rule gives d(φ∘r)/dt=∇φ(r)·r′. Integrate to obtain φ(B)−φ(A) and φ(C)−φ(B). Adding cancels φ(B) and gives φ(C)−φ(A). This is the model level of justification for a piecewise path; simply writing “conservative” would hide the needed potential.

Second test a false theorem: “If both partials of f exist at p, then every directional derivative equals their dot product with the direction.” Use f(x,y)=xy/(x²+y²), f(0,0)=0. Both partials at the origin are zero, but along u=(1/√2,1/√2), f(tu)=1/2 for t≠0, so [f(tu)−f(0)]/t=1/(2t), which has no finite two-sided limit. The repair is to require differentiability at p. Continuous partials in a neighborhood are one sufficient condition, but not a necessary condition for every differentiable function.

Third audit Green's theorem for the vortex on the unit circle. Its curl vanishes at every nonzero point, and its circulation is 2π. The proposed filled disk includes the excluded origin, so the C¹-neighborhood condition fails. On an annulus ε≤r≤1, the field is smooth. The outer counterclockwise circle contributes +2π; the inner boundary is clockwise and contributes −2π. Their sum is zero, matching the integral of zero curl. That calculation shows how the correct multiply connected boundary repairs the apparent contradiction.

Mixed proof and calculation

Let F=(−y,x,z) on the cube [0,1]³. Compute curl and divergence, then choose one theorem for a boundary square and one for the closed cube. State what direct calculations would check them.

Worked mixed solution

Curl F=(0,0,2); divergence F=1. For the upward oriented bottom square, Stokes predicts counterclockwise circulation ∫∫2 dA=2. Directly, the bottom and left edges contribute zero, while the right and top edges contribute one each. For the whole cube, the divergence theorem predicts outward flux ∭1 dV=1. Directly, x-face contributions cancel, y-face contributions cancel, the top z=1 contributes one, and the bottom z=0 contributes zero. The two answers differ because the boundary square is a one-dimensional loop while the cube boundary is a two-dimensional closed surface.

Now change the field to the vortex extended constantly in z, F=(-y/(x²+y²),x/(x²+y²),0), on a cube intersecting the z-axis. Even where curl and divergence are zero, neither theorem can be applied across that cube without handling the singular axis. A valid region excluding a cylindrical tube around the axis acquires an extra inner boundary. That is the domain check the numerical computations cannot replace.

Capstone: repair a zero-curl argument

A proposed proof says: “For F=(-y/(x²+y²),x/(x²+y²)) on the punctured plane, Qx−Py=0. Green's theorem on the unit disk makes every loop integral zero, so F=∇φ globally.” Audit every implication. Give the actual circulation of the counterclockwise unit circle, explain why the disk step fails, and state a correct domain-aware theorem sufficient for a potential. Finally show what Green's theorem says on an annulus ε≤r≤1, including both boundary orientations.

Model capstone solution

For s=x²+y², Qx=(y²−x²)/s² and Py=(y²−x²)/s², so curl is zero for s>0. Parametrize r(t)=(cos t,sin t). Then F(r(t))=(-sin t,cos t)=r′(t), and circulation is ∫₀²π1 dt=2π. The unit disk contains (0,0), where F is undefined; Green's smoothness premise fails, so that step cannot conclude zero circulation. A correct sufficient theorem says a C¹ planar field with zero scalar curl on a simply connected open domain has a potential there. The punctured plane fails simple connectivity, and its nonzero loop integral directly proves there is no global potential. On the annulus, Green applies because the field is smooth near the region: the outer counterclockwise integral is +2π, the inner clockwise integral is −2π, and their sum equals the zero curl integral. The two boundary components are both required.

Self-review rubric. A complete audit identifies the undefined origin before invoking Green, calculates 2π directly, states a correct sufficient domain condition without claiming it is necessary, and assigns +2π and −2π to the annulus's induced boundaries. It also explains why a zero-curl calculation is local while a global potential is a statement about all admissible paths. If any premise is missing, rewrite the theorem in words before doing more algebra. A satisfying stopping point is the ability to catch an invalid domain or orientation premise and to prove the box and rectangle identities from one-variable calculus. General curved-surface proofs remain a distinct further study.
