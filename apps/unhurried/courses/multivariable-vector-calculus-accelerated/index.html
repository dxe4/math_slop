Rebuild the toolkit by checking assumptions

This route is for a reader who has studied multivariable calculus before and wants the core calculations back in working memory. Allow about ten hours for eight linked lessons, their exercises, the diagnostic, mixed problems, and a capstone. The route recovers gradients, coordinate integrals, line work, and three boundary-to-interior theorems in specific settings. It does not supply general proofs of the curved-boundary theorems.

Start with a diagnostic on paper. Can you compute ∇(x²+y²)=(2x,2y)? Can you describe 0≤x≤y≤1 in the opposite integration order? Can you identify the positive boundary direction for an upward disk? Can you name the missing condition when a zero-curl field has a hole in its domain? If any answer is uncertain, make that item your first checkpoint in the corresponding module. A refresher is useful when it changes what you can set up, not when it merely feels familiar.

Recall the units: ∇f·u is change per unit distance for a unit u; a Jacobian scales area; F·dr measures signed work along a curve; F·n dS measures signed flux through a surface. Curl concerns local circulation, while divergence concerns local outward flow. Sorting these types before calculating prevents many plausible but invalid formulas.

Three modules to revisit in order

Differential tools

  1. Reading a landscape without seeing the mountain. Use contour equations to recover the geometry of a two-variable function. The bridge to a gradient is that equal-height motion has zero first-order height change when a differentiable contour is traversed tangentially.
  2. A compass that points uphill. Compute directional rates only after normalizing a direction and checking differentiability. At a stationary point, a zero gradient needs second-order or other information; it does not make the whole function constant.

Integral setup

  1. Why polar coordinates need an extra r. Reconstruct area factors from the derivative matrix rather than memorizing them as symbols.
  2. The same region, two ways to slice. Write inequalities for the set before changing bounds. The bridge from coordinate change to order change is that both preserve a geometric integral while altering its computational description.
  3. Does a round trip cost any work?. Parametrize oriented paths and compute F·r′. Do not confuse area Jacobians with a line's velocity factor. A potential gives a reliable endpoint check where it exists globally.

Boundary theorems

  1. A boundary remembers the whole interior. A planar counterclockwise circulation becomes integrated scalar curl. Its rectangular proof shows why interior edges cancel.
  2. Count the flow through a box. A closed three-dimensional boundary uses outward normals and integrated divergence. Pair opposite box faces before generalizing the mental picture.
  3. The same circulation from different surfaces. Stokes relates a space curve to curl flux through a spanning surface. The bridge among all three theorems is orientation: counterclockwise in Green, outward in divergence, and a matched normal-boundary pair in Stokes.

Mixed setup practice

Take F(x,y,z)=(-y,x,z) on the unit cylinder x²+y²≤1, 0≤z≤1. Compute circulation around the upper rim counterclockwise from above using the path, compute curl flux through the upward top disk, and compute outward flux through the whole cylinder by divergence. Label which theorem checks each result. Then ask whether the top disk alone is a closed surface.

Worked mixed solution

Parametrize the upper rim r(t)=(cos t,sin t,1). Then F=(-sin t,cos t,1), r′=(-sin t,cos t,0), and F·r′=1. Circulation is 2π. Curl F=(0,0,2), so upward disk curl flux is 2 times area π, also 2π; Stokes checks this matched pair. Divergence is 0+0+1=1. The cylinder volume is π, hence outward flux over side, top, and bottom together is π by the divergence theorem. Directly, side flux is zero for the rotational xy part and zero from z in the horizontal radial normal; top contributes π and bottom contributes zero. The top disk by itself is open as a boundary component of the solid, so its flux alone is not the theorem's closed-surface left side.

For sign traps, reverse the upper rim: circulation becomes −2π while upward curl flux remains +2π, so the pair is incompatible until the normal also reverses. For a domain trap, remove the cylinder's central axis and consider a field undefined there; no theorem may be applied across the excluded axis without checking the hypotheses. These checks are as valuable as arithmetic.

Capstone: circulation and flux twice

On the unit cube V=[0,1]³ use F(x,y,z)=(-y,x,z). Compute circulation around the bottom square counterclockwise from above by four edge integrals and by Stokes on the upward square. Compute outward flux through the cube by six faces and by divergence. Explain why the two totals answer different questions even though both involve the same F.

Model capstone solution

On the bottom edge y=0, travel x: F·dr=0. On the right edge x=1, travel y upward: F·dr=1 dy, giving 1. On the top edge y=1, travel x backward: F·dr=−1 dx from 1 to 0, giving 1. On the left x=0, the y component vanishes. Circulation is 2. Curl F=(0,0,2), upward square area is 1, and Stokes gives 2. For flux, x faces have ±(-y) contributions that cancel, and y faces have ±x contributions that cancel. The top z=1 contributes 1 and bottom z=0 contributes 0. Total outward flux is 1. Divergence is 1 and cube volume is 1, confirming 1. Circulation samples tangential force along a loop; flux samples normal force over a closed surface. Their dimensions and orientation rules differ.
