A sustained route through small games and ranked-ballot limits

This course takes about sixteen hours including nine articles, proof checks, exercises and a constitution capstone. Its depth comes from stating hypotheses and testing implications on finite models. You will derive a repeated-game incentive threshold, solve a mixed equilibrium, compare three ballot procedures on identical rankings, and state Arrow's theorem with its exact domain. A complete reading of this bounded route is not a credential in game theory, economics or political theory, and the full proof of Arrow's universal theorem remains beyond the article's scope.

Entry recap. A strategy is an available action or a rule for choosing actions after a history. A payoff function assigns each player a score at each action profile. An expected payoff averages such scores with probabilities; a discounted value weights future rounds by powers of a number δ between zero and one. A strict ranking lists every option once; a complete, transitive weak ordering may include ties. Do not confuse a winner chosen by a rule with a full social ordering of all options. The first kind of output is common in elections; Arrow's stated result addresses the second.

Module 1: Foundations

  1. Two players, four outcomes anchors the notation ui(s). Reconstruct each coordinate separately and say whose choice changes when moving to a neighboring cell.
  2. A move that wins every comparison turns table reading into universally quantified inequalities. For every assertion of dominance, write all opposing cases. The bridge from a payoff number to a theorem is the word “every.”
  3. Stable choices are not always nicest checks unilateral deviations at a proposed profile. Compare this local test with dominance's all-opponents test, and use the coordination exercise to show that equilibrium need not be unique.

At the end of this module, write a short proof that a strictly dominant action is a best response against each pure opposing action: every required strict inequality is already part of the dominance definition. Then explain why the converse fails: a move can be best against one opponent choice and worse against another. This proof check prepares you to distinguish a locally stable outcome from a universally preferred move.

Module 2: Mechanisms and examples

  1. Why randomize a move? shows that every positively supported action must be a best response. Derive both players' indifference equations and verify no unlisted action exists. A numerical solution outside [0,1] would signal the wrong assumed support.
  2. What changes when play repeats? adds observed history and discounting. Derive the 1/2 threshold rather than memorizing it. The bridge is from a one-round unilateral deviation to the present value of future punishments.
  3. The group can prefer A to B to C to A moves from two players' incentives to a group's rankings. Count each pair separately and identify where transitivity fails. A cycle is an existence counterexample, not a statement that every profile cycles.

For proof practice, show why an announced final round breaks the grim-trigger incentive in the article's prisoner-style game: in the last round D strictly dominates, so future punishment vanishes; repeat backward. Then check the majority cycle by listing the two voters supporting each arrow. These two arguments have different structures, backward reasoning and exhaustive counting, but both depend on assumptions that must be said aloud.

Module 3: Limits and deeper questions

  1. Scoring ranked choices changes the winner applies one fixed 2–1–0 score to full rankings. Reconcile total points and handle ties explicitly. Count the same ballots by plurality before judging which method better serves the club's purpose.
  2. What Arrow's theorem actually assumes separates a social welfare function from a winner-only rule. Reproduce the two Borda profiles that violate IIA while each voter retains the same A/B preference. Treat the general impossibility as a cited theorem; the worked profile proves a specific rule failure.
  3. Sincere ballots can be unstable asks whether a voter's false report can improve the chosen winner. Its plurality example is a manipulation of a named winner rule and tie priority. The bridge from Arrow is scope discipline: an IIA violation and a profitable misreport are distinct properties, even though both involve changing ballots.

When you finish the third module, compare three claims in one paragraph: “this cell is a Nash equilibrium,” “this rule violates IIA,” and “this voter can manipulate this profile.” The first is a no-profitable-action-change statement at one game profile. The second compares a social pair's ranking across two voter profiles with fixed individual pairwise views. The third compares two reports by one voter while others are fixed. Their quantifiers and outputs differ.

Worked proof and calculation bridge

In a recurring game, mutual C pays 3 per round, a one-time deviation from C pays 5 now, and permanent D afterward pays 1 per round. With δ = 2/3, compare the two discounted totals. Next take ballots from three voters: A > B > C, B > C > A, and C > A > B. Compute Borda totals and all pairwise majorities. Explain whether a Borda tie cures the pairwise cycle.

Worked solution

Cooperation yields 3/(1−2/3) = 9. One-time deviation yields 5+(2/3)/(1−2/3) = 7, so the punishment threat deters that deviation under the stated infinite-horizon rule. In the three-ballot profile, each option appears once in each rank and receives 2+1+0 = 3 Borda points; Borda is a three-way tie. Pairwise counts are A over B 2–1, B over C 2–1, C over A 2–1. Borda's tie is a separate output under a different rule; it does not change the cycle in pairwise majority comparisons.

Capstone: write a miniature club constitution

The club chooses among mural colors A, B and C. Its six complete ballots are: two A > C > B, one B > C > A, two C > B > A, and one A > B > C. The constitution must specify one of three procedures: plurality, Borda with 2–1–0 points, or pairwise majority. Count every procedure using these same ballots, including every pairwise contest. Declare ties as ties and describe what extra rule would be needed to choose one color. State the exact hypotheses of Arrow's theorem and explain why those hypotheses alone do not select a winner in this profile.

The club also plays an indefinitely repeated resource-sharing game. The one-round payoffs are (C,C) = (3,3), (C,D) = (0,5), (D,C) = (5,0), and (D,D) = (1,1). Both players observe every action, discount future payoffs by δ, and propose grim trigger: play C until any D, then play D forever. Check the one-round equilibrium and derive the exact δ condition for this proposed repeated equilibrium. Include one boundary case in the constitution, such as a known last round or uncertain observation.

Capstone model solution

Plurality counts A = 3, B = 1 and C = 2, so A wins uniquely. Borda scores A = 2×2 + 1×0 + 2×0 + 1×2 = 6; B = 2×0 + 1×2 + 2×1 + 1×1 = 5; C = 2×1 + 1×1 + 2×2 + 1×0 = 7. The totals sum to 18, six complete ballots times three points. C wins Borda uniquely.

Pairwise A versus B: the two A > C > B ballots and one A > B > C ballot favor A, while B > C > A and the two C > B > A ballots favor B, so 3–3. Pairwise A versus C: the same three A-first ballots favor A, the other three favor C, so 3–3. Pairwise B versus C: B > C > A and A > B > C favor B, while the other four favor C, so C wins 4–2. No option strictly beats both rivals: C ties A, A ties both, and B loses to C. Pairwise majority alone does not pick a unique winner here. The constitution might specify a separate tie or completion rule in advance; choosing one after seeing the tallies would change the procedure.

Arrow's theorem concerns a social welfare function on every profile of complete, transitive individual ordinal rankings, with at least three alternatives and at least two voters, that outputs a complete, transitive social ranking. With unrestricted domain, weak Pareto and IIA, it must be dictatorial, so it cannot also satisfy non-dictatorship. A winner-only plurality result and a Borda score calculation are not themselves a full proof of that theorem. The theorem does not tell this club which of its three procedures to value most.

In one round D strictly dominates C: 5 > 3 against C and 1 > 0 against D, so (D,D) is the unique pure equilibrium. Under grim trigger, complying forever gives 3/(1−δ). Defecting once on the cooperative path gives 5+δ/(1−δ), because future punishment pays 1 each round. Cooperation is a best response when 3 ≥ 5−4δ, or δ ≥ 1/2. During punishment, D remains a best response to D, completing the incentive check for this specified history-dependent profile. At δ = 1/2 the player is indifferent; for smaller δ the proposed cooperation fails. A known final round removes the infinite punishment stream and changes that conclusion.
