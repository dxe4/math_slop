Claims and counterexamples

A universal claim says that every object in a stated domain has a property. An existence claim says that at least one object in the domain has it. A counterexample is an allowed object that makes the asserted property false. One counterexample refutes a universal claim.

Worked example. Consider “every positive integer n has n²>n.” The positive integer 1 is allowed, but 1²=1. Because equality is not strict inequality, this single case refutes the claim.

12=11≯1

A repaired statement is true: every integer n greater than 1 has n²>n. Indeed n²−n=n(n−1), and both factors are positive under that hypothesis, so their product is positive. This argument covers every allowed integer in the repaired domain.

Try one. Does “there exists a positive integer n with n²>n” survive the original counterexample?

Worked answer

Yes. Choose n=2: its square is 4, and 4>2. The failed case n=1 refutes the universal version but leaves the existence version true.

Watch the scope. Checking many successful inputs proves those tested cases. For an infinite domain, a general argument is needed to establish a universal claim.

From local surface data to a global turning calculation

This proof-oriented route studies a narrow chain of results: regular surface patches supply metric and curvature calculations; a global theorem links the integral of curvature to topology; transport around one spherical triangle turns that integral into an arrow rotation. The course is a selected application dossier, not a comprehensive differential geometry qualification. Allow about fifteen hours for the six lessons, derivative checks, proof writing, mixed problem and capstone. The general Gauss–Bonnet theorem and general holonomy theorem are invoked with hypotheses, not proved here.

Entry recap. For a patch X(u,v) into ℝ³, Xᵤ and Xᵥ span a tangent plane when their cross product is nonzero. Dot products form the first fundamental coefficients E,F,G. A unit normal n and second derivatives form e=n·Xᵤᵤ, f=n·Xᵤᵥ, g=n·Xᵥᵥ. The quotient (eg−f²)/(EG−F²) is Gaussian curvature. A compact surface has no escaping ends; “without boundary” removes edge terms from the displayed global Gauss–Bonnet formula. Euler characteristic χ is a topological count, V−E+F for a suitable cell structure.

Before each proof, write what is being established. Directly differentiating a sphere patch proves a local coefficient formula. Substituting those coefficients into a theorem verifies an application. Neither operation proves the theorem itself. Keeping that boundary visible is a core skill of this route.

Local geometry module

  1. Surface patches supplies the rank test. Reproduce the sphere cross-product magnitude R²sinθ and mark the excluded poles and longitude seam. A local chart is enough for pointwise geometry, but a whole sphere needs multiple charts when we integrate.
  2. The first fundamental form derives physical route length from Xᵤu′+Xᵥv′. Its sphere coefficients E=R²,F=0,G=R²sin²θ will serve twice: first for route lengths, then for the area element R²sinθ dθ dλ. Show why EG−F²>0 on the regular chart.
  3. Geodesics checks that a great circle's ambient acceleration is normal to the sphere. This matters because the octant capstone uses great-circle edges; their geodesic curvature term vanishes. Do not infer global shortest status from this local acceleration test.
  4. Gaussian curvature turns first and second form matrices into K. Repeat the saddle-origin calculation and the sphere-pole calculation with an explicit unit normal. Note that K is independent of reversing that normal. The intrinsic-curvature theorem is stated in the lesson, but its proof is deferred.

Global bridge module

  1. Gauss–Bonnet states the compact, oriented, boundaryless theorem ∫K dA=2πχ and computes the round-sphere case. The bridge from local to global is integration over the entire surface. Explain why a disk cannot use the closed-surface formula without a boundary term.
  2. Parallel transport compares a tangent arrow before and after a loop. For a positively oriented simple spherical region, holonomy is area/R² modulo 2π. The octant has geodesic edges and three right angles. Relate the resulting π/2 rotation to the region's area and to spherical excess, while labeling the invoked transport theorem.

The named stopping boundary is deliberate: you should be able to perform and defend local computations, and to apply the two global statements correctly. Deriving Gauss–Bonnet through moving frames and patchwise boundary cancellation would need a longer course. Our proof practice targets the arguments actually in scope.

Mixed proof practice: a sphere in two kinds of coordinates

Let X(θ,λ)=(Rsinθ cosλ,Rsinθ sinλ,Rcosθ), where 0<θ<π and λ lies in an open interval shorter than 2π. Derivative dot products give E=R²,F=0,G=R²sin²θ. Thus the area density is √(EG−F²)=R²sinθ. The patch is regular because this is positive. Integrating over 0<θ<π and a longitude range of length 2π, omitting only the seam and poles, which have area zero, gives area 4πR²: ∫₀^{2π}∫₀^π R²sinθ dθ dλ=4πR². Compatible charts cover neighborhoods of the omitted points.

A sphere has principal curvatures of magnitude 1/R in both independent tangent directions, so K=1/R². Consequently ∫K dA=4π, independent of scale. This local-to-global computation checks the right side 2πχ(S²)=4π. The equality for this one model does not prove the theorem for all compact oriented closed surfaces. It does show how a coordinate formula, an area integral and a topological value meet in a verifiable example.

Proof checkpoint: explain why the pole singularity in latitude-longitude coordinates does not invalidate the area calculation. The excluded poles are points with zero area; smooth overlapping charts describe their neighborhoods. The integral over the regular chart approaches the full sphere area. The normal and curvature can also be obtained in a pole-centered graph chart. A coordinate failure is not a missing physical piece of positive area.

Capstone: an octant dossier

On the radius-R sphere, let A=(R,0,0), B=(0,R,0), C=(0,0,R), and traverse A→B→C→A on shorter great-circle arcs. Produce a short proof dossier with five parts: show the arcs are geodesic candidates; find the three interior angles; compute the enclosed area in two ways; compute total curvature of that region; and state the returned parallel-transport rotation, including sign convention and modulo 2π. Name each theorem that is invoked rather than derived.

Hint

Each pair of vertices lies on a coordinate-plane great circle. The octant is one eighth of the sphere. For the second area route, compare angle sum with π.

Model solution

Each edge lies where the sphere meets one coordinate plane through its center, hence on a great circle. Parameterizing any such circle by arc length gives γ″=−γ/R², which is normal to the sphere; its tangential acceleration is zero. At each vertex the two edges follow perpendicular coordinate-plane tangent directions, so all three interior angles are π/2 and their sum is 3π/2.

The symmetry calculation gives area=(1/8)(4πR²)=πR²/2. Independently, the geodesic-triangle form of Gauss–Bonnet says area/R² equals angle sum minus π on a radius-R sphere. Its right side is 3π/2−π=π/2, so area=πR²/2 again. The triangle form is invoked here, not derived from the closed-surface formula alone; a region with boundary needs the boundary-and-corner version.

Since K=1/R² throughout the sphere, the curvature integral over the octant is (1/R²)(πR²/2)=π/2. With the outward normal and the A→B→C→A traversal keeping the octant on the left, the transport-curvature relation gives returned rotation +π/2 modulo 2π. Reversing the loop gives −π/2 modulo 2π. This final equality invokes the holonomy relation; the explicit inputs have been calculated, but a general proof of transport-curvature equality is outside the dossier.

Rubric. A complete dossier verifies great-circle acceleration rather than calling arcs straight by appearance; shows each right angle in the tangent plane; gives both area methods with the boundary Gauss–Bonnet hypothesis; computes KdA with units cancelling; and states orientation, sign and modulo 2π for holonomy. Mark the argument incomplete if a closed-surface theorem is applied directly to the triangular region without boundary terms, or if the holonomy theorem is presented as proved by the area arithmetic alone.

Where this route ends

Stop after you can write the dossier without peeking and can label exactly which parts are calculations, local proofs and theorem applications. That is a coherent understanding of one bridge from metric data to curvature, topology and transport. Further study could prove the boundary Gauss–Bonnet formula from moving frames, then derive holonomy from the connection one-form. Those sustained proofs are beyond this selected route.
