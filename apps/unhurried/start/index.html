The idea ↗

Start here

This library is a shelf of mathematical investigations, not a test you have to pass. Each article is written to make one bounded idea readable on its own: it gives a local refresher for the notation and prerequisites it uses, works through an example, and says what its argument does and does not establish. You can read in order, follow a subject, or begin with whichever question catches your attention.

Want to try mathematics before browsing? A small dinner-party mystery is a complete ten-minute investigation with a hint, a proof and a second case to try. It needs no course background.

Choose a comfortable first step

Small discoveries offers six five-to-seven-minute starting questions about puzzles, dice, symmetry, infinity, voting and patterns. Each has a complete explanation and an optional question to try. You can stop there, or follow its links into longer articles whose time and background are shown separately.

If you want a quick orientation, browse by subject and choose an article whose question sounds interesting. An “open” entry aims to start with familiar ideas and build locally; a “bridge” entry expects some named background, such as derivatives or proof language. These labels describe the entry point, not your ability. The article’s prerequisite capsule is the useful check: if one item feels rusty, use the refresher or glossary, then return. You do not need to finish every prerequisite in the library before you begin.

Articles are independent invitations, even when they sit in a larger subject. A link to a related article is a possible next view, not an assignment. If an explanation moves too quickly, pause at the worked example, write down the objects and assumptions, and try to restate the claim in your own words. If an article does not fit today, leave it; another route may make the same notation feel natural later.

Use the page as a small workshop

Start with the question and the concrete example. In the explanation, notice the exact hypotheses: a statement about continuous functions, finite sets, or positive numbers may stop being true when those conditions change. The practice section includes hints and worked solutions so you can compare approaches. Try a problem before opening its solution if that sounds useful; reading the solution first is also a perfectly good way to learn a technique.

Keep a scratch page for definitions, examples, and counterexamples. When a symbol is unfamiliar, look it up in the glossary and then return to the sentence where it appeared. Search can help you find a topic or term; filters can narrow the shelf by route or time. The notebook can save a note or a place to resume on this device. Use those features as conveniences, not as a progress score.

Refresher: three tiny checks

If you would like a worked reminder first, refresh a starting tool. The short pages cover fractions, algebra, functions, coordinates, sets, proof language and other common ingredients. Relevant reminders also open beside lessons, so you can recover a tool and continue on the same page.

These are reminders rather than a placement quiz. Take as long as you like, or skip them.

  1. Fractions. Simplify 6/8. One common factor is 2, so 6/8=3/4.
  2. Functions. If f(x)=x²−1, what is f(3)? Substitute the input: 3²−1=8.
  3. Proof language. Is “every even integer is divisible by 4” true? No: 2 is even and is not divisible by 4, so one counterexample settles the universal claim.

If one answer felt unfamiliar, that gives you a useful next question, not a reason to stop. You can refresh fractions in a number-theory example, functions in a calculus or modelling article, and proof language in Read a definition or Test a conjecture.

Longer paths, when you want them

Courses gather articles around a shared outcome and add transitions, mixed practice, and a capstone. A primer is a broad, gentler route that emphasizes essential ideas and worked examples. An accelerated route assumes more comfort with the listed prerequisites and spends less time rebuilding them. A deep route is longer and proof-focused, with named results and more sustained practice. “Deep” describes the work in that course, not a credential or complete coverage of a subject. The course page states its scope and a satisfying stopping point; it is reasonable to stop when the outcome you wanted is met.

If proofs are new or rusty, the first proof course in small sittings starts with an even-number definition and ends with an argument you can explain to a friend. Its optional plan pairs one article with one paper-and-pencil attempt, then gives you a question to recall next time. You can split a sitting or repeat it.

Each course still points to articles that can be read independently. If a course module feels like a jump, open its lesson, check the local refresher, and return to the course after the missing idea has a foothold. There is no required pace, streak, score, or finish date. A few careful minutes can be a complete visit.

Five interest-based starting routes

  • Patterns and proof: begin with Test a conjecture, continue to Induction and dominoes, and try The pigeonhole principle. Look for the point where examples stop being evidence and a general argument begins.
  • Change and accumulation: start with A rate at an instant, compare it with A corner that defeats the derivative, then follow the pairing of change and area in Why accumulation undoes differentiation.
  • Symmetry and structure: explore Symmetry as a group, test the idea in The order of a symmetry, and see how structure can be preserved by a map in Maps that preserve structure.
  • Shapes and holes: meet open sets through Open sets as neighborhoods, consider sameness in When are two spaces the same?, then follow loops in Counting loops around a circle.
  • Chance and evidence: define possible outcomes in A sample space for small games, connect chance to an average in Expected value is a weighted average, and examine uncertainty in What a confidence interval covers.

Make the library yours

Pick a question, follow one thread, and let curiosity decide when to branch. Return to an idea later if its first version feels unfamiliar; mathematical understanding often comes from meeting the same structure in a second setting. The library offers many entry points, but it does not claim to cover every prerequisite or every part of mathematics. Use the stated scope to decide what you have learned here and what you may want to explore next.
