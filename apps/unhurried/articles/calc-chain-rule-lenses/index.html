Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

What happens when one change passes through another?

Imagine the temperature of a liquid depends on time, and a sensor's voltage depends on temperature. Voltage changes with time even though its formula names temperature, not time. The chain rule says the two local conversion rates multiply, evaluated at the right places.

Background capsule. A composition f(g(t)) first computes g(t), then feeds that result into f. The derivative g′(t) is the limiting ratio of output change to input change. In units, if g maps seconds to degrees and f maps degrees to volts, their derivative product has volts per second. Differentiability means a finite derivative exists at the point in question. We use a local linear approximation with an error small relative to the input change, written o(h): a remainder r(h) is o(h) if r(h)/h tends to zero.

Two lenses on one example

Worked example. Let temperature be T(t)=20+t² degrees and voltage be V(T)=T³/1000 volts. At t=2, temperature is 24 degrees. The temperature rate is 2t=4 degrees per second. The voltage sensitivity at 24 degrees is 3(24)²/1000=1.728 volts per degree. Multiplication predicts 6.912 volts per second. This is a local rate, not the voltage change over an entire second.

Check with a short step h=0.01. The new temperature is 24.0401, an increase of 0.0401, close to 4h=0.04. The voltage change is approximately 1.728×0.0401=0.0692928, with a small curvature correction. Dividing by h gives a slope near 6.912. At smaller steps the correction, after division by h, disappears. The units cancel correctly: volts/degree times degrees/second becomes volts/second.

A simpler exact example is f(u)=u² and g(t)=3t−1. At t=2, g=5 and g′=3; f′(5)=10, so the composed rate is 30. Direct expansion, (3t−1)²=9t²−6t+1, has derivative 18t−6=30 at t=2.

The rule and a safe derivation

Statement. If g is differentiable at t and f is differentiable at g(t), then f∘g is differentiable at t and its derivative is the product below. Both hypotheses matter. No inverse of g is required, and g′(t) may be zero.

(f∘g)′(t)=f′(g(t))g′(t)

Derivation. Put y=g(t) and k=g(t+h)−g(t). Differentiability of f at y means f(y+k)−f(y)=f′(y)k+r(k), where r(k)/k→0 as k→0, and define r(0)=0. Differentiability of g gives k=g′(t)h+s(h) with s(h)/h→0. Also k/h→g′(t), so k/h is bounded near zero. Divide the composition's increment by h: f′(y)[k/h]+[r(k)/h]. The first term tends to f′(y)g′(t). If k≠0, the second is [r(k)/k][k/h]→0; if k=0, it equals zero by our definition. This avoids dividing by a possibly zero k. Thus the limit exists and equals the displayed product.

The rule does not assert that a composition must fail when an ingredient lacks a derivative. For example, f(u)=u² and g(t)=|t| give f(g(t))=t², differentiable even at zero. The stated hypotheses are sufficient, not necessary. Conversely, a numerical product of guessed rates is not evidence that either defining limit exists.

Check placement and hypotheses

1. Calculate. For H(t)=(1+t²)³, find H′(2) and explain the units if t is minutes and H is millilitres.

Hint for 1

Use g(t)=1+t² and f(u)=u³; evaluate f′ at g(2), not at 2.

Worked solution for 1

g(2)=5 and g′(2)=4. Since f′(u)=3u², f′(5)=75. Therefore H′(2)=75×4=300 millilitres per minute. Direct differentiation of (1+t²)³ gives 3(1+t²)²(2t), the same value.

2. Explain. Let f(u)=|u| and g(t)=t². Why is f(g(t)) differentiable at zero even though f′(0) does not exist?

Hint for 2

First simplify the composition for real t. Then compute its difference quotient.

Worked solution for 2

Since t²≥0, f(g(t))=|t²|=t². Its quotient at zero is h²/h=h→0, so its derivative is zero. The chain-rule hypotheses do not hold because f is not differentiable at g(0)=0. We did not apply the formula; we proved the composition's derivative directly.

Multiply local conversions

For a differentiable composition, the outer sensitivity is evaluated at the current inner output and multiplied by the inner rate. The local-remainder proof covers inner changes that vanish for some steps. A global change over a large interval can differ from this local prediction; curvature accumulates.
