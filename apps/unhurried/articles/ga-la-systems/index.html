Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Where do two constraints meet?

When each equation has at least one nonzero variable coefficient, two linear equations describe two crossing lines, the same line twice, or distinct parallel lines. The corresponding system has one solution, infinitely many, or none. A degenerate equation such as 0x+0y=0 describes the whole plane, while 0x+0y=1 describes no points. Row operations let us decide the solution set without relying on a possibly imprecise graph.

Background capsule. A pair (x,y) solves an equation when substituting its coordinates makes the equality true. It solves a system only when it satisfies every equation. The system ax+by=p and cx+dy=q can be abbreviated A(x,y)ᵀ=(p,q)ᵀ, where A has rows (a,b) and (c,d). The superscript T marks a column; it is not an extra variable. A row operation rewrites the equations while preserving their common solution set, provided its inverse is allowed.

Eliminate a variable, then check

Worked example. Solve x+2y=7 and 3x−y=7. Subtract three copies of the first equation from the second: −7y=−14, so y=2. The first equation then gives x=3. Substitution checks both: 3+2·2=7 and 3·3−2=7. The lines cross at exactly (3,2), because after eliminating y there is no free variable left.

The last two cases look alike on the left side: the second row is twice the first. Their right sides decide whether the lines coincide or stay parallel. A graph may hide that difference if two nearly parallel lines lie close together; exact arithmetic exposes it.

Why elimination preserves the answers

Exact statement and proof. For a finite system of real linear equations, swapping two equations, multiplying an equation by a nonzero number, or replacing one equation by itself plus a multiple of another preserves the set of simultaneous solutions. We can write the third operation as

Rj←Rj+tRi(i≠j)

A common solution makes every original equation true. It therefore makes any scaled equation or sum of equations true. Conversely, a swap reverses by swapping back; multiplication by nonzero t reverses by multiplying by 1/t; and the displayed row replacement reverses by subtracting t times the unchanged row i from row j. Thus every solution of the new system also solves the old one. This proves equivalence in both directions for each permitted step.

For a 2×2 system, elimination leaves either two pivot variables and one solution, at least one free variable and infinitely many solutions, or an impossible row 0=c with c≠0 and no solution. One zero row may leave one free variable; two zero rows leave two. A free variable in a consistent real system ranges over infinitely many real values. This classification assumes exactly two unknown real numbers; it is not a claim about nonlinear curves, whose intersections behave differently.

Why disallow scaling by zero? Multiplying x=1 by zero changes it to 0=0. Every x would satisfy the new equation, although only x=1 solved the original. The missing inverse destroys information. The same care applies when dividing by an expression containing a variable: if it could be zero, a special case must be checked first.

Classify as well as solve

1. Calculation. Solve 2x+y=5 and x−y=1. Show a reversible row operation and verify your result in both original equations.

Hint for question 1

Add the two rows to remove y, then use either original equation.

Full solution for question 1

Replace the first row by its sum with the second: 3x=6, while the second remains x−y=1. Hence x=2 and y=1. Checking gives 2·2+1=5 and 2−1=1. Addition of one row to another is reversible by subtraction, so no solutions were lost.

2. Explain a failure. A proposed solution to x+y=2 and 2x+2y=5 multiplies the first equation by two, subtracts it from the second, obtains 0=1, and then writes x=0,y=1. Diagnose the conclusion.

Hint for question 2

Ask whether any pair can satisfy 0=1 and substitute the proposed pair.

Full solution for question 2

Subtracting twice the first row gives 0=5−4=1, an impossibility. Therefore the system has no solution. The pair (0,1) fails the first equation already, since 0+1=1 rather than 2. Writing coordinates after a contradiction cannot repair it; the two original lines are distinct and parallel.

Arithmetic with geometric meaning

A system is about a shared solution. Reversible row operations preserve that set, so elimination can certify a crossing, coincidence, or inconsistency. A tempting divide-by-zero step can erase a case, and a graph alone may miss an exact inconsistency. If you can explain what 0=0 and 0=1 each mean, this is a satisfying stopping point. Kernel and image extend the same reasoning to the dimensions of a linear map.
