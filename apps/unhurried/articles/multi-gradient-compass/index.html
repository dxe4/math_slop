Which way is steepest?

On a smooth landscape, a single vector can predict first-order height change in any direction. It is called the gradient. Its direction is the fastest uphill direction among unit steps, with a precise exception at flat points.

Background capsule. A partial derivative fx freezes y and measures the limiting eastward rate; fy freezes x and measures the northward rate. The dot product (a,b)·(c,d)=ac+bd combines components, and a unit vector u has length √(u1²+u2²)=1. A directional derivative is the limit [f(p+hu)−f(p)]/h as h tends to zero. Here differentiability means one linear approximation works for every sufficiently small displacement, with an error small compared with displacement length.

Try a compass on an oval hill

Worked example. Take f(x,y)=x²+2y² at p=(1,1). Freezing y gives fx=2x; freezing x gives fy=4y. Thus ∇f(p)=(2,4). North gives rate 4; east gives 2. Northeast along u=(1/√2,1/√2) gives (2+4)/√2=3√2, about 4.24. The gradient's own unit direction is (1/√5,2/√5), and the predicted rate there is 2/√5+8/√5=2√5, about 4.47.

Check the prediction at a finite h. Along u=(1/√5,2/√5), direct expansion yields f(p+hu)−f(p)=2√5 h+(9/5)h². At h=1/10, the actual increase exceeds the linear prediction by 9/500. A larger step has a larger quadratic correction. The derivative is the coefficient left after dividing by h and letting h shrink; it is not a promise that finite steps have exactly linear effect.

Normalize before comparing. The vector (1,2) has length √5, so its dot product with (2,4) is 10. That number describes change per unit of the parameter in p+t(1,2), which travels √5 distance units per parameter unit. Dividing the direction by √5 gives rate 2√5 per unit distance. Without this normalization a longer direction vector would appear steeper simply because the walker travels farther. Steepest-direction comparisons require equal travel lengths.

The theorem and its condition

Exact statement. If f is differentiable at p, then for any unit vector u, Duf(p)=∇f(p)·u. If ∇f(p) is nonzero, the maximum over unit directions is ||∇f(p)||, attained only when u points along the gradient; the minimum is its negative, in the opposite direction. If the gradient is zero, every directional derivative is zero, and no unique uphill direction exists at first order.

Duf(p)=∇f(p)·u≤‖∇f(p)‖

For the first claim, write f(p+h)=f(p)+L(h)+o(||h||), where L is linear. The coordinate directions identify L(h)=fxh1+fyh2. Replace h by tu, divide by t, and take the limit. The inequality follows from Cauchy–Schwarz. Equality with positive sign requires aligned nonzero vectors. This proves the steepest direction statement; a contour sketch is only an illustration.

Existing partial derivatives at a point do not by themselves guarantee the shared approximation used above. The partials counterexample has both partials at its origin but is discontinuous there. Therefore the differentiability condition belongs in the statement. At the origin of our quadratic, ∇f=0 even though every nonzero displacement raises height; the first-order compass is silent about this second-order bowl.

One more useful check is to compare two unit directions that differ only by sign. Their directional derivatives are opposites because the dot product is linear in u. This does not mean walking a finite distance in opposite directions changes height by exactly opposite amounts: quadratic terms can raise both endpoints, as they do at the bottom of a bowl. The gradient describes the first-order part only.

Check the compass

1. Calculation. For g(x,y)=3x²+y² at (1,−2), find the derivative along (3/5,4/5) and the steepest ascent rate.

Hint for question 1

Compute the two partials first, then dot with a unit direction.

Worked solution for question 1

∇g=(6x,2y), so ∇g(1,−2)=(6,−4). The supplied vector has length 1. Its derivative is 6(3/5)−4(4/5)=2/5. Cauchy–Schwarz gives maximum √(36+16)=2√13, reached along (3,−2)/√13.

2. Explanation. At (0,0), g has gradient zero. Does that prove nearby heights equal g(0,0)?

Hint for question 2

Look at g(h,0) and the order of its change.

Worked solution for question 2

No. g(h,0)=3h²>0 for h≠0, while g(0,0)=0. Zero gradient says the first-order directional rate vanishes; it does not say the quadratic or higher-order change vanishes. The origin is actually a strict minimum here.

One vector, with a boundary

For a differentiable landscape, the gradient packages every first-order directional rate, and its length is the largest such rate for unit motion. The same claim can fail without differentiability. A practical stopping point is to compute the gradient, normalize a requested direction, and check what approximation the formula assumes. If a finite step disagrees, inspect the neglected higher-order term before doubting the derivative.
