A singularity sets a limit on a local expansion

The geometric series reconstructs 1/(1−z) perfectly near zero and fails beyond the unit circle. That boundary matches the pole at z=1, even though the function is perfectly finite at many other points outside. Convergence belongs to a particular series centered at a particular point, not just to the formula it happens to represent.

Background capsule. A power series centered at a is ∑n=0∞cn(z−a)n. It converges on some disk |z−a|<R and diverges when |z−a|>R; behavior on the boundary needs separate checking. A geometric series has finite sum ∑n=0Nwn=(1−wN+1)/(1−w) for w≠1. The modulus satisfies |wn|=|w|n.

Compute an error, not just a pattern

Worked example. For |z|<1, zN+1→0, so 1/(1−z)=∑n≥0zn. The exact truncation error after N+1 terms is zN+1/(1−z), with modulus at most |z|N+1/(1−|z|). At z=1/2 and N=3, the partial sum 1+1/2+1/4+1/8=15/8; the exact value is 2 and the error is 1/8. The bound also gives (1/2)4/(1−1/2)=1/8, exactly sharp here.

At z=−1, the terms alternate 1,−1,1,−1,… and do not approach zero, so the series diverges even though 1/(1−z)=1/2 is defined. At z=2, terms grow. This distinction prevents the false statement that the analytic expression's domain is automatically the convergence domain of one chosen expansion.

Locate the radius precisely

The central identity is

11−z=∑n=0∞znfor |z|<1

The finite identity proves convergence inside |z|<1. Outside, terms zn do not approach zero, so the series cannot converge. On |z|=1 they also have modulus one, so again the necessary term-to-zero condition fails. Thus the radius is exactly 1. This proof is self-contained for this particular series.

For a rational example with two complex poles, write 1/(1+z²)=1/[(1−iz)(1+iz)]. A simple expansion is 1/(1+z²)=∑n≥0(−1)nz2n by setting w=−z² in the geometric identity. It converges exactly when |z|<1; the poles at i and −i both lie on the boundary. The absence of odd powers is algebraic, not a claim that the function is constant in odd directions.

A useful general theorem says a holomorphic function's Taylor series at a converges at least up to every closed disk centered at a on which the function is holomorphic on a neighborhood; Cauchy's coefficient estimates prove this. A more sweeping slogan, “radius equals distance to nearest singularity,” needs qualification: one must refer to the singularities of the analytic continuation of the germ, including whether apparent singularities are removable. We do not prove general analytic continuation here. For our rational examples, the explicit geometric identities and actual poles settle the radius directly.

Shift the center and check the edge

1. Calculate. Expand 1/(3−z) around zero and give its exact radius of convergence and an error bound after N+1 terms when |z|<3.

Hint for 1

Factor out 3 and use w=z/3 in the geometric sum.

Worked solution for 1

1/(3−z)=(1/3)∑n≥0(z/3)n=∑n≥0zn/3n+1 for |z|<3. The pole at z=3 and the term test outside show radius 3. The tail has modulus at most (1/3)(|z|/3)N+1/(1−|z|/3). The exact complex tail is (1/3)(z/3)N+1/(1−z/3).

2. Explain. Why does the finite value 1/2 at z=−1 not make ∑n≥0(−1)n converge to it? Can another expansion still represent the function near −1?

Hint for 2

Check the necessary term-to-zero condition; then recenter with w=z+1.

Worked solution for 2

The terms (−1)n never tend to zero, so their series diverges. Recenter: 1/(1−z)=1/(2−(z+1))=(1/2)∑n≥0((z+1)/2)n for |z+1|<2. This new series converges at z=−1 and equals 1/2. A function can admit several local series with different centers and disks.

Radius belongs to a center and a series

Finite geometric sums prove the expansions and exact errors here. The unit-circle boundary for 1/(1−z) is set by the pole at 1, yet many points outside still have finite function values and admit other expansions. For general holomorphic functions, Cauchy estimates and analytic continuation provide the broader theory; do not use the nearest-singularity slogan without specifying that setting.
