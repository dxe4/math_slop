Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

How can two resting states disappear?

Consider x′=μ−x², where μ is a fixed real parameter during each run. An equilibrium is a constant state whose right-hand side is zero. When μ>0 there are two; at μ=0 they meet; when μ<0 there are none. This is the saddle-node or fold normal form, a local model for an equilibrium collision. It does not assert that every real system follows this exact quadratic over a large range.

Local background. For a scalar autonomous equation x′=f(x), a phase line marks zeros of f and draws arrows right where f>0 and left where f<0. An equilibrium attracts nearby trajectories if arrows on both sides point toward it. Its derivative f′(x*) gives a local sign test when nonzero; a zero derivative needs direct analysis.

Track the parameter

Worked example. With μ=4, the equilibria are x=−2 and x=+2. Between them, x′=4−x²>0, so states move right. Outside them, x′<0, so states move left. At −2, arrows point away on both sides: unstable. At +2, arrows point in: attracting. If μ=−1, x′=−1−x²<0 everywhere, so no resting state exists. At μ=0, x′=−x²: states to the right move toward zero, but states to the left move away. The single equilibrium is one-sided attracting and unstable on the whole real line.

Derive the branches and the boundary

Setting μ−x²=0 gives x²=μ. Real roots exist only for μ≥0, with two distinct roots when μ>0. The rate derivative is f′(x)=−2x. At x=+√μ it is negative, which matches arrows toward the equilibrium. At x=−√μ it is positive, matching arrows away. As μ decreases to zero, the equilibrium branches meet at x=0 and the derivative there becomes zero.

x±(μ)=±μfor μ>0,f′(x±)=∓2μ

At μ=0, linearization says x′≈0 and cannot classify the equilibrium. The exact sign f(x)=−x²≤0 shows why the two sides differ. On the right, x′<0 and x decreases toward zero. On the left, x′<0 and x becomes more negative. Indeed x(t)=x0/(1+x0t). For x0>0 it approaches zero; for x0<0 it diverges to negative infinity at t=−1/x0. Thus zero is unstable as a two-sided equilibrium.

For μ<0, write μ=−a² with a>0. Then x′=−(a²+x²)≤−a², so every trajectory moves left at a speed at least a² while it exists. This inequality alone does not quantify its entire lifetime. Separation gives arctan(x/a)=−at+C; the tangent representation reaches a pole in finite forward time for every finite start. A numerical solver should stop before overflow rather than report a trajectory after this singular time. For μ>0, starts below the unstable lower equilibrium also can run toward negative infinity in finite time; the phase-line classification is local around each equilibrium.

Calling this a generic saddle-node bifurcation requires conditions on a wider family F(x,μ), such as a nonzero parameter derivative and nonzero second state derivative at the collision. This page proves the behavior of the stated normal form only. The name supplies context, not an unproved universality claim.

Move the branches

1. Change the scale

Exercise 1. For x′=9−x², find equilibria and classify each using signs or derivatives.

Hint for 1

Set x²=9, then evaluate −2x at both roots.

Worked solution for 1

The equilibria are −3 and +3. At −3, f′=6>0 and nearby arrows move away, so it is unstable. At +3, f′=−6<0 and arrows move toward it, so it is locally asymptotically stable.

2. Inspect the collision

Exercise 2. At μ=0, a student says that zero is stable because solutions starting just to its right move toward it. Identify the missing starts and demonstrate failure.

Hint for 2

Choose an arbitrarily small negative x0 and use x(t)=x0/(1+x0t).

Worked solution for 2

Stability on ℝ requires all sufficiently nearby starts, including those to the left. For x0=−δ/2, the solution is negative and its magnitude grows as the positive denominator 1−δt/2 shrinks. It reaches any fixed negative threshold before its blow-up time 2/δ. Therefore no δ works for the full two-sided stability definition.

The model's precise transition

The fold normal form has two equilibria for positive μ, one nonhyperbolic equilibrium at zero, and none for negative μ. Phase arrows, not the root count alone, determine stability. The collision illustrates why a zero derivative calls for direct analysis. Compare the cubic examples for another zero-derivative boundary.
