Question and background

On a sphere, the shortest nearby route between two points looks straight to a traveler who stays on the surface, even though it bends in the surrounding three-dimensional space. Such a route is a geodesic. We will verify great circles as examples and be careful about how far “shortest” extends.

Local capsule. A surface path γ(s) parameterized by arc length has unit velocity T=γ′. Its acceleration γ″ can be split into a component tangent to the surface and a component normal to it. The tangential component is the intrinsic acceleration ∇TT. A geodesic has zero tangential acceleration. A locally length-stationary path with fixed endpoints satisfies the same equation under regularity assumptions; deriving that variational equivalence for all surfaces needs a first-variation argument beyond this article.

A circle drawn in a plane is curved as a space curve. On a sphere, a great circle can also have nonzero ordinary acceleration. Zero tangential acceleration, rather than zero acceleration in ℝ³, is the right test.

A worked example

Worked example. Consider a sphere of radius R and the equatorial path γ(s)=(Rcos(s/R),Rsin(s/R),0), parameterized by distance. Then γ′=(-sin(s/R),cos(s/R),0), which has length one. Its acceleration is γ″=-(1/R)(cos(s/R),sin(s/R),0)=−γ/R². At each point γ is a normal vector to the sphere, so γ″ has no tangential component. This proves that the equator is a geodesic.

Rotating the whole sphere and the path sends this equator to any great circle. Rigid rotations preserve dot products and tangency, so the same calculation verifies every great circle. This is a direct example proof, not a proof that every sphere geodesic must be a great circle.

Now fix colatitude θ₀ other than π/2 and take the latitude circle. Its spatial radius is Rsinθ₀. Its acceleration toward the latitude-circle axis has a component tangent to the sphere except at the equator. Thus the latitude circle is generally not a geodesic, even though it looks like a round and symmetric route on a map.

A helical geodesic on a cylinder

On the unit cylinder x²+y²=1, let γ(s)=(cos(s/√2),sin(s/√2),s/√2). Its derivative has squared norm (sin²(s/√2)+cos²(s/√2))/2+1/2=1, so s is arc length. The second derivative is γ″=−(1/2)(cos(s/√2),sin(s/√2),0). The vector in parentheses is the outward cylinder normal at γ(s). Thus the acceleration is entirely normal, and the helix satisfies the stated geodesic test.

The route looks curved in surrounding space because it winds around the cylinder. Cut the cylinder along a vertical generator and use coordinates (angle,height) on a patch avoiding that seam. Its metric is dθ²+dz². The helix becomes the straight line θ=s/√2, z=s/√2 on the unrolled sheet. This supplies a second interpretation of the zero tangential acceleration.

The unrolling is local: angle values separated by 2π describe the same place around the cylinder. Consequently the calculation does not establish that a long helical segment is globally shortest between its endpoints. A different winding choice can produce a shorter connection. The geodesic equation certifies local straightness in the surface metric.

The statement and its boundary

Exact local test. For a unit-speed C² path γ on a smooth surface, the geodesic equation is:

∇γ′γ′=0

The symbol ∇ here means “take the ordinary derivative and project it onto the tangent plane.” Hence the equation says the ambient acceleration is entirely normal. On a radius-R sphere the outward normal points along γ, which makes the great-circle calculation transparent. A general derivation from stationary length uses a variation γ(s,ε), fixes the endpoints and integrates a first-variation expression by parts. We state that connection and defer its full proof, along with general existence and uniqueness theory.

Being geodesic does not imply globally shortest. Two antipodal points on a sphere are joined by infinitely many half great circles of equal length πR. Follow one great circle beyond a half turn and its longer arc between two points is still geodesic locally, but the shorter complementary arc wins globally. For non-antipodal points the shorter great-circle arc is the usual distance candidate; the local equation alone does not establish that global claim.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. an equatorial segment. On a sphere of radius 5, find the length of an equatorial arc spanning central angle π/3 and write its acceleration in terms of position.

Hint for exercise 1

Use s=R times central angle and the unit-speed great-circle formula above.

Full solution to exercise 1

The length is 5π/3. In unit-speed form γ(s)=(5cos(s/5),5sin(s/5),0), so γ″=−γ/25. Since γ points normal to the sphere, this acceleration has no tangential component and the arc satisfies the geodesic equation.

Exercise 2. local versus global. A traveler follows a great circle through a central angle 3π/2 between two fixed points. Is that traveled arc globally shortest?

Hint for exercise 2

Use the other arc of the same great circle.

Full solution to exercise 2

No. The traveled length is 3πR/2. The complementary arc between the same endpoints spans π/2 and has length πR/2. The long arc still has normal acceleration at every interior point and is geodesic there, but its local property cannot defeat the explicit shorter competitor.

What to carry forward

A geodesic has zero intrinsic acceleration and is locally the surface analogue of a straight route. Great circles pass the test on a sphere. Global minimality requires more information about endpoints and how long the path is followed.

Surface paths will also appear in parallel transport. There the question changes: instead of choosing a path, we carry a tangent arrow along an already chosen path and examine what happens after a loop.
