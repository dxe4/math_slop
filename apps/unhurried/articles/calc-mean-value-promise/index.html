Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Must an average rate occur somewhere exactly?

A vehicle travels 120 kilometres in two hours. Its average velocity is 60 kilometres per hour. If position changed continuously and had an instantaneous velocity at every interior time, must its velocity have equalled 60 at least once? The mean value theorem answers yes, even when the velocity varies wildly.

Background capsule. A secant slope between (a,f(a)) and (b,f(b)) is [f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a), with a<b. A derivative f′(c) is the local secant limit at interior c. Continuity on [a,b] includes both endpoints; differentiability is required only on (a,b). A continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains a maximum and minimum. At an interior local maximum or minimum where it is differentiable, the derivative is zero: right-hand secants and left-hand secants have opposite allowable signs, so their common limit is zero.

A curving journey

Worked example. Take position s(t)=t² kilometres over t from 1 to 3 hours. The change is 9−1=8 kilometres over 2 hours, so average velocity is 4 kilometres per hour. Since s′(t)=2t, at t=2 the instantaneous velocity is exactly 4. This is not an accidental midpoint rule: for this quadratic and interval, algebra happens to put the matching point at the midpoint.

Consider instead s(t)=t³ on [0,2]. Average velocity is (8−0)/2=4, while s′(t)=3t². The promised point is t=2/√3≈1.155, not the midpoint 1. The theorem promises existence, not an easy location or uniqueness. If the derivative has a plateau at the average slope, many matching points may occur.

In both examples, position is continuous across the closed interval and differentiable in its interior. One cannot simply draw a line parallel to the secant and assume it touches: the proof needs hypotheses and a maximum principle.

Subtract the secant, then apply Rolle

Mean value theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), with a<b, then some c strictly between a and b satisfies the displayed equality.

f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−a

Proof boundary and derivation. First prove Rolle's special case: if q has the same endpoint value and meets the same continuity and differentiability conditions, then q′(c)=0 somewhere inside. If q is constant, any interior c works. Otherwise, the closed-interval extreme-value theorem puts a maximum or minimum different from the common endpoint value at an interior point. Fermat's local-extremum argument in the capsule makes its derivative zero. This derivation invokes the extreme-value theorem; its proof from completeness of the real numbers is outside this lesson.

Now put m=[f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a) and q(x)=f(x)−m(x−a). Then q(b)=f(b)−[f(b)−f(a)]=f(a)=q(a). Rolle gives q′(c)=0. Since q′(c)=f′(c)−m, the promised equality follows. The endpoint hypotheses ensure Rolle applies; the interior derivative hypothesis makes the final equation meaningful.

Failure case. f(x)=|x| on [−1,1] has equal endpoint values, so its average slope is zero. Its interior derivatives are −1 on the negative side and 1 on the positive side; at zero no derivative exists. There is no c with derivative zero. Continuity alone cannot replace differentiability. A jump at an endpoint can likewise defeat the theorem if closed-interval continuity is dropped.

Find and test the promised point

1. Calculate. For f(x)=x²+2x on [0,2], find the average slope and every interior c at which f′(c) equals it.

Hint for 1

Compute f(0), f(2), then solve 2c+2=m.

Worked solution for 1

f(0)=0 and f(2)=8, so m=(8−0)/2=4. The derivative is 2x+2. Setting 2c+2=4 gives c=1, which lies strictly between 0 and 2. Thus it satisfies the theorem.

2. Explain. A function equals x on [0,1) but is assigned f(1)=3. The average slope on [0,1] is 3. Why is there no contradiction when f′(x)=1 at every interior x?

Hint for 2

Check the endpoint limit at x=1 against the assigned value.

Worked solution for 2

f(0)=0 and f(1)=3, hence the average slope is 3. On (0,1), f′=1, so it never equals 3. But as x approaches 1 from the left, f(x) approaches 1, not f(1)=3. The function is not continuous on the closed interval, so the theorem's first hypothesis fails.

An existence promise with exact conditions

Subtract the average-rate line and a zero-derivative point appears through Rolle's theorem. The result certifies that some local rate matches a secant rate; it does not identify a unique point or say the midpoint works. Corners and endpoint jumps show why the hypotheses belong in the statement. A useful next application is comparing derivatives to bound how far a function can change.
