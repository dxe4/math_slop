Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

What happened to the unit square?

A linear map can stretch the square with corners (0, 0), (1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1) into a parallelogram. The determinant is one number that records its signed area: magnitude tells how much area changed, sign tells whether the order of the two side arrows has been reversed. Zero means the square was flattened.

Background capsule. A 2×2 matrix is four numbers arranged as A = [[a, b], [c, d]]. Applied to an input (x, y), it returns (ax + by, cx + dy). Its columns u = (a, c) and v = (b, d) are the images of the horizontal and vertical unit arrows. A parallelogram made by placing u and v tail to tail has ordinary area base × perpendicular height, which is never negative. The determinant adds an orientation sign. In the usual x-right, y-up plane, traverse the parallelogram boundary in the order (0, 0), u, u + v, v. A counterclockwise traversal has positive sign; a clockwise traversal has negative sign. If the two sides are collinear, the area and determinant are zero and there is no orientation to assign.

Three squares, three outcomes

Worked example. Take the map T(x, y) = (2x + y, y) from the preceding exploration. Its columns are u = (2, 0) and v = (1, 1). The image of the unit square has vertices (0, 0), (2, 0), (3, 1), (1, 1). Its base is two units and its height is one, so its area is two. The side order remains counterclockwise, making the signed area +2.

The area formula and its limits

Exact statement, with derivation. For a real 2×2 matrix A with first column (a, c) and second column (b, d), the determinant is

det(A)=ad−bc,area of the image of the unit square=|det(A)|

Why ad − bc? Walk around the image parallelogram in order: (0, 0), (a, c), (a + b, c + d), (b, d). For a polygon whose successive vertices are (xi, yi), the signed area is half the sum of xiyi+1 − yixi+1. This “edge sum” follows by adding the signed trapezoid areas between each directed edge and the x-axis; shared interior strips cancel. Here the only nonzero contributions are a(c + d) − c(a + b) = ad − bc and (a + b)d − (c + d)b = ad − bc. Half their sum is ad − bc. Taking its absolute value gives ordinary area. The argument works whether the parallelogram tilts, reflects or crosses an axis.

This coordinate calculation proves the signed-area formula for the image of the unit square. It also covers translated copies of that square, because shifting a figure does not change its area. The broader theorem that |det A| scales the area of any suitable region requires an additional argument through triangles and area limits; that extension is stated here as context, not proved in this lesson.

Singular case, with proof. det A = 0 exactly when u and v lie on one line (including the possibility that one is zero). If u = 0 the claim is immediate. Otherwise, if a ≠ 0, set t = b/a. From ad − bc = 0 we get d = tc, so v = tu. If a = 0, then c ≠ 0; zero determinant gives b = 0, and v = (d/c)u. Conversely, when v = tu, the determinant is a(tc) − (ta)c = 0; when u = 0, it is also zero directly. When u is nonzero, every output xu + yv is a multiple of u; when u = 0, every output is a multiple of v. Either way, the entire plane collapses onto a line or point. A negative determinant is different: it reverses orientation but does not flatten anything.

Compute, then interpret

1. Calculation. Let A have columns (−1, 0) and (2, 3). Compute its determinant, the area of the transformed unit square, its orientation, and A(1, 1).

Hint for question 1

Here a = −1, c = 0, b = 2 and d = 3. Add the columns to find A(1, 1).

Full solution for question 1

det A = (−1)(3) − (2)(0) = −3. The image area is |−3| = 3 square units. The negative sign means the ordered side arrows have reversed orientation. A(1, 1) = (−1, 0) + (2, 3) = (1, 3).

2. Explanation. “If a matrix has determinant zero, it sends every vector to zero.” Is this true? Give a counterexample and say what zero determinant actually guarantees.

Hint for question 2

Try two nonzero columns where one is twice the other.

Full solution for question 2

It is false. Columns (1, 2) and (2, 4) give determinant 1·4 − 2·2 = 0, yet A(1, 0) = (1, 2), which is not zero. Zero determinant guarantees that the output columns are dependent, so all outputs lie on at most one line and the image of the unit square has area zero.

One number, two pieces of information

For a real 2D linear map, |det A| is the area of the image of the unit square and the sign records its orientation. Det A = 0 is the precise collapse test. No finite picture proves those statements on its own; the coordinate derivation does. If you continue, ask how a map can be described when its square collapses but some line still survives. That is the beginning of rank and kernel.
