What if the best direction crosses a fence?

At an unconstrained smooth minimum, all small directions are available and the gradient must vanish. On a smooth constraint curve, only tangent directions are permitted. The gradient of the objective may be nonzero yet perpendicular to that curve. A multiplier records this alignment; solving its equations finds candidates, which still need classification.

Background capsule. For f(x,y), the gradient ∇f=(∂f/∂x,∂f/∂y) points in the direction of steepest local increase. An equality g(x,y)=0 describes a curve when ∇g≠0 nearby; its tangent vectors v satisfy ∇g·v=0. Two nonzero vectors perpendicular to the same one-dimensional tangent line are parallel. A regularity condition such as ∇g(x*)≠0 is essential for the elementary multiplier theorem.

The closest point on a line

Worked example. Minimize f(x,y)=x²+y² subject to g(x,y)=x+y−4=0. The objective is squared distance from the origin, so the closest point should lie where the perpendicular from the origin meets the line. Gradients are ∇f=(2x,2y) and ∇g=(1,1). Setting ∇f=λ∇g gives 2x=λ and 2y=λ, hence x=y. The constraint makes x=y=2 and λ=4. The objective is 8.

This candidate is global: write y=4−x, then f(x,4−x)=x²+(4−x)²=2(x−2)²+8≥8. The square completion supplies the certificate; the multiplier equations alone did not. The line is unbounded, but the squared distance grows away from (2,2), so an optimum does exist.

Why the gradients align

The central necessary condition is ∇f(x*)=λ∇g(x*),g(x*)=0

Precise scope. If f and g are continuously differentiable near an interior constrained local optimum x* in ℝ², and ∇g(x*)≠0, then some real λ satisfies the equation. The regularity condition allows a smooth local parametrization of the constraint with a nonzero tangent v. Along that curve, f has a one-variable local optimum, so its tangential derivative ∇f·v is zero. Since ∇g·v is also zero and in the plane both gradients lie in the one-dimensional normal space, they are parallel. This invokes the elementary local-curve theorem under the stated regularity; it is not a claim for arbitrary singular constraint sets.

The sign convention is flexible for equality constraints. If one writes L=f−λg, stationarity is ∇f−λ∇g=0. If one writes L=f+μg, then μ=−λ. By contrast, inequality constraints require feasibility, nonnegative multipliers under an agreed ≤0 convention, and complementarity; those additional conditions appear in KKT.

Failure case: minimize f(x)=x subject to g(x)=x²=0. The only feasible point, x=0, is certainly both minimum and maximum. But g′(0)=0 while f′(0)=1, so f′(0)=λg′(0) has no solution. Regularity fails, exactly where the theorem said it must hold. Another caution: on the unit circle g(x,y)=x²+y²−1=0, let f(x,y)=x. Then ∇f=(1,0) and ∇g=(2x,2y). The multiplier equations force y=0 because λ cannot be zero, and feasibility gives (1,0) or (−1,0). At (1,0), λ=1/2 and f=1, the global maximum of x on the circle. At (−1,0), λ=−1/2 and f=−1, the global minimum. Equality multipliers may have either sign. Solving the equations without comparing values would leave both candidates unclassified.

Classify, then certify

1. Calculation. Minimize x²+4y² subject to x+y=3. Find x,y and the multiplier for L=f−λ(x+y−3).

Hint for question 1

Stationarity says 2x=λ and 8y=λ; then use x+y=3.

Full solution for question 1

From 2x=8y, x=4y. Thus 5y=3, so y=3/5 and x=12/5; λ=24/5. Substitute x=3−y: f=(3−y)²+4y²=5(y−3/5)²+36/5. Hence 36/5 is the unique global minimum on the entire line.

2. Hypothesis check. Let f(x)=x and constrain x by x²=0. Why is rejecting x=0 because no multiplier exists wrong?

Hint for question 2

Describe the feasible set before writing stationarity.

Full solution for question 2

The feasible set is the singleton {0}; x=0 is automatically its global minimizer. The multiplier equation would demand 1=λ·0, impossible for any λ. This is no contradiction because the constraint gradient is zero at 0. The necessary-condition theorem explicitly requires a nonzero constraint gradient.

A candidate generator with a visible limit

At a regular equality-constrained optimum, objective and constraint gradients align. That alignment has no built-in minimum test and can fail to describe optima on singular constraints. Always substitute back, compare candidates and use a separate certificate when possible. For straight-edged feasible regions, linear programs offer a different finite candidate method.
