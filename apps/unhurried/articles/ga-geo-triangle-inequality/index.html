Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

Can a detour beat a straight route?

Put two displacement arrows head to tail. Their combined displacement goes directly from the first tail to the second head. The triangle inequality says the direct distance cannot exceed the total distance walked along the two arrows. This is a statement about every pair of Euclidean plane vectors, including arrows pointing backward or arrows of length zero.

Background capsule. A vector u = (a,b) has Euclidean length ||u|| = √(a²+b²). Addition means (a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d). The dot product u·v=ac+bd satisfies u·u=||u||². The familiar distance between points P and Q is the length of the displacement Q−P. We will derive the one inequality about dot products needed in the proof, so no previous vector theorem is required.

An exact three-four-five detour

Worked example. Start at (0,0), travel along u=(3,0) to (3,0), then along v=(0,4) to (3,4). The two legs have lengths 3 and 4; the direct displacement u+v=(3,4) has length 5. Thus 5≤3+4. If instead v=(2,0), both legs point the same way: the direct displacement has length 5, exactly the sum 3+2. If v=(−2,0), walking back leaves a direct displacement of length 1 although the walk totals 5.

The table is exact, yet three cases cannot prove a universal claim. It does suggest when equality occurs: arrows in the same direction waste no distance. The next argument turns that observation into a proof for arbitrary real coordinates.

A lower bound from the same inequality

The inequality also limits how much two lengths can differ. Write u=(u−v)+v. The proved result gives ||u||≤||u−v||+||v||, so ||u||−||v||≤||u−v||. Interchanging u and v gives the opposite difference bounded by the same distance. Together these say | ||u||−||v|| |≤||u−v||. This is the reverse triangle inequality, derived from the original one rather than a new assumption.

For u=(3,4) and v=(3,0), the lengths are 5 and 3, while u−v=(0,4) has length 4. Their difference 2 is indeed at most 4. The bound does not assert equality: two points can have nearly equal distances from the origin and still be far apart. For u=(5,0) and v=(−5,0), their lengths agree exactly, but their separation is 10. Remember which pair of endpoints each norm measures before using a length estimate.

Why every Euclidean pair obeys the bound

Exact statement and full proof. For all u,v in the real coordinate plane,

‖u+v‖≤‖u‖+‖v‖

First prove the needed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in two dimensions. If u=(a,b) and v=(c,d), expand (ad−bc)²≥0. Rearranging gives (ac+bd)²≤(a²+b²)(c²+d²), hence |u·v|≤||u||||v|| after taking nonnegative square roots. This derivation includes zero vectors; no division by a length has been used.

Now expand ||u+v||²=(u+v)·(u+v)=||u||²+2u·v+||v||². Since u·v≤||u||||v||, this is at most (||u||+||v||)². Both sides whose square roots we take are nonnegative, proving the displayed inequality. This is a complete coordinate proof, not an appeal to how straight a drawing looks.

When are the two sides equal? The only potentially strict step is u·v≤||u||||v||. For nonzero arrows, equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz calculation requires ad−bc=0, so the arrows are collinear; the positive dot product then says they point the same way. Equivalently v=tu for some t≥0. Equality also holds if either arrow is zero. Oppositely directed nonzero arrows are collinear but generally make a strict inequality: direction matters as much as alignment.

Check the route, then test a claim

1. Calculation. Let u=(1,2), v=(2,1). Find the direct length, total walked length, and the slack in the inequality.

Hint for question 1

Add coordinates before taking the direct length. Keep radicals exact.

Full solution for question 1

u+v=(3,3), so ||u+v||=√18=3√2. Each leg has length √5, making the total 2√5. The slack is 2√5−3√2>0, since their squares are 20 and 18 and both lengths are positive. Equality fails because (1,2) and (2,1) are not positive multiples.

2. Explanation. Someone claims that collinear arrows always give equality. Give a counterexample and identify the missing condition.

Hint for question 2

Try a pair pointing in opposite directions.

Full solution for question 2

Take u=(3,0), v=(−2,0). They are collinear, but ||u+v||=1 while ||u||+||v||=5. For nonzero arrows equality needs the same direction, meaning a nonnegative scalar multiple, not mere collinearity.

What the bound does and does not say

A two-leg Euclidean walk can tie the straight route only when its nonzero legs point the same way. The proof used a specific length built from coordinate squares; an arbitrary rule called “length” need not satisfy this inequality. For example, define the invented quantity L(a,b)=|a²−b²|. Then L(1,1)=L(1,−1)=0 but L((1,1)+(1,−1))=L(2,0)=4, violating 4≤0. This rule also assigns zero to nonzero arrows, so it is not a distance. To reason about a new geometry, first state what its distances mean. The triangle inequality is a property to establish, not a consequence of drawing a triangle.
