Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Why odd handshake counts cannot stand alone

At a small meeting, each handshake involves two people. If everyone remembers how many hands they shook, could exactly one person report an odd number? This is a graph question once each person is a vertex and each handshake between distinct people is an edge. We assume any pair shakes hands at most once, so the graph is simple and undirected.

Background capsule. A graph is a finite vertex set V together with a set E of unordered pairs of distinct vertices. The degree of v, written deg(v), is the number of edges touching v. An isolated vertex has degree zero. A finite sum such as Σ deg(v) adds the degrees for every v in V exactly once. We will count the same collection of edge ends in two ways. Neither a picture nor a real party is required for the proof.

Use a four-person example: AB, AC, BC, and CD occur. The degree counts are A:2, B:2, C:3, D:1. Their sum is 8, while there are 4 edges. The two odd counts are C and D. Is this ratio and parity pattern forced in every finite simple graph?

Count endpoint tokens

Worked example. Give one token to each endpoint of an edge. AB deposits one at A and one at B; AC deposits one at A and one at C; BC deposits one at B and one at C; CD deposits one at C and one at D. The token piles are 2,2,3,1, exactly the degrees. Counting by piles gives 2+2+3+1=8. Counting by the four edge deliveries gives 4·2=8. This worked example makes the counting mechanism visible without relying on a diagram.

Try adding an isolated person E. The edge count stays four and the degree sum gains zero. Try adding edge AD instead. Both A and D gain one degree, so the degree sum gains two. The pattern survives both changes for a reason we can state exactly.

The handshaking identity

Theorem. Every finite simple undirected graph G=(V,E) satisfies

∑v∈Vdeg(v)=2|E|.

Complete proof. Count ordered incidences (v,e) in which vertex v is an endpoint of edge e. Fixing v first yields deg(v) incidences, and summing over all vertices gives the left side. Fixing e first yields precisely two incidences because a simple edge has two distinct endpoints. There are |E| edges, giving the right side. Both procedures count the identical finite set of incidences, so the totals agree. This proof does not require connectedness.

Parity consequence. The number of odd-degree vertices is even. Degrees at even-degree vertices sum to an even number; the complete sum 2|E| is even. A sum of an odd number of odd integers would be odd, because pairing odd summands makes even sums and leaves one odd summand. Therefore the odd-degree summands must occur in an even number. In the four-person example, C and D form such a pair.

There is an important failure of the converse. Knowing that the number of odd-degree vertices is even does not determine the edges, or even whether the graph is connected. Two disjoint triangles have six vertices of degree two and no odd-degree vertices, yet there is no route from one triangle to the other. Also, if a problem records a loop, the simple-graph convention is broken. In the usual multigraph convention a loop contributes two to degree, and the handshaking identity still holds; counting a loop as one degree while keeping the same formula would be inconsistent. State the convention before applying it.

Audit two degree reports

Exercise 1. A simple graph has six vertices with degrees 3,2,2,1,1,1. How many edges does it have? Does the parity consequence object to the list?

Hint for exercise 1

Add all six degrees and divide by two. Count how many of the six entries are odd.

Full solution to exercise 1

The degree sum is 3+2+2+1+1+1=10, so the handshaking identity gives |E|=10/2=5. Four entries are odd: 3 and the three 1s. Four is even, so parity does not rule the list out. Indeed a graph with edges AB, AC, AD, BE, CF has degrees 3,2,2,1,1,1 when the vertices are A,B,C,D,E,F respectively.

Exercise 2. Someone claims a network of five people has degree counts 4,3,2,2,0. Can these be degrees of a simple undirected graph? Explain without trying every edge set.

Hint for exercise 2

Use parity first. Then consider what a vertex of degree four means among five vertices.

Full solution to exercise 2

The sum is 11, which is odd, while every edge contributes two. Therefore the list is impossible. There is a second contradiction: degree four means that person is adjacent to all other four, including the supposedly isolated degree-zero person. Either reason is sufficient. The degree-sum test is necessary, not a guarantee that every even-sum list can be built.

Use parity as a quick necessary test

Every edge has two ends, so the total degree is twice the edge count and the odd-degree vertices come in an even number. These are exact results for finite undirected graphs under a consistent degree convention. They can reject an impossible report quickly; they do not reconstruct a graph from degrees alone. The same local counting will help decide when an edge-by-edge route is possible in Euler trails.
