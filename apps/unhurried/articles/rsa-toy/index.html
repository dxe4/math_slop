A small public lock made of congruences

Suppose you publish two numbers, n=55 and e=3, and keep a third number d=27 private. Someone encodes the message number 7 by computing 7³ modulo 55. You then raise that result to the 27th power modulo 55 and recover 7. This is a toy RSA calculation: its tiny factors are obvious, so it illustrates arithmetic only. It gives no security guarantee.

Here is the necessary local background. The primes p=5 and q=11 give n=pq=55 and φ=(p−1)(q−1)=40. We choose e=3 coprime to 40; d=27 satisfies ed=81=1+2·40. Congruence x ≡ y (mod n) means n divides x−y. Fermat's little theorem says that if p is prime and p does not divide a, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). We will use this theorem and the elementary fact that if coprime 5 and 11 both divide a difference, their product 55 divides it. The proof below is explicitly for messages coprime to 55.

Follow one complete round-trip

Worked example. The published operation is c ≡ me (mod n). For m=7, c=7³=343 ≡ 13 (mod 55), because 343=6·55+13. The private operation gives cd ≡ 1327 (mod 55). Repeated squaring keeps that manageable. Powers of 13 with exponents 1,2,4,8,16 have residues 13,4,16,36,31 modulo 55. Since 27=16+8+2+1, multiply 31·36·4·13 modulo 55. In stages 31·36=1116 ≡ 16; 16·4=64 ≡ 9; 9·13=117 ≡ 7. The recovered residue is the original 7.

The trace checks one message. Correctness for every message in the stated class needs a theorem, and the exponent equation alone does not prove it without relating 40 to the two prime moduli.

Why the chosen messages return

Scoped RSA correctness claim. Let p and q be distinct primes, n=pq, φ=(p−1)(q−1), and choose positive e,d with ed ≡ 1 (mod φ). For every integer m with gcd(m,n)=1, decrypting the encoded residue yields m modulo n: (me)d ≡ m (mod n). Write ed=1+kφ. Because m is coprime to n, neither p nor q divides m. Fermat's theorem gives mp−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Since φ is a multiple of p−1, mkφ ≡ 1 (mod p), and therefore med ≡ m (mod p). The same reasoning with q−1 gives med ≡ m (mod q). The two distinct primes are coprime, so their product n divides med−m. This completes the proof for coprime messages.

med≡m(modpq)

The exact proof boundary matters. A stronger theorem handles messages sharing a factor with n by considering the zero case separately modulo each prime, but we have not established that extension here. Nor have we shown why a real implementation hides its factors, resists chosen-message attacks, pads messages, or manages keys. Those are engineering and cryptographic questions beyond this numerical model. Even within the toy, if e is not coprime to φ, a modular inverse d may not exist. For example e=2 has gcd(2,40)=2, so no integer d makes 2d ≡ 1 (mod 40).

Failure case. Using the wrong private exponent breaks the round-trip. If d=3 instead of 27, then ed=9 is not 1 modulo 40. For m=7, the encoded residue remains 13, but 13³=2197 ≡ 52 (mod 55), not 7. The exponent congruence is a real condition, not a decorative key label.

Test the key condition and the computation

1. With the same key (n,e,d)=(55,3,27), encode m=2. Explain why the correctness proof applies, and give the recovered residue without evaluating a 27th power directly.

Hint for question 1

First calculate 2³ modulo 55. Then check gcd(2,55) and the relation 3·27=1+2·40.

Full solution to question 1

The encoded residue is c=2³=8. Since gcd(2,55)=1 and 3·27=81=1+2·40, the scoped theorem applies: c27 ≡ 281 ≡ 2 (mod 55). For a direct modular check, 8² ≡ 9, 8⁴ ≡ 26, 8⁸ ≡ 16, and 8¹⁶ ≡ 36. Multiplying the residues for 27=16+8+2+1 gives 36·16·9·8 ≡ 2 (mod 55). The theorem and the trace agree.

2. A proposed public exponent is e=6 for n=55. Can it use the same construction with φ=40? Explain precisely, without claiming that every other exponent fails.

Hint for question 2

Look for an integer d with 6d−40k=1 and test the gcd of 6 and 40.

Full solution to question 2

gcd(6,40)=2. Every integer of the form 6d−40k is even, so it cannot equal 1. Thus 6 has no inverse modulo 40, and this key-construction rule cannot supply a d for e=6. This says nothing against choices such as e=3, which do have an inverse; it identifies the exact obstruction for the proposed exponent.

Arithmetic correctness is a narrow promise

For coprime messages, the relation ed ≡ 1 modulo (p−1)(q−1), Fermat's theorem at each prime, and divisibility by both primes explain the round-trip. The small numbers let you inspect every step, including a bad exponent. They do not demonstrate secrecy. The satisfying stopping point is the exact correctness proof for this stated class of messages; real RSA requires additional mathematical and implementation safeguards.
