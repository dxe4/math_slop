Why do two steady tones seem to pulse?

Imagine two clean sine-wave tones, one at 440 cycles per second and one at 444. Each alone has steady amplitude. Add them and the result grows and shrinks in a repeating pattern. A mathematical identity predicts that pattern exactly for the stipulated signals. Whether a listener hears a particular beat depends on the sound source, room and hearing, so the algebra and the perceptual claim must be kept separate.

Background capsule. Frequency f in hertz (Hz) means cycles per second. A sine wave sin(2πft) repeats after 1/f seconds because its angle advances 2π radians in one cycle. A radian is a unitless angle measure; π is approximately 3.14159. The identity sin(A)+sin(B)=2sin((A+B)/2)cos((A−B)/2) follows from the sine addition formulas. We need that identity and the idea that waves add point by point.

Assume equal amplitudes, zero initial phases, frequencies f₁ and f₂ measured in Hz, and time t in seconds. Superposition means the displacement or ideal signal at time t is y(t)=sin(2πf₁t)+sin(2πf₂t). No instrument timbre or phase drift is included.

Follow the slow factor

Worked example. For 440 Hz and 444 Hz, the identity gives y(t)=2sin(884πt)cos(4πt). The first factor has average frequency 442 Hz: it changes very rapidly. The cosine factor changes slowly, from +1 through zero to −1 and back. Its signed period is 0.5 seconds because cos(4π(t+0.5))=cos(4πt). But loudness follows the magnitude of that factor: |cos(4πt)| has peaks every 0.25 seconds. Thus the familiar beat-peak rate is 4 per second, the 4 Hz difference.

The table describes the envelope, not the instantaneous signal. At t=0, both sine terms are actually zero even though the envelope has its maximum possible magnitude. Sampling the carrier at t=0 alone would therefore not reveal the peak. This distinction helps interpret waveform pictures: envelope bounds the rapid oscillation.

Check another time scale. If the second frequency moves from 444 Hz to 442 Hz while the first stays at 440 Hz, the difference becomes 2 Hz. Envelope peaks are now half a second apart and the first cancellation occurs after a quarter second. The signed cosine takes a full second to repeat. Changing the difference changes the slow pattern even though the carrier remains near 440 Hz. This is a direct consequence of the same identity, with time still measured in seconds.

Identity, envelope and limits

Applying the addition identity with A=2πf₁t and B=2πf₂t yields

sin(2πf1t)+sin(2πf2t)=2sin(π(f1+f2)t)cos(π(f1−f2)t)

This is an exact identity for all real t and frequencies. For positive difference Δf=|f₁−f₂|, |cos(πΔft)| peaks whenever πΔft is an integer multiple of π, so successive peaks are 1/Δf seconds apart. The signed cosine itself takes 2/Δf seconds to return to the same sign. Calling Δf the beat frequency refers to peaks in the magnitude envelope, not the signed modulation frequency.

If amplitudes differ, A sin(2πf₁t)+B sin(2πf₂t) need not cancel completely. A useful squared envelope is A²+B²+2AB cos(2πΔft), which has minimum magnitude |A−B|. Our zero troughs therefore rely on equal amplitude. Real sound also has harmonics, reverberation and auditory effects; the ideal signal is a controlled mathematical model, not a prediction of perceived loudness for every listener.

Compute and question

1. Calculate. Two equal-amplitude waves have frequencies 300 Hz and 305 Hz. Give the average carrier frequency, beat-peak rate, time between envelope peaks and first cancellation time after t=0.

Hint for question 1

Use Δf=5 Hz and find when |cos(πΔft)| first reaches zero.

Full solution to question 1

The average frequency is (300+305)/2=302.5 Hz. Difference Δf=5 Hz, so beat peaks occur 5 times per second, separated by 1/5=0.2 s. First cancellation occurs when π(5)t=π/2, giving t=0.1 s. The carrier is rapid while this envelope changes slowly.

2. Find the failed assumption. Two waves have equal frequencies of 400 Hz but amplitudes 1 and 0.6. Will they produce periodic complete silence under the zero-phase model?

Hint for question 2

Combine the sine terms directly when their frequencies and phases agree.

Full solution to question 2

The sum is 1.6sin(800πt), a single 400 Hz tone with constant amplitude 1.6. There is no slow beat because Δf=0. The signal crosses zero each carrier half-cycle, but that is not periodic cancellation of an envelope. If the frequencies differed, unequal amplitudes would leave envelope minimum |1−0.6|=0.4 rather than silence.

Keep the two meanings of period apart

Superposition and a trigonometric identity give an exact envelope for ideal equal-amplitude sine waves. Its magnitude peaks at the frequency difference; its signed cosine needs twice as long to return to the same sign. A waveform image or optional sound can illustrate this, but the algebra carries the claim. For a related model of oscillation with a physical approximation, visit the pendulum sketch.
