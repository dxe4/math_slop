A cusp and a crossing share a warning sign

At the origin, both y²=x³ and y²=x²(x+1) have zero gradient. Yet their nearby real shapes differ: one has two half-branches meeting in a cusp, while the other has branches crossing. We will distinguish them by solving their equations near zero and by looking at their lowest-degree terms.

Background capsule. A singular point of a chosen reduced plane-curve equation F=0 is a zero where Fx=Fy=0. A branch is a local route of solutions toward the point. The tangent cone records directions selected by the lowest nonzero homogeneous-degree terms of F. A repeated direction in that cone can signal branches sharing a tangent; it does not alone classify every singularity. We work over real coordinates for the branch pictures, with x+1 positive near x=0.

Compute the branches

Worked example. For the cusp F=y²−x³, real solutions require x≥0 because y²≥0. For x>0, y=±x√x. Both signs approach the origin from the right. Their slopes relative to the origin are y/x=±√x, which tend to zero. Thus both half-branches have the x-axis as limiting tangent direction. A convenient parametrization is (x,y)=(t²,t³); positive and negative t describe the two halves. Its derivative vanishes at t=0, another sign that ordinary regular-curve parametrization needs care.

For the node G=y²−x²(x+1), take |x| small enough that x+1>0. Then y=±x√(1+x), where using x rather than |x| simply swaps the two signs for negative x. The two parametrized branches pass through zero and have slopes y/x=±√(1+x)→±1. Their limiting tangents are y=x and y=−x. Each branch continues to both sides of the origin, unlike the cusp's two right-hand halves.

What the algebra establishes

Singular-point check. For the cusp, Fx=−3x² and Fy=2y. For the node, Gx=−2x−3x² and Gy=2y. Both equations and both derivative pairs vanish at (0,0):

F(p)=Fx(p)=Fy(p)=0

This condition says the smooth tangent formula cannot be used at the point. It does not distinguish cusp from node. To obtain the first tangent directions, separate each polynomial by total degree: x³ is degree three, while y² is degree two, so the cusp's degree-two part is y². For the node, expanding x²(x+1)=x²+x³ gives degree-two part y²−x²=(y−x)(y+x). A direction (u,v) in the tangent cone satisfies that lowest-degree equation: v²=0 for the cusp, or (v−u)(v+u)=0 for the node. The repeated cusp line and two distinct node lines match the explicit branch limits calculated above.

Proof boundary. The formulas y=±x√x and y=±x√(1+x) fully establish the real local branch and tangent claims for these two polynomials. We have not proved a general theorem that every factor of a tangent cone corresponds to a separate real branch. Indeed, y²+x² has degree-two tangent expression y²+x² but only the origin as a real zero; over complex numbers it factors into two lines. The field matters, and higher-degree terms can change local behavior. A complete singularity classification uses tools beyond this lesson.

Also beware a repeated defining equation. The zero set of (y−x²)² is a smooth parabola even though its gradient vanishes all along it. Thus “gradient zero” is an algebraic test for the chosen equation, not an automatic proof that the visible set has a cusp. In algebraic geometry one often first removes repeated factors, or treats the repeated structure deliberately. The next algebraic viewpoint appears in coordinate rings.

Distinguish local directions

1. Calculation. For H=y²−4x²−x³, check the origin and find the two limiting real tangent lines near it.

Hint for question 1

Factor the equation as y²=x²(4+x), which has a positive square root near zero.

Full solution for question 1

H(0,0)=0, Hx=−8x−3x² and Hy=2y, so both derivatives vanish at zero. For |x| small, y=±x√(4+x) parameterizes two branches. Dividing by x for x≠0 and taking x→0 gives slopes ±2. The tangent lines are y=2x and y=−2x; the lowest-degree part y²−4x² gives the same result.

2. Explanation. Does the zero gradient of K=(y−x)² at (0,0) prove that its real zero set crosses itself?

Hint for question 2

Solve K=0 before interpreting its derivative.

Full solution for question 2

No. K=0 is equivalent to y−x=0, so its real zero set is a single straight line. Its gradient is 2(y−x)(−1,1), hence zero on that line because the polynomial repeats the factor. There is no crossing; the algebraic equation has repeated structure.

Read singularities locally

The cusp and node both defeat the nonzero-gradient rule, but explicit branch equations reveal different limiting directions. For these examples, the lowest-degree part gives the tangent cone and the branch formulas justify the real picture. Stop once you can state which part is an exact local calculation and which broader classification remains unproved.
