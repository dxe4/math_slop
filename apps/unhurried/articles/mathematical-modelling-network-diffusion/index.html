Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

How can a marker travel through a drawn network?

Draw four fictional people A, B, C and D as vertices and connections A–B, A–C and C–D as edges. Put an active marker on A. We can stipulate a one-step spreading rule and calculate exactly what it predicts. The drawing is a toy mechanism for comparing structures; its probabilities are not measured facts about friendship or rumour sharing.

Background capsule. A graph is a set of vertices with edges connecting chosen pairs. A neighbour of v is a vertex joined to v by an edge. A probability p is between 0 and 1; chance of failure for an event with chance p is 1−p. If events are independent, the chance they all fail is the product of their individual failure chances. Conditional independence means this multiplication holds after the present active set and edge probabilities are fixed.

Use a synchronous rule: in each step, every active vertex tries independently to activate each currently inactive neighbour once; an activation takes effect at the next step, and active vertices stay active. Set every edge attempt probability to p=1/2. These timing and independence choices define the model.

Work through a small graph

Worked example. At time 0, only A is active. At time 1, A tries B and C separately. The four patterns (neither, B only, C only, both) each have probability 1/4 because the two Bernoulli attempts are independent. D cannot activate at time 1: it is connected to C, which only becomes active at the end of the step. This is a useful timing check against an implementation that accidentally allows within-step cascades.

The expected total active after one step is (1+2+2+3)/4=2. A single simulated run can yield any of the four totals; it is an illustration of the rule, not a forecast. At the next step, the attempts depend on which vertices activated at time 1. To compute probabilities correctly, condition on that current state rather than pretending all second-step trials occur in the same scenario.

The product rule and its hypothesis

Let Iₜ be the active set at the start of step t. For an inactive vertex v, let Aₜ(v) be its active neighbours. If each u in Aₜ(v) independently makes a Bernoulli attempt with probability pᵤᵥ, then

P(v stays inactive at step t+1|It)=∏u∈At(v)(1−puv)

The product over an empty set is 1: if no active neighbour can try, v certainly stays inactive under this rule. If two active neighbours each have p=1/2, v avoids both attempts with probability (1/2)²=1/4, so it activates with probability 3/4. That conclusion is exact only given the conditional independence assumption. If one shared event makes both people forward or both remain silent, their failures are correlated; multiplying two marginals can be wrong.

Also, the formula concerns one step given Iₜ. It does not say eventual activation probabilities equal a simple product. Loops and repeated opportunities change those questions. A simulation with fixed seed and finite repetitions estimates the behaviour of this specified toy process; it cannot validate the social assumptions or establish causality.

Check the mechanism

1. Calculate. An inactive vertex has two active neighbours. Their independent attempt probabilities are 0.2 and 0.4. Find its chance of staying inactive and of activating next step.

Hint for question 1

Both attempts must fail for the vertex to stay inactive.

Full solution to question 1

The two failure chances are 0.8 and 0.6. Their independent product is 0.8×0.6=0.48, so the activation chance is 1−0.48=0.52. The result lies between 0.4 and 0.6, as expected when a second opportunity is added but neither opportunity is certain.

2. Counterexample. Two active neighbours always act together: with probability 1/2 both send a marker, otherwise neither does. Each marginal attempt chance is 1/2. What is the true chance the target stays inactive, and what would the independent formula incorrectly give?

Hint for question 2

List the only two possible joint outcomes.

Full solution to question 2

Neither sends with probability 1/2, so the target stays inactive with probability 1/2. Multiplying marginal failure chances would give (1/2)(1/2)=1/4, which is wrong because failures occur together. This directly identifies conditional independence as a necessary hypothesis for the product formula.

What the network model can say

An explicit graph, clock and attempt rule produce checkable one-step probabilities. Structural comparisons can then be made inside that rule, with independent attempts clearly named. The same picture under a different timing or correlated-attempt rule can produce different results. Stress-testing these assumptions is the job of model validation, and it still cannot turn a fictional graph into a real social forecast.
