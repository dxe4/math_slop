Which integers may count as zero?

Modulo n arithmetic declares every multiple of n equivalent to zero. The multiples are not just a subgroup under addition: multiplying any of them by any integer still produces a multiple of n. That absorbing property is what makes an ideal fit a quotient ring, where both addition and multiplication must survive.

Background capsule. A commutative ring has addition, multiplication and distributivity. In Z the usual integer operations make a ring. An ideal I⊆Z is an additive subgroup with the extra rule that ri∈I whenever r∈Z and i∈I. Write nZ={nk:k∈Z}; the notation (n) means the ideal generated by n and equals nZ. Cosets a+I collect numbers differing by an element of I. The well-ordering principle says every nonempty set of positive integers has a least member.

Collapse the multiples of four

Worked example. Let I=4Z. Then 7+I=3+I because 7−3=4 belongs to I. Adding cosets gives (7+I)+(2+I)=9+I=1+I. Multiplying gives (7+I)(2+I)=14+I=2+I. If 7 is replaced by 3, the product becomes 3·2=6, which is still 2+I. The difference 14−6=8 belongs to I because it is a multiple of four.

The sample table supports the rule in this example, but the general calculation is stronger. If a′=a+i and b′=b+j with i,j∈I, then a′b′−ab=aj+bi+ij. Each term lies in I by absorption, so changing representatives cannot change the product coset. Addition is even simpler: (a+i)+(b+j) differs from a+b by i+j∈I.

Every integer ideal has one generator

Z/nZ=Z/(n)

Exact statement and proof. Every ideal I of Z equals nZ for a unique integer n≥0. If I={0}, take n=0; this separate case matters because it has no positive element. Otherwise some nonzero a belongs to I, and its opposite −a also belongs, so I contains a positive integer. Let n be the least positive element of I. Closure under multiplication by arbitrary integers gives nZ⊆I. For any a∈I, integer division gives a=qn+r with 0≤r<n. Since qn∈I and I is an additive subgroup, r=a−qn∈I. Minimality of n forces r=0. Thus a∈nZ and I⊆nZ. Equality follows. Uniqueness follows because a nonzero nZ has n as its least positive member; 0Z alone has no positive member.

For n≥2, the quotient ring Z/(n) consists of n cosets and uses [a]+[b]=[a+b] and [a][b]=[ab]. The representative calculation above proves both are well-defined. The zero coset is (n), and the multiplicative identity is 1+(n). When n=0, the quotient Z/(0) is just a copy of Z; when n=1 it has one element, so 0=1. The latter is a valid zero ring under some conventions but not a field. Naming these boundary cases prevents a false claim that every quotient ring has n distinct residues without restricting n.

We proved a special classification for Z using integer division and well-ordering. It does not transfer unchanged to every ring. For instance the ideal (x,y) in the polynomial ring R[x,y] cannot be generated by a single polynomial when R is a field: any common generator would divide both x and y, so would be a nonzero constant and produce the whole ring, yet every polynomial in (x,y) has zero constant term. The point is that ideals encode relations, not that every ideal has one generator.

Find the generator and test absorption

1. Calculation. Let I be an ideal of Z containing 18 and 30. What must I contain? If I is generated by just these two integers, what is I?

Hint for question 1

Subtract integer multiples: 30−18=12 and 18−12=6. Check that both original generators are multiples of 6.

Full solution for question 1

Every ideal containing 18 and 30 contains 30−18=12 and 18−12=6, then all multiples of 6 by absorption. The ideal generated by 18 and 30 is all integer combinations 18u+30v. Every such combination is divisible by 6, while 6=2·18−30 belongs to it. Hence the generated ideal is exactly 6Z. A larger ideal containing those integers could also exist, such as 3Z.

2. Explanation. The even integers are an ideal of Z. Are the odd integers an ideal? Identify the first failed requirement.

Hint for question 2

An additive subgroup must contain zero and be closed under addition.

Full solution for question 2

The odd integers do not contain zero, and 1+1=2 is not odd. They are therefore not an additive subgroup, so they cannot be an ideal. They also fail absorption: 2·1=2 is even. An ideal is more than a selected residue class; it is the set of elements treated as zero in a quotient.

Relations as an ideal

Every ideal of Z is nZ, including the zero ideal. The ideal property makes multiplication on cosets independent of representatives, giving the quotient ring Z/(n). The classification proof rests on least positive elements and integer division, so it is specific to integers. A coset such as 1+2Z is not an ideal: it is one block of the quotient, and it does not contain zero.

The next question is when a nonzero coset has a multiplicative inverse. Prime moduli make every nonzero coset invertible; composite moduli create a zero-divisor obstruction.
