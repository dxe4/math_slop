Powers and logarithms

For a positive integer n, aⁿ means a multiplied by itself n times. For example, 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. For a nonzero base, a⁰=1 and a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. A negative exponent describes a reciprocal.

Worked example. With base 3, the logarithm asks which exponent produces a positive input. Since 3⁴=81, log₃(81)=4. More generally, for a positive base b different from 1, log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with x positive.

34=81log3(81)=4

Multiplying powers with the same base adds their exponents: 2³×2²=2⁵. For positive inputs, this becomes the logarithm rule log₂(uv)=log₂(u)+log₂(v). It is a rule about a product.

Try one. Find log₂(1/8) and explain the negative sign.

Worked answer

Because 2⁻³=1/2³=1/8, the logarithm is −3. For a base larger than 1, inputs between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms.

Watch the operation. Logarithms do not generally turn a sum into a sum: log₂(2+2)=2, while log₂(2)+log₂(2)=2 happens to agree in this case. Using 2+4 instead gives log₂(6), which differs from 1+2=3 because 2³=8.

What grows when a miniature gallery grows?

A designer doubles every length in a scale drawing of a cubic display booth. The floor and walls look twice as wide, yet paint and enclosed air do not merely double. The distinction matters when a sketch becomes a materials estimate. We will derive the different scaling rules from multiplication of dimensions and use them to catch a common budgeting mistake.

Background capsule. A scale factor s means every corresponding length in a similar shape becomes s times its old length. Area multiplies two lengths; volume multiplies three. Squaring s means s × s, and cubing means s × s × s. A square metre is an area unit, a cubic metre a volume unit. Similarity is the key assumption: if only one direction changes, these powers need adjustment.

Imagine a 1 m by 1 m by 1 m open-front cube. Its back and four side panels are each 1 m², so painted interior area is 5 m². Its enclosed box volume is 1 m³, ignoring the open front for this volume calculation. The labels “paint area” and “volume” answer different material questions.

Double, then compare

Worked example. Scale every dimension of the booth by s = 2. Each panel is now 2 m by 2 m and has area 4 m². Five such panels need 20 m² of paint area, four times the original 5 m². The box dimensions are 2 m by 2 m by 2 m, so volume is 8 m³, eight times the original. No calculus is needed: two or three factors of 2 explain the answer.

Suppose paint covers 10 m² per litre. At the original size the ideal amount is 5/10 = 0.5 L; at double size it is 20/10 = 2 L. Packaging and coats could change a purchase order, but the area calculation is the relevant starting quantity. Multiplying the original paint by eight would confuse volume with painted surface.

Derive the powers of scale

For a rectangular object with lengths L, W and H, scaling all three by the same positive dimensionless s produces sL, sW and sH. The area of a corresponding rectangular face changes from LW to (sL)(sW) = s²LW. The volume changes from LWH to (sL)(sW)(sH) = s³LWH. Therefore, for similar shapes whose relevant pieces all follow that scale,

Lnew=sLold,Anew=s2Aold,Vnew=s3Vold

These are exact consequences of the geometric scaling rule. They do not assert that every practical quantity scales this way. Paint thickness, door sizes, worker hours and structural support may stay fixed or follow other rules. Nor does a room with only its width doubled become a similar room. If length and height remain 1 m while width becomes 2 m, the floor doubles and volume doubles, rather than growing by factors of four and eight.

The units provide another check. A unitless s² multiplies an old area in m² and leaves the result in m². Applying s³ to paint area gives a number in m² but a physically unjustified factor; unit consistency is necessary, not sufficient, for a good model. We also need the right geometry and purpose.

Scale a new display

1. Calculate. A similar sculpture has original height 0.8 m, surface area 3 m² and volume 0.4 m³. Its new height is 1.2 m. Find the scale factor, new area and new volume.

Hint for question 1

Divide the heights first, then use its square and cube.

Full solution to question 1

The factor is 1.2/0.8 = 1.5. The area factor is 1.5² = 2.25, giving 3 × 2.25 = 6.75 m². The volume factor is 1.5³ = 3.375, giving 0.4 × 3.375 = 1.35 m³. The outputs retain their original area and volume units.

2. Challenge the rule. A rectangular wall is 4 m wide and 3 m high. Only its width grows to 6 m. Does the old area get multiplied by (6/4)²? Compute the actual new area and identify the missing hypothesis.

Hint for question 2

Write area as width times height; check whether both factors changed.

Full solution to question 2

The old area is 4 × 3 = 12 m². The new area is 6 × 3 = 18 m², factor 1.5. Squaring 1.5 would predict 27 m², which is false. Uniform scaling of both width and height is missing. The altered wall is a stretch in one direction, not a similar copy.

Where the shortcut stops

Length, area and volume carry one, two and three scaled dimensions respectively. The proof is simply the product of the dimensions under a common factor. The shortcut fails when different directions scale differently or when the outcome is governed by a separate process. For the mural question, tile count tracks area only if tile size and waste assumptions stay fixed. Sensitivity checks show what happens when one of those assumptions moves.
