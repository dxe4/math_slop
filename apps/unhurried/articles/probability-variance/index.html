The question and needed ideas

Two puzzle machines both average five points. Machine S always awards five. Machine W flips a fair coin and awards zero or ten. Their expected scores agree, but one machine is predictable and the other is not. A single number for the mean hides the difference, so we need a measure of spread around that mean.

Background capsule. Write μ=E[X] for the mean. The deviation from μ is x−μ. Simply averaging deviations always gives zero, because positive and negative deviations cancel. Squaring before averaging avoids cancellation. Variance is the expected squared deviation; standard deviation is its nonnegative square root. Scores may be in points, while variance is in squared points.

Work through the model

Worked example. For S, the only deviation is 5−5=0, so Var(S)=0. For W, the mean is 0(1/2)+10(1/2)=5. Both possible scores differ from five by five; each squared deviation is 25. Thus Var(W)=25(1/2)+25(1/2)=25, and its standard deviation is √25=5 points.

The shortcut gives the same answer. E[W²]=0²(1/2)+10²(1/2)=50, and E[W]²=25; their difference is 25. For S, E[S²]=25 and E[S]²=25, giving zero. The shortcut is useful when scores have many values, but the squared-deviation definition is the meaning to remember.

A table with rows (0, 1/2, 25) and (10, 1/2, 25) for score, probability and squared deviation makes the calculation transparent. Each row contributes 12.5 to W’s variance. Neither table row is a “typical” outcome in the sense of being close to the mean: the mean itself never occurs.

The statement and its limits

Exact statement. For a finite distribution, Var(X)=E[(X−μ)²] with μ=E[X]. Expand the square: E[X²−2μX+μ²]=E[X²]−2μE[X]+μ²E[1]. Since E[X]=μ and E[1]=1, this equals E[X²]−μ². This is an algebraic proof of the shortcut; it does not require a bell curve or repeated trials.

Variance is never negative because every squared deviation is nonnegative and probabilities are nonnegative. It equals zero exactly when all positive-probability outcomes equal the mean. Adding a constant c leaves the deviations unchanged, so Var(X+c)=Var(X). Scaling scores by a multiplies variance by a²: Var(aX)=a²Var(X).

A failure case for “same mean means same game” is S versus W. Also, a large standard deviation does not by itself reveal whether low or high scores are more frequent; different distributions can have the same first two moments. Inspect the underlying probabilities for decisions that depend on tails or worst cases.

Var(X)=E[X2]−E[X]2

To interpret the scale, compare a third machine T that awards four or six points with equal chances. It also has mean five, but each deviation is only one point, so Var(T)=1 and its standard deviation is one point. The ordering S, T, W is now visible: zero, one and five standard-deviation points. Variance amplifies large deviations because it squares them. A score ten points from the mean contributes one hundred squared points before weighting, while a score one point away contributes only one. This sensitivity is useful, but it means variance is not a complete description of a distribution.

Try two cases

Exercise 1. X is equally likely to be 1, 3 or 5. Find its mean, variance and standard deviation.

Hint for exercise 1

The mean is the middle value; square each distance from it.

Full solution to exercise 1

μ=(1+3+5)/3=3. Squared deviations are 4, 0 and 4. Their average is Var(X)=8/3. The standard deviation is √(8/3), about 1.633, in the original score units. The shortcut checks it: E[X²]=(1+9+25)/3=35/3, minus 3²=9 leaves 8/3.

Exercise 2. Define Y=2X+7 for the same X. Calculate Var(Y) and explain why adding seven alone does not increase spread.

Hint for exercise 2

Use deviations from E[Y], or the scaling rule.

Full solution to exercise 2

E[Y]=2·3+7=13. The values of Y are 9, 13 and 17, with deviations −4, 0 and 4, so Var(Y)=(16+0+16)/3=32/3. Equivalently 2²Var(X)=4·8/3=32/3. The constant seven shifts each value and the mean equally; it changes location, not distances from the mean.

What to carry forward

Variance measures average squared distance under the stated probabilities, while standard deviation restores the original unit. The identity proved here is exact for finite distributions. It does not say every high-variance game is undesirable; that is a preference question. For repeated independent trials, binomial counts give a useful case with a compact mean and variance.
