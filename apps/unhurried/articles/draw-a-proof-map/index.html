Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Where does the conclusion come from?

A persuasive argument can quietly use an assumption it never earned. A proof map makes that gap visible. Its boxes hold the given facts, the intermediate claim, and the exact destination; its arrows must carry reasons. We will map a familiar integer claim: if an integer is divisible by 6, then it is even. The destination is deliberately modest, so attention can go to the reasoning rather than difficult arithmetic.

Background capsule. “a divides b” means that b = ak for some integer k. An implication P ⇒ Q promises that whenever P holds, Q holds. To show an integer is even, produce an integer j such that it equals 2j. These are all the ingredients needed here; a proof map is a reading and writing aid, not a formal deduction calculus.

Label every arrow

Worked example. Take any integer n divisible by 6. By the definition of divisibility, n = 6k for some integer k. Rewrite 6k as 2(3k). Because integers are closed under multiplication, 3k is an integer. It is the witness required by the definition of evenness. Thus n is even. The proof has a start, a bridge, and a destination, and each transition names its licence.

The bridge matters more than the picture. If the table merely drew an arrow from “6 divides n” to “even” with no witness, the reader would have to invent the missing argument. A map can also expose a false claim. Try reversing the arrow: 2 is even, but 6 does not divide 2, so “even implies divisible by 6” is false.

Composition of valid implications

Letters can stand for whole claims

Here P, Q, and R name statements, rather than numbers to calculate. Read P ⇒ Q as “if P is true, then Q is true.” For example, P could mean “6 divides n” and R could mean “n is even.” The long arrow in the middle says that the two implications on its left justify the implication on its right. None of these arrows promises a reverse direction.

P⇒Q,Q⇒R⟹P⇒R

Exact claim. For propositions P, Q, and R, two valid implications P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ R give P ⇒ R. To prove it, suppose P holds. The first implication gives Q; the second then gives R. That is the whole logical justification. In the integer example, the first arrow translates divisibility to a witness; the second reshapes that witness into one for evenness.

Proof maps have a boundary. They show dependencies, but they do not make an invalid arrow valid. Every arrow still needs a definition, a prior theorem, or a proved calculation. In particular, the map cannot turn P ⇒ Q into Q ⇒ P. That reversed statement is the converse, and it needs its own proof or counterexample. Our n = 2 example refutes the converse immediately.

Nor may a map change its variable midway without saying so. The original claim concerns any integer divisible by 6. If the calculation were done only for n = 12, it would establish one instance, not the universal theorem. The phrase “take any integer n satisfying the hypothesis” and the symbolic witness keep the proof general.

Audit the arrows

Exercise 1. Map a proof that every integer divisible by 10 is divisible by 5. Name the witness at each step.

Hint for exercise 1

Start with n = 10k, then factor 10 as 5·2.

Full solution to exercise 1

Let n be any integer divisible by 10. Then n = 10k for an integer k. Rewriting gives n = 5(2k). Since 2k is an integer, it witnesses that 5 divides n. The map is hypothesis → integer witness → factorization → conclusion.

Exercise 2. Someone argues, “If a number is divisible by 4, it is even. Therefore every even number is divisible by 4.” Identify the arrow error and give a counterexample.

Hint for exercise 2

Write the given implication and the claimed implication in opposite directions.

Full solution to exercise 2

The given arrow is divisible by 4 ⇒ even, whereas the claim reverses it to even ⇒ divisible by 4. Reversal is not licensed by implication. The integer 2 is even because 2 = 2·1, but 2/4 is not an integer, so 4 does not divide 2. This is a counterexample to the claimed converse.

Keep the arrows honest

A proof map is useful when every node is a precise claim and every arrow has a checkable reason. We completely proved the divisibility example and the general rule for chaining implications; we did not build a formal logic system. If you want to construct the witness in a new setting, the direct-proof lesson makes that technique the main event. If an arrow reverses, test its converse separately.
