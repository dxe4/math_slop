Why is the best-looking result from twenty tries suspicious?

Try twenty patterns in random coin tosses and report the most striking. The question is what the observed numbers allow us to say, and where the tempting shortcut fails.

Background capsule. A valid p-value under a true null is super-uniform: P(p≤t)≤t for every t between 0 and 1. A continuous exact null p-value may be Uniform(0,1), making equality hold. Discrete tests, including many binomial tests, usually have jumps and are typically conservative at some thresholds. A family of tests is the set of questions the analyst committed to treat together.

Worked example

Imagine twenty unrelated patterns in random coin sequences, each tested once. If all twenty nulls are true and each p-value is independent Uniform(0,1), the chance a particular test gives p≤0.05 is 0.05. The chance none does is 0.95^20≈0.3585. So the chance of at least one apparently significant result is 1−0.95^20≈0.6415. A report that names only the smallest p-value hides the twenty chances to find it. The calculation describes a stipulated model, not evidence that any particular selected pattern is false.

For twenty tests, the expected number of p-values at or below 0.05 is 20×0.05=1 under the exact uniform true-null model, even though the probability of at least one is about 0.6415. Expectation and probability of any hit are different summaries of the same experiment.

A dependence example. Suppose the twenty reported tests are actually copies of one exact uniform null p-value. Then either every copy crosses 0.05 or none does. The chance of at least one crossing is exactly 0.05, rather than 0.6415. Each individual p-value still has the same uniform distribution. What changed is their joint dependence. This calculation shows why counting twenty labels does not establish twenty independent opportunities, while the union bound continues to apply.

The claim and its conditions

P(minpi≤α)=1−(1−α)m

Independence multiplies the probabilities of twenty no-hit events. Exact uniformity supplies each factor 1−α. If p-values are independent but merely valid super-uniform, each no-hit chance is at least 1−α, making 1−(1−α)^m an upper bound rather than an equality. Without independence, the union bound still gives P(any false rejection)≤mα for m true nulls and valid p-values. Bonferroni therefore tests each of m prespecified hypotheses at α/m to keep familywise false-rejection probability at most α, without needing independence. This bound can be conservative when tests share data or have discrete p-values.

Proof boundary and failure case. The displayed equality needs m independent continuous Uniform(0,1) true-null p-values. For discrete binomial tails it is generally not exact. Dependence can make the exact familywise rate larger or smaller than that expression, though the union bound remains valid for individually valid tests. Redefining the test family after seeing which results look good defeats a claim that a prespecified correction protected that family.

For dependent tests, write A_i for the event that test i falsely rejects. The union bound P(∪A_i)≤ΣP(A_i) follows without multiplying probabilities. If each valid p-value is compared with 0.05/20=0.0025, the familywise false-rejection probability is at most 0.05. This remains a bound even if several tests are nearly duplicates.

Try two checks

1. Calculation. For five independent exact Uniform null p-values, calculate the probability of at least one value at or below 0.1.

Hint for exercise 1

Subtract the probability that all five exceed 0.1.

Full solution to exercise 1

Each exceeds 0.1 with probability 0.9, so all five do with probability 0.9^5=0.59049. At least one is at or below 0.1 with probability 1−0.59049=0.40951.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. Ten related valid tests use the same data. Give a threshold that ensures familywise error at most 0.05 without an independence assumption, and explain the cost.

Hint for exercise 2

Use the union bound on ten events, each with probability at most its threshold.

Full solution to exercise 2

Bonferroni uses 0.05/10=0.005 for each prespecified test. Under all true nulls, the probability of any false rejection is at most 10×0.005=0.05, regardless of dependence. The bound may be loose, so a genuine effect can be harder to detect than under a more tailored procedure.

In the coin-pattern story, the family must include patterns that were tried but not reported. If the twenty candidate patterns were themselves chosen after exploring data, the effective search could be wider still. The analyst should distinguish prespecified tests from exploration.

What you can carry forward

Count every tested or selected claim in the relevant family. A selected minimum is expected to be small more often than a single test. Distinguish exact independent-uniform calculations from valid upper bounds, and state the selection rule before treating one attractive result as confirmatory.

An individual p-value may be valid for its own predeclared test while the published minimum is a selected statistic with a different distribution. Publishing the tested list, selection rule, and correction lets a reader reconstruct the family being discussed.
