What does it mean for a count to fit exactly?

A maker packs screws in trays of six. An order of 42 screws fills seven trays; an order of 43 leaves one screw. The distinction is more useful than the familiar phrase “goes into”: it asks for a whole-number witness. For integers a and b, a divides b, written a ∣ b, means that some integer k satisfies b = ak. We call k a witness. Thus 6 ∣ 42 has witness 7. The definition permits negative integers: −6 ∣ 42 has witness −7. It also permits zero as the number being divided: every nonzero a divides 0, with witness 0.

The background here is only multiplication of integers. There is no need yet to factor a number into primes. We will make one exact claim about chains of divisibility, then notice why some tempting variations fail. The tray picture helps when counts are positive, but the integer definition is what makes the general statement precise.

Keep track of the witnesses

Worked example. Suppose 4 ∣ 12 and 12 ∣ 60. The first statement has witness 3 because 12 = 4·3; the second has witness 5 because 60 = 12·5. Combining them gives 60 = (4·3)·5 = 4·15. The new witness is 15, so 4 ∣ 60. The numerical work is a model for the proof, but the proof must work with unspecified integers.

Some texts reserve the word “divisor” for a nonzero number even while using the same witness relation. We will say explicitly when a nonzero divisor is needed, especially before a division algorithm. A zero row in the table is therefore a useful test of the definition rather than a practical tray arrangement.

A small theorem from one substitution

Transitivity of divisibility. For any integers a, b and c, if a ∣ b and b ∣ c, then a ∣ c. By the two hypotheses, there are integers r and s with b = ar and c = bs. Substitute the first equality into the second: c = (ar)s = a(rs). Integers are closed under multiplication, so rs is an integer witness. That completes the proof; no assumption that a, b or c is positive was used.

a∣b∧b∣c⇒c=a(rs)⇒a∣c

A related rule follows from the same definition: if a ∣ b and a ∣ c, then a divides b+c and b−c. Write b = ar and c = as. Their sum is a(r+s), and their difference is a(r−s). This explains why adding a multiple of a to a number does not alter whether that number is divisible by a. It will power the remainder steps of the Euclidean algorithm.

Failure case. Reversing the transitivity claim does not work. Although 4 ∣ 60, choosing 7 as a supposed middle number gives neither 4 ∣ 7 nor 7 ∣ 60. More concretely, “a divides b+c, so a divides both b and c” is false: 3 ∣ (1+2), yet 3 divides neither 1 nor 2. A sum can create a multiple by cancellation of remainders.

Find a witness, then test a claim

1. Show that −4 divides 28 and 28 divides 84. Give a witness for −4 ∣ 84 obtained by multiplying your first two witnesses.

Hint for question 1

Write each target as the stated divisor times an integer, keeping the minus sign on the first multiplier.

Full solution to question 1

28 = (−4)(−7), so the first witness is −7. Also 84 = 28·3, giving witness 3. Substitution yields 84 = (−4)(−7·3) = (−4)(−21). The product −21 is the requested witness, and the two negative signs correctly make 84 positive.

2. A proposed rule says: if 6 divides the product xy, then 6 divides x or 6 divides y. Find a counterexample with positive x and y, and explain the missing condition.

Hint for question 2

Split the factors 2 and 3 between the two numbers.

Full solution to question 2

Take x = 2 and y = 3. Then xy = 6, so 6 ∣ xy with witness 1. But 6 ∤ 2 and 6 ∤ 3 because neither has a whole-number quotient by 6. A prime divisor has a product rule of this form; 6 is composite, and its required factors can live in different factors of xy. We have only refuted the proposed general rule, not proved the prime version here.

Carry the witness with the claim

Divisibility is an existence statement: find an integer k for b = ak. A proof can manipulate that witness, as transitivity did, while a counterexample can show no such integer exists. Watch the zero case whenever an argument later divides by a number. A remainder table is the next useful step: it finds the greatest positive number dividing two inputs by repeatedly preserving their common divisors.
