When does degree predict intersections?

A line has degree one and a conic degree two, so the slogan “one times two” predicts two intersections. An ordinary real plot can show two, one, or none. The slogan becomes a theorem only after specifying the field, the projective setting, common components, and multiplicity. We will state that theorem accurately and prove the line–conic case by restricting a quadratic to the line.

Background capsule. A projective plane curve is the zero set of a nonzero homogeneous polynomial F(X,Y,Z). Its degree is the common total degree of its terms. A point is a nonzero triple [X:Y:Z] modulo nonzero scaling. Working over ℂ ensures every nonconstant one-variable polynomial has roots there. An intersection multiplicity Ip(C,D) is a positive integer measuring how the curves meet at p; transverse smooth crossings have multiplicity one, while a tangent meeting can have more. Its full local-algebra definition is beyond this article.

A line touches a conic

Worked example. Take the complex projective conic C: X²+Y²−Z²=0 and the line L: Y−Z=0. In the affine chart Z=1, L is y=1 and C is x²+y²=1. Substitution gives x²=0, so the sole affine point is (0,1), or [0:1:1]. At infinity Z=0, the line requires Y=0, then the conic requires X²=0, which would force the forbidden zero triple. Thus there is no extra point there.

Along this line, use homogeneous line coordinates [S:T] by [X:Y:Z]=[S:T:T]. Restricting the conic equation yields S²+T²−T²=S². The only zero of this degree-two homogeneous polynomial is [S:T]=[0:1], with root order two. In this line-conic case, that order is the intersection multiplicity, so the single point contributes two. The count is 1·2=2, despite there being just one distinct geometric point.

State the theorem with its conditions

Bézout's plane-curve theorem, stated rather than proved. Let C and D be projective plane curves over an algebraically closed field, defined by homogeneous polynomials of degrees m and n with no common irreducible component. Then their intersection is finite and

∑p∈C∩DIp(C,D)=mn

Here “no common component” rules out two equations sharing a whole curve; otherwise there are infinitely many common points and the finite sum is not the right expression. The field condition matters: over ℝ, y=2 and the unit circle have no real intersections, while over ℂ they meet at x=±i√3. The projective condition matters because affine curves can miss points at infinity. For example, the affine line y=0 and parabola y=x²+1 have no real intersection and have two complex affine intersections, while other polynomial pairs can send an intersection to Z=0. Each hypothesis repairs a different way that an apparent real affine count can fall short.

The full special-case argument below extends the preceding calculation: when a line is not a component of a homogeneous degree-two conic, substituting a projective parametrization of the line gives a nonzero homogeneous quadratic in two variables; over ℂ it has two roots counted with order. The local parameter calculation below explains those root orders as intersection multiplicities in this special case. We have not developed the local intersection theory needed for arbitrary singular curves, and we do not claim a proof of general Bézout. The MIT algebraic-geometry notes linked in the references give the broader theorem and proof context.

Prove the line–conic count for every allowed line

Special-case theorem. Let F be a nonzero homogeneous quadratic over an algebraically closed field k, and let L be a projective line that is not a component of F=0. Then the restriction of F to L has exactly two zeros counted with their root orders. Choose independent vectors u,v spanning the two-dimensional solution space of L's linear equation. Every point of L has a unique parameter [S:T] with representative Su+Tv, up to common nonzero scaling. Restriction gives Q(S,T)=F(Su+Tv)=aS²+bST+cT².

This restriction is not identically zero. Indeed, extend u,v to a basis of k³. In those coordinates L has third coordinate zero. If F vanished identically after setting that coordinate to zero, every monomial of F would contain that coordinate as a factor. The line equation would divide F, making L a component, contrary to the hypothesis.

There are three cases. If a≠0, the polynomial Q(S,1) has degree two. Algebraic closure and the factor theorem give two linear factors counted with repetition. They supply exactly two finite parameter roots counted with order, and Q(1,0)=a excludes the parameter point at infinity. If a=0 but b≠0, then Q=T(bS+cT). There is one root [1:0] at infinity and one root [−c:b] with T≠0, each simple. If a=b=0, nonzero restriction forces c≠0 and Q=cT², so [1:0] is the sole root, of order two. These cases exhaust all coefficients, including fields where a discriminant calculation needs care.

For this special case, the multiplicity can be read entirely on the line. Near a root choose an affine parameter t that is zero at that point. The restricted polynomial is trU(t), where U(0)≠0. Locally U is invertible, so imposing the conic equation is equivalent to imposing tr=0. The quotient has basis 1,t,…,tr−1 and dimension r. This is the line–curve local intersection multiplicity: a simple root contributes one and a double root two. The parameterization does not merge distinct projective parameters, so summing the computed root orders proves the line–conic total 2.

For example, the parabola YZ−X²=0 and its line at infinity Z=0 restrict to −X² on that line. Their only intersection is [0:1:0], counted twice. An affine drawing would omit it completely. The general degree-product theorem for two arbitrary curves still needs its broader local intersection theory.

Audit the count

1. Calculation. Intersect the conic X²+Y²−Z²=0 with the projective line X−Z=0. Find the points and their count over ℂ.

Hint for question 1

Set X=Z in the conic equation, then check whether Z=0 gives a valid projective point.

Full solution for question 1

Substituting X=Z leaves Y²=0. If Z=0, then X=Y=0, which is forbidden, so scale Z=1. The only point is [1:0:1]. The restricted polynomial has a double root Y=0, so this tangent intersection contributes multiplicity two. The total is 2=1·2.

2. Explanation. Why may we not apply the finite degree-product count to the curves XY=0 and XZ=0 as written in projective space?

Hint for question 2

Factor both homogeneous equations and inspect their common zero set when X=0.

Full solution for question 2

Both polynomials contain X, so the entire projective line X=0 lies in both curves. They share a component and thus infinitely many intersection points. Although both equations have degree two, the no-common-component hypothesis fails; the finite sum 2·2 is not applicable to this pair.

Count with the right objects

Degree products count complex projective intersections with multiplicity when there is no shared component. A real affine sketch may show fewer points, and tangency can merge distinct roots into one point. Stop once you can list all four conditions before invoking Bézout and can compute the line-conic tangent example directly.
