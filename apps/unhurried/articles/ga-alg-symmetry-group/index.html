Eight moves, one operation

Label a square's corners 1, 2, 3, 4 clockwise, starting at the top left. These are the same labels and fixed vertical mirror used in the short turn-and-flip discovery. A symmetry puts the square back on its outline while possibly moving its labels. Four turns and four reflections do that. Could combining two of them ever produce a ninth kind?

Background capsule. A function assigns one output to each input. Here its input is a corner's original position and its output is that corner's destination. A bijection uses each destination exactly once, so it can be undone. Two moves count as the same symmetry when every original corner reaches the same destination. We study those final actions, rather than how long a physical motion takes.

Read a product from the right

Call the clockwise quarter-turn R, the fixed vertical reflection F, and doing nothing E. The product FR, also written F ∘ R, means apply R first, then F. A superscript counts repetitions: R2 is two quarter-turns, R3 is three, and R0 means E. These are powers of a move, not powers of a corner number.

Follow a corner, then collect the moves

Worked example. A quarter-turn sends positions 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 1. The vertical flip exchanges 1 with 2 and 4 with 3. To calculate FR on original corner 1, turn first: 1 goes to 2. Then flip: 2 goes to 1. Reversing the order sends 1 to 2 by the flip and then to 3 by the turn. The two products disagree on this one corner, so they are different symmetries.

Why exactly eight? Original corner 1 has four possible destinations. Its adjacent corner 2 must then go to one of that destination's two neighbours. The image of corner 3 must be opposite the image of 1, and corner 4 takes the remaining position. That gives at most 4 × 2 = 8 possible actions. All eight rows are distinct and are realised by actual turns or reflections, so the count is exactly eight. The last four rows are reflections in the vertical, top-left-to-bottom-right diagonal, horizontal, and other diagonal axes, respectively.

Four reasons this collection is a group

Exact statement. The eight symmetries of this square form a group under composition. “Group” names a collection with one operation satisfying the following four requirements. In this example, the operation is doing one move after another.

  1. Closure: a product stays in the collection. A rigid motion preserves distances. Doing two such motions still preserves distances, and if each puts the square onto itself, their combination does too. We already counted every possible final symmetry, so that combination must be one of our eight.
  2. Identity: one action changes nothing. The move E leaves every corner fixed. Doing it before or after any symmetry leaves that symmetry's action unchanged.
  3. Inverses: every action can be undone. A quarter-turn is undone by three quarter-turns; a half-turn by another half-turn; and E by itself. Each of the four reflections is its own inverse: it swaps pairs of corners or fixes them, so repeating it restores every corner. Thus all inverse actions belong to our collection.
  4. Associativity: grouping does not change a fixed order. Take any three moves f, g and h, and any original corner i. Both (fg)h and f(gh) send it to f(g(h(i))). Each applies h first, then g, then f. The parentheses group the work but do not swap the moves, so the final actions agree on every corner.
R4=E,F2=E

The two equations record four quarter-turns and two vertical flips returning to the identity. This eight-element group is often called D4, the dihedral group of the square. A group is abelian if every pair of its actions commutes, meaning the order can be swapped. Ours is nonabelian: FR and RF already supplied a counterexample. Associativity holds even though commutativity fails; they ask different questions.

Try a product, then test a claim

1. Calculation. Read (FR)(R2) from the right. Simplify it and find its inverse. Which position receives original corner 1?

Hint for question 1

First do two quarter-turns, then the quarter-turn and flip in the left factor. Combine the three turns and find that row in the table.

Full solution for question 1

Associativity lets us write (FR)(R2) = F(RR2) = FR3. The table gives destinations 3, 2, 1, 4, so original corner 1 goes to position 3. This is a reflection, swapping 1 and 3 and fixing 2 and 4; repeating it restores all corners. Its inverse is itself.

2. Explanation. Someone says, “Since parentheses do not matter in this group, the order of the moves does not matter either.” Give one corner that defeats this claim, and name the two properties being confused.

Hint for question 2

Compare FR and RF on original corner 1. Parentheses and swapping are separate changes.

Full solution for question 2

FR sends 1 to 2 by the turn, then to 1 by the flip. RF sends 1 to 2 by the flip, then to 3 by the turn. Their final actions differ. Associativity permits regrouping a fixed sequence of moves; commutativity would permit swapping two moves. The first holds here, and the second fails.

A square you can reason with

You have collected every symmetry of the square and justified closure, identity, inverses and associativity. One corner also proves that the group is nonabelian. The argument depends on exact destinations, not how a physical animation looks. A table of all 64 products could record the answers; the reasons above explain why the operation has the required laws.

For another sitting, try explaining the difference between regrouping three moves and swapping two. If you want an example where every pair commutes, continue to addition on a modular clock.
