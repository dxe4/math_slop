Count turns even when a loop crosses itself

A loop can travel twice around a missing point, reverse direction, or cross its own trace. Counting visible intersections with a ray can fail when the loop touches the ray at a vertex. A continuous angle, followed without resetting at 2π, gives a stable count. This lesson computes it for polygonal loops avoiding the origin and states exactly what is needed to extend the count to arbitrary continuous homotopies.

Background capsule. A polygonal loop is a finite sequence of nonzero plane vertices v₀,…,vm=v₀, joined by straight segments; we require every segment to avoid 0. The argument of a nonzero point is an angle θ with coordinates r(cos θ,sin θ), where r>0. Angles differing by 2π describe the same point. An unwrapped angle records a continuous real value along a path instead of jumping from near 2π back to 0. Winding is oriented: counterclockwise counts positively.

Follow a square all the way around

Worked example. Take vertices (1,1), (−1,1), (−1,−1), (1,−1), then back to (1,1), in that order. Starting with angle π/4, the unwrapped angles at the successive vertices are π/4, 3π/4, 5π/4, 7π/4 and 9π/4. Each edge avoids the origin. The total change is 9π/4−π/4=2π, so the winding number is 1. Reversing the vertex order produces change −2π and winding −1.

Now traverse that same square twice. The final unwrapped angle is 17π/4, giving change 4π and winding 2. The trace on paper is still just one square; the parametrized walk matters. If the loop travels once counterclockwise and then retraces clockwise, the changes cancel and winding is 0. Such a loop may cross itself or repeat edges; repeated points do not invalidate the calculation as long as the path never touches the origin.

A useful boundary check is the segment from (1,0) to (−1,0): it passes through the origin. Its endpoint angles tempt a half-turn assignment, but winding is undefined for a loop using that segment in the punctured plane. The exclusion is a mathematical hypothesis, not a numerical inconvenience.

Why the total is an integer

Exact statement for polygonal loops. Choose a continuous real angle θ(t) along each origin-avoiding edge, matching values at shared vertices. Then

wind(γ,0)=θ(1)−θ(0)2π∈ℤ

Why can an edge use a continuous angle? A straight segment missing 0 cannot sweep an angle of π or more relative to the origin without crossing the opposite ray; equivalently, the normalized segment stays inside one open semicircle after choosing a suitable viewing direction. A continuous angle branch exists there. Begin with any angle at v₀ and, edge by edge, choose the branch that starts at the preceding endpoint. At the final vertex the physical point equals v₀. Therefore its final angle differs from its initial angle by an integer multiple of 2π. Changing the initial angle by 2πk changes both endpoint angles equally, leaving the quotient unchanged. This proves that the polygonal count is well-defined.

For computation, add the signed angle changes of the edges, each chosen continuously along the edge. The square gives four increments of π/2. This method is exact for symmetric coordinates, and numerical approximations need an error check near an edge through 0. A tiny change of a path that crosses 0 can change winding; a deformation required to avoid 0 cannot.

Homotopy boundary. For an arbitrary continuous loop γ in ℝ²∖{0}, the map γ(t)/|γ(t)| into S¹ has a continuous real angle lift once a starting angle is chosen. For a continuous homotopy of such loops, the lift can be chosen continuously over the parameter square. Then Δθ(s)/(2π) varies continuously with s but is always an integer. A continuous integer-valued function on an interval is constant, proving homotopy invariance. The angle-lifting theorem is the external input; its general proof is deferred to covering-space theory. The polygonal edge construction above alone does not establish it for every homotopy.

Compute and inspect the hypotheses

1. A polygonal loop goes through (2,0),(0,2),(−2,0),(0,−2),(2,0) in order. Find its winding around 0. What happens if the route is reversed?

Hint for question 1

Use unwrapped vertex angles 0, π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π.

Full solution for question 1

Each edge stays away from the origin and changes angle by π/2. The total change is 2π, hence wind=1. Reversing the route changes every signed increment, so Δθ=−2π and wind=−1. Both loops have the same geometric diamond as an unparametrized trace, but opposite orientations.

2. A proposed homotopy shrinks the square from the worked example by H(s,t)=(1−s)γ(t). Does homotopy invariance force a contradiction? Locate the failed hypothesis exactly.

Hint for question 2

Inspect the final parameter s=1 and the target space ℝ²∖{0}.

Full solution for question 2

At s=1, H(1,t)=0 for every t, so H is not a map into ℝ²∖{0}. The invariance theorem applies only to homotopies whose image avoids 0 at every (s,t). There is no contradiction: the proposed formula is a homotopy in ℝ², where winding about a deleted origin is not an invariant of paths allowed to hit that origin.

A number with a clear domain

For an origin-avoiding polygonal loop, angle increments yield an integer independent of the starting branch. Extending this to arbitrary continuous loops and homotopies uses the stated angle-lifting theorem. Winding distinguishes some loop classes but a value of zero by itself is not a general claim about all spaces. The circle group lesson makes the integer into a composition law.
