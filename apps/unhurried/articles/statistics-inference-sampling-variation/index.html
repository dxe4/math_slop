Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Why can two fair samples disagree?

Repeated tile samples reveal how a statistic varies. Start with a fixed jar and independent draws, then compare one hidden bag per run with independent bag selection before every draw. The protocols share individual blue probabilities but give different sampling distributions.

Background capsule. A parameter is a fixed population or model feature; a statistic is computed from a random sample. Code blue as 1 and other colours as 0; their average is the sample proportion. Independence means one draw does not change another's model probability. Variance averages squared deviation from the mean; standard deviation is its square root. Covariance averages products of centered deviations and vanishes for independent finite-variance draws.

Worked example

Imagine sampling with replacement from a jar whose blue probability is p=0.4. In five draws, one sample might contain 1 blue tile, giving 1/5=0.2, while another contains 3, giving 3/5=0.6. Neither result changes the jar’s fixed p. Under this model the chance of exactly one blue draw is 5(0.4)(0.6)^4=0.2592. The chance of exactly three is 10(0.4)^3(0.6)^2=0.2304. Both samples are ordinary possibilities, so disagreement alone is no sign that either sampling operation was dishonest.

For the five-draw jar, the distribution of the blue count has six possible values, 0 through 5. The sample proportion therefore changes in steps of 0.2. A normal bell curve would be a rough picture at this sample size; the exact binomial probabilities are available and more informative.

The claim and its conditions

SE(p^)=p(1−p)n

Let X_i be the zero-one result of draw i. Each has mean p and variance p(1−p). The average p-hat=(X_1+...+X_n)/n has mean p by linearity. For independent draws, covariance terms vanish, so its variance is n p(1−p)/n²=p(1−p)/n. Taking the square root gives its standard deviation, called the standard error here. This derivation is exact for iid Bernoulli draws with replacement. It does not need a normal approximation. In a finite jar sampled without replacement the draws are dependent, and a finite-population correction changes the variance.

Proof boundary and failure case. A standard error describes spread of the statistic across hypothetical repetitions under a named design. It is not a bound that every sample must obey. Plugging p-hat into the formula estimates an unknown standard error and adds approximation error. Systematic exclusion from a sampling frame is a separate problem: increasing n shrinks the iid random component but does not repair a biased frame.

Expanding Var(X₁+⋯+Xₙ) gives individual variances plus twice each pairwise covariance. Independence removes those terms; dependence can increase or decrease spread. The next experiment specifies that dependence.

A hidden bag changes the joint law

A bag population has two types, equally likely when a bag is selected: its blue rate P is either 1/4 or 3/4. A draw returns its tile before the next draw. Conditional on the selected rate, draws from that bag are independent Bernoulli trials. Independent selection of a bag means a fresh draw from this population; it may select the same type again.

  • One bag per run. Select a single hidden P, then take all n replacement draws from it. A new hypothetical run starts with a fresh bag choice.
  • Redraw the bag each draw. Independently select a bag before each draw, then take one tile from it. Bag choices and the draw randomness are independent across draws.

Either protocol gives a particular draw blue probability (1/2)(1/4)+(1/2)(3/4)=1/2. The population mixture mean μ=1/2 is a fixed parameter. The selected bag rate P is random across runs. A sample proportion from one bag may estimate that bag's rate well while estimating μ poorly. Stating the target is part of stating the problem.

Predict before changing n. At n=4, which protocol makes extreme counts more likely? At n=16, can either variance fall below 1/16? Does agreeing on each draw's marginal probability force the same count distribution?

Exact count laws, not an observed histogram

Let K count blue draws and Y=K/n be their proportion. Conditional on P=p, K has the binomial law. Averaging over the two possible shared bags gives

Pr(K=k)=12(nk)[(14)k(34)n−k+(34)k(14)n−k]

Here the stacked n and k mean the binomial coefficient, the number of ways to place k blue draws. The binomial lesson derives it. With a redrawn bag, each draw has a fresh independent rate and fresh draw randomness; after averaging these out, the indicators are independent Bernoulli(1/2). Its count law is therefore Pr(K=k)=C(n,k)/2ⁿ. Shared-bag counts are a mixture of two binomial laws, not a binomial law with their averaged parameter.

For n=4, shared-bag probabilities for K=0,1,2,3,4 are (41,60,54,60,41)/256. Redraw probabilities are (1,4,6,4,1)/16. Both have mean count 2. Their equal means conceal different spreads: Pr(K=0 or 4) is 41/128 in the shared protocol and 1/8 in the redraw protocol. The instrument enumerates count probabilities exactly; no observed runs are generated.

The covariance that survives averaging

For two distinct draws from the shared bag, conditional independence gives E[X_i X_j | P]=P². Thus E[X_i X_j]=E[P²]=5/16. Each marginal mean is 1/2, so Cov(X_i,X_j)=5/16−1/4=1/16. Independence after conditioning on P does not imply independence before conditioning. Seeing blue makes the high-blue bag more plausible, which changes another draw's probability.

There are n(n−1) ordered pairs of distinct draws in the variance expansion. Each indicator has marginal variance 1/4. Consequently Var(Y)=[n/4+n(n−1)/16]/n²=(n+3)/(16n). Redraws have zero covariance and Var(Y)=1/(4n). At n=1, both laws and variances agree; a single marginal cannot reveal their different dependence.

Separate variation within bags from variation between bags

The same calculation has a useful general form. Suppose a shared bag has any finitely supported random blue rate P, with independent replacement draws conditional on P. Let μ=E[P], v=Var(P), and w=E[P(1−P)]. These are fixed features of the bag population. Conditional on P, the sample proportion has mean P and variance P(1−P)/n.

Var(Y)=E[Var(Y|P)]+Var(E[Y|P])=wn+v

This is the law of total variance. To prove it here, write Y−μ=(Y−P)+(P−μ), square, and average. The cross term has expectation zero because E[Y−P | P]=0. The first squared term averages to w/n and the second to v. This finite conditional calculation is also a proof of the displayed identity in this model; it requires neither a bell curve nor a simulated trend. MIT's conditional-expectation lecture gives the general identity and its proof.

For our equally weighted bags, w=3/16 and v=1/16. The shared variance is 3/(16n)+1/16: increasing n removes the within-bag contribution but leaves the between-bag floor. With a freshly independent bag for every draw, Var(Y)=μ(1−μ)/n=(w+v)/n instead. At n=16 these are 19/256 and 1/64. The first remains larger than 1/16; the second is smaller. These are variances, not standard errors; take square roots to compare standard deviations.

Why one increasingly large bag sample is not consistent for the mixture mean

An estimator is consistent for μ if, for every fixed ε>0, the probability that its error exceeds ε tends to zero as the sample size grows. Unbiasedness, E[Y]=μ for every n, is a different statement. Our shared-bag Y is unbiased but is not consistent for μ=1/2.

We can prove more than a positive variance floor. Conditional squared error around the selected bag satisfies E[(Y−P)²]=3/(16n). Whenever |Y−1/2|≤1/8, either bag rate is at least 1/8 away from Y. For a nonnegative variable Z, E[Z]≥a Pr(Z≥a), because those outcomes each contribute at least a. Apply this with Z=(Y−P)² and a=1/64 to obtain

Pr(|Y−12|≤18)≤min(1,12n)→0

The bound is uninformative when n≤12, but its limit proves Pr(|Y−1/2|>1/8)→1. Meanwhile E[(Y−P)²]→0: Y does approach the chosen bag's rate in probability. More draws within that single bag improve estimation of P, not of the mixture mean μ. This conclusion concerns repeated runs under the stipulated model, not a claim that every finite observed sample lies near a bag rate.

A nonzero limiting variance alone would need care for unbounded estimators, since rare extreme errors can keep a variance large. Here Y is bounded, and the direct probability argument establishes the precise failure of consistency.

Use the sampling protocol

1. Calculation. For p=0.4 compare the exact standard errors for n=25 and n=100.

Hint for exercise 1

Compute p(1−p)=0.24, divide by n, then take square roots.

Full solution to exercise 1

At n=25, SE=sqrt(0.24/25)=sqrt(0.0096)≈0.0980. At n=100, SE=sqrt(0.0024)≈0.0490. Quadrupling n halves the standard error under the same iid model.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. A poll samples 1,000 people from a list that omits half the neighborhood. Does the small iid standard-error formula settle accuracy for the whole neighborhood?

Hint for exercise 2

Ask which population supplied the observations and whether draws from it represent the target.

Full solution to exercise 2

No. The formula can quantify random variation for an iid model of the listed people, if that model is credible. It says nothing about the omitted half’s responses. A narrow sampling distribution around the wrong target may still produce inaccurate neighborhood claims.

3. A two-draw diagnostic. Return to equally likely rates 1/4 and 3/4. Without using the explorer, find both count laws for n=2. Compute the chance of two blue draws under each protocol and the covariance of the two indicators. Why can the one-draw distributions not settle the question?

Hint for exercise 3

For a shared bag average p² over its two possible values; for independent bag redraws multiply the two marginal blue probabilities. Use Cov(X₁,X₂)=E[X₁X₂]−E[X₁]E[X₂].

Full solution to exercise 3

The shared-bag law for K=0,1,2 is (5,6,5)/16: Pr(K=2)=[(1/4)²+(3/4)²]/2=5/16, symmetry gives Pr(K=0)=5/16, and the middle mass is 6/16. The redraw law is (1,2,1)/4, with Pr(K=2)=1/4. Each indicator has mean 1/2 in either protocol. Shared covariance is 5/16−1/4=1/16; redraw covariance is zero. Both one-draw distributions are Bernoulli(1/2). Marginal probabilities specify individual draws, while a joint law also specifies how draws depend on each other.

4. A fresh population and a fixed sample budget. A new bag population has rate 1/8 with probability 1/3 and rate 5/8 with probability 2/3. Independently select B bags and take m independent replacement draws from each, with independent randomness across bags. The total N=Bm is fixed at 24. Let Y be the grand proportion across all 24 draws. Assume all draws cost the same and selecting additional bags has no setup cost.

  1. Find μ, v=Var(P), and w=E[P(1−P)] for this population. Does treating all 24 indicators as iid follow from their common marginal probability?
  2. Derive Var(Y) for general B and m, then compare three designs: 3 bags with 8 draws each, 8 bags with 3 draws each, and 24 bags with 1 draw each.
  3. Among equal-size designs with Bm=24, which variance is smallest? Explain what assumption permits the comparison and which term stays fixed. Would extra draws from one already selected bag supply the same information as extra independent bag selections?
Hint for exercise 4

Compute E[P²]−μ² and E[P−P²] separately. Each bag mean has variance v+w/m by the conditional calculation above. The B bag means are independent and equally weighted in Y. Average their variances before inserting N=Bm.

Full solution to exercise 4

The marginal mean is μ=(1/3)(1/8)+(2/3)(5/8)=11/24. E[P²]=(1+2×25)/(3×64)=17/64, so v=17/64−(11/24)²=1/18. Within-bag variation averages to w=(1/3)(7/64)+(2/3)(15/64)=37/192. As a check, w+v=143/576=μ(1−μ). Common marginals do not make indicators independent: distinct draws within one bag have covariance v=1/18; draws from independently selected bags have covariance zero.

Write Y=(Y₁+⋯+Y_B)/B, where Y_b is the proportion within bag b. Each Y_b has variance v+w/m, and the bag means are independent. Hence Var(Y)=B(v+w/m)/B²=v/B+w/(Bm). For N=24 this is 1/(18B)+37/4608. The designs give

For fixed N, w/N stays fixed while v/B decreases as B grows. Since v>0, the minimum among the allowed equal-size designs occurs at B=24,m=1. This is exactly the independently redrawn-bag protocol, with variance μ(1−μ)/24. It follows under independent bag selection and the stipulated cost model. Bag setup costs, dependence between selected bags, or unequal bag sample sizes require a new design calculation. Extra within-bag draws reduce that bag's draw noise; extra independent bag choices also average the between-bag variation. They are not interchangeable.

Repetition rules differ: the fixed jar uses p=0.4 in every run; each shared-bag run first selects P. Specify what is independently repeated before deriving a sampling distribution.

What you can carry forward

Separate a population parameter, a statistic, and the distribution of that statistic across specified repetitions. The iid square-root law follows from zero covariances. A shared hidden bag preserves each draw's marginal probability while creating positive covariance and a variance floor. Increasing the number of independent bags addresses a different source of uncertainty from increasing draws within one bag.

Always name the target and the sampling protocol. A proportion can be unbiased for a mixture mean yet fail to approach it, while successfully approaching its own bag's rate. A standard error reports scale rather than a guaranteed error band; an exact variance calculation does not repair an omitted population or establish that a real sampling procedure matches the model.
