Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

What could “meeting at infinity” mean?

In the ordinary plane, y=1 and y=2 are parallel and share no point. Projective coordinates add a line of points at infinity so that these two lines meet at a definite new point. This is an algebraic construction, not a claim that a distant visible location exists. We will calculate it and see why the scaling rule is essential.

Background capsule. In this lesson coordinates are real, so the numerical divisions below are valid; the same constructions work over ℂ. A projective point [X:Y:Z] is an equivalence class of nonzero triples: (X,Y,Z) and (λX,λY,λZ) represent the same point whenever λ≠0. The all-zero triple is excluded. In the chart Z≠0, scale by 1/Z to write [x:y:1], which corresponds to the familiar affine point (x,y). Points with Z=0 lie outside that chart and form the projective line at infinity. A projective line has an equation aX+bY+cZ=0 with coefficients not all zero.

Two horizontal lines

Worked example. To extend y=1, replace y by Y/Z in the chart Z≠0 and multiply by Z: Y−Z=0. Likewise y=2 becomes Y−2Z=0. Solve both homogeneous equations: subtracting gives Z=0, then Y=0. A projective triple with Z=Y=0 must have X≠0, so scaling gives the unique point [1:0:0]. It belongs to both lines and is at infinity because Z=0.

The same calculation works for y=mx+b and y=mx+c when b≠c. Their projective equations are Y−mX−bZ=0 and Y−mX−cZ=0. Subtracting gives (c−b)Z=0, hence Z=0. The remaining equation Y=mX gives the common point [1:m:0]. The point depends on slope m, not the intercept. This is why parallel lines in one direction acquire a shared projective point.

Homogeneous coordinates are classes

Exact definition. The projective plane over k consists of nonzero triples modulo common nonzero scaling:

[X:Y:Z]=[λX:λY:λZ]forλ≠0

For example, [1:2:0]=[3:6:0], so they are one point, not two intersections. A homogeneous linear equation is well-defined on classes: if aX+bY+cZ=0, then a(λX)+b(λY)+c(λZ)=λ·0=0. That scaling compatibility is why we use homogeneous equations. A nonhomogeneous expression like X+1=0 would not define a projective locus: [−1:0:0] satisfies it, while the same point represented as [−2:0:0] does not.

We can prove directly that two distinct projective lines over ℝ meet in exactly one projective point. Their two coefficient rows are linearly independent. Eliminating one unknown with the first equation and a second with the other leaves one free scalar, so the simultaneous homogeneous system has a one-dimensional solution space. Every nonzero solution is a scalar multiple of every other, giving one equivalence class. This elementary linear-algebra fact is the precise scope of “two lines meet.” It does not say two arbitrary curves meet in one point, nor does it count tangencies.

For a polynomial curve of degree d, one can homogenize by introducing suitable powers of Z to make every term total degree d, then examine its points with Z=0. We only carried this out for lines. The projective plane over ℝ and the one over ℂ have different available coordinates; later intersection counting uses ℂ so polynomial equations have the needed roots. See Bézout's hypotheses for the careful degree-product statement.

Calculate with classes

1. Calculation. Extend y=3x+1 and y=3x−4 to projective lines. Find their common point.

Hint for question 1

Replace x,y by X/Z,Y/Z and clear Z. Subtract the two equations.

Full solution for question 1

The equations are Y−3X−Z=0 and Y−3X+4Z=0. Subtracting yields 5Z=0, so Z=0. Then Y=3X. Since the triple is nonzero, X≠0; scale it to [1:3:0]. This point is the unique common projective point.

2. Explanation. Are [2:4:0] and [−1:−2:0] different points? Could [0:0:0] repair a failed line intersection?

Hint for question 2

Find a nonzero scalar relating the triples, and recall the exclusion in the definition.

Full solution for question 2

Multiplying (2,4,0) by −1/2 gives (−1,−2,0), so the triples represent the same point. The zero triple is excluded because scaling would never yield a chart coordinate and it lies on every homogeneous equation without representing a direction. It cannot serve as an additional intersection.

Infinity is a coordinate boundary

The chart Z≠0 recovers the affine plane; Z=0 records directions omitted by that chart. Parallel affine lines with equal slope meet at one projective point, calculated by homogeneous linear equations. Stop once you can solve an intersection using classes and can explain why [1:m:0] is an exact algebraic answer rather than a plotted faraway point.
