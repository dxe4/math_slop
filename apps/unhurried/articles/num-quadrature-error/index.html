An area estimate with an error ceiling

Dividing an interval into more trapezoids often improves an integral estimate, but a numerical pattern alone does not say how far the answer is from the true area. For a function with bounded second derivative, the composite trapezoidal rule has a provable error ceiling. Curvature supplies the missing information: a straight line is integrated exactly, while bending creates the gap.

Background capsule. The definite integral ∫abf(x)dx is signed accumulated area. A trapezoid over [s,t] has area (t−s)[f(s)+f(t)]/2. For n equal subintervals, h=(b−a)/n and nodes x_j=a+jh. A twice continuously differentiable function has a continuous second derivative; on a closed interval its maximum absolute second derivative exists.

Estimate the integral of a parabola

Worked example. Take f(x)=x² on [0,1]. The exact integral is 1/3. With n=2, nodes 0, 1/2, 1 have heights 0, 1/4, 1. The composite trapezoidal estimate is (h/2)[f(0)+2f(1/2)+f(1)] = (1/4)(0+1/2+1)=3/8. Its actual error is 3/8−1/3=1/24≈0.04167.

Here f″(x)=2 everywhere. The standard bound gives (b−a)h²·2/12 = (1/4)/6 = 1/24. Equality occurs for this quadratic, so the bound is sharp in this example. With n=4, h=1/4, the bound becomes 1/96, exactly one-quarter as large. It is reasonable to expect second-order behavior for this smooth case; that expectation is backed by the bound, not merely by a plot.

Where the h² bound comes from

|ET|≤(b−a)h212maxa≤x≤b|f″(x)|

On one panel [s,t] of length h, let L(x) be the line through the endpoint values of f. The interpolation remainder for a twice continuously differentiable f is f(x)−L(x)=f″(ξ_x)(x−s)(x−t)/2 for some ξ_x in the panel. This follows by applying Rolle’s theorem twice to f−L minus a multiple of (x−s)(x−t); it is an exact pointwise statement, though ξ_x may vary with x. Taking absolute values and using |f″|≤M gives |f(x)−L(x)|≤(M/2)(x−s)(t−x). Integrating that nonnegative parabola over the panel gives Mh³/12. The integral of L is the trapezoid area.

Summing n panel bounds gives nMh³/12 = (b−a)Mh²/12. This proves the displayed inequality. It assumes exact function values and exact arithmetic for the quadrature sum. Rounding and uncertain measurements add other error terms. A finite difference of successive estimates can suggest an observed rate, but it does not certify the unknown integral without a theorem or independent enclosure.

The hypothesis matters. For f(x)=√x on [0,1], f″ is unbounded near zero, so the displayed global M does not exist. The trapezoidal rule still produces numbers, but this particular C² certificate cannot be invoked. Discontinuities and singular integrands call for separate analysis or adapted methods.

Use the bound correctly

Exercise 1. For f(x)=x² on [0,2] with n=4 equal panels, find T₄, the exact integral, and the bound. Check whether equality holds.

Hint for exercise 1

Use nodes 0, 1/2, 1, 3/2, 2 and M=2.

Solution to exercise 1

Here h=1/2. The endpoint heights are 0 and 4, interior heights 1/4,1,9/4. Thus T₄=(1/4)[0+2(1/4+1+9/4)+4]=(1/4)(11)=11/4. The exact integral is [x³/3]₀²=8/3. Their difference is 11/4−8/3=1/12. The bound is (2)(1/4)(2)/12=1/12, again equality for a quadratic.

Exercise 2. On [0,1], suppose |f″|≤6. How many equal panels suffice for a trapezoidal error at most 0.0005? Explain what would change if the sampled f values each had unknown error up to 0.01.

Hint for exercise 2

Solve 6/(12n²)≤0.0005; then consider the weighted average of sample errors.

Solution to exercise 2

The exact-arithmetic bound is 1/(2n²). Requiring it ≤1/2000 gives n²≥1000, so n≥32 suffices. If every sampled value may differ by 0.01, the trapezoidal weights are nonnegative and sum to interval length 1, giving an additional possible error of 0.01. Thus the 0.0005 total-error certificate is lost regardless of n unless those data errors are reduced or otherwise controlled.

A theorem, with its domain attached

The composite trapezoidal error is at most (b−a)Mh²/12 when f is C² and M bounds |f″|. The proof is a one-panel interpolation error summed across the interval. A singular endpoint, unreliable function samples, or rounding changes the error budget. For a complementary lesson about choosing a step when rounding competes with approximation, see finite differences.
