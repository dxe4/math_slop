A committee either includes its chair or it does not.

You are choosing a three-person planning committee from six volunteers, one of whom is Mira. The formula says C(6,3) = 20. But you can also sort committees by the simple question “Is Mira on it?” This split explains why every inner entry of Pascal's triangle is the sum of two entries above it. It also gives a method that can be used when a direct factorial calculation feels opaque.

Prerequisite capsule. A k-subset is a group of exactly k distinct objects selected without order. The symbol C(n, k) counts such groups from n distinct objects. For 0 ≤ k ≤ n, it equals n!/[k!(n−k)!]. We set C(n,k) = 0 when k falls outside 0,…,n, because no such subset exists.

Count a concrete case

Worked example. If Mira is absent, choose all three committee members from the other five people: C(5,3) = 10. If she is present, choose the remaining two from those five: C(5,2) = 10. No committee belongs to both lists, and every committee belongs to one. Hence 20 = 10 + 10. To build a triangle, start with C(0,0) = 1. At each row, the left and right edges are 1, because the empty set and the full set are unique. Each inner value is the sum of its two parents: row n=4 is 1,4,6,4,1 and row n=5 is 1,5,10,10,5,1. The entries count actual collections; the picture is a record of the partition proof.

The exact rule and its proof

(nk)=(n−1k)+(n−1k−1)

Pascal's identity. For every integer n ≥ 1 and 0 ≤ k ≤ n, the displayed equality holds under the zero-outside-range convention. Fix one distinguished element x among the n. The k-subsets excluding x correspond one-to-one with k-subsets of the remaining n−1 elements. The k-subsets including x correspond one-to-one with (k−1)-subsets of the remaining elements: remove or replace x. Those two cases are disjoint and exhaustive, proving the equality. At k=0, the inclusion case has zero members because selecting −1 others is impossible, and the empty set appears in the exclusion case. At k=n, the reverse holds. This is a complete combinatorial proof; it does not rely on the numerical pattern as evidence.

Where the rule fails. The partition needs a single fixed distinguished element. If you choose a different “special person” separately for each committee, the two lists are no longer defined against the same universe of remaining people. Another trap is to say C(6,3) = C(5,3) + C(5,3): in the Mira-present case only two places remain. That mistaken equation gives 20 = 20 by accident for this symmetric example; its second 10 counts the wrong objects. For C(5,2), the analogous mistake gives 10 versus 6+6=12, exposing the error.

Worked example: choosing a pair around one fixed person

Choose two people from five volunteers A,B,C,D,E. Distinguish E. Teams excluding E are AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD, giving C(4,2) = 6. Teams including E are AE, BE, CE, DE, giving C(4,1) = 4. The cases are disjoint because E cannot be both present and absent, and they cover every pair. Therefore C(5,2) = 6 + 4 = 10.

The two lower entries differ in their selection sizes. In the second case E already fills one of the two places, leaving exactly one person to choose. If we wrongly used C(4,2) twice, we would obtain 12 and count no well-defined partition of the original pairs. This nonsymmetric example exposes an error that the equal values in the earlier six-person example could hide.

Try two counts

Exercise 1. Use Pascal's identity to compute C(7,2) from row six, and explain the two cases using one named volunteer.

Hint for exercise 1

A two-person team either contains the named volunteer or excludes them. What sizes are selected from the six others?

Full solution to exercise 1

Excluding the volunteer gives C(6,2)=15 teams; including them gives C(6,1)=6 teams. They are disjoint and exhaustive, so C(7,2)=15+6=21. A direct check gives 7×6/2=21.

Exercise 2. Explain why the sum of all entries in row four is 16 without adding the numbers one by one.

Hint for exercise 2

Each subset of a four-element set has some size k from zero to four. Count subsets by recording four yes/no decisions.

Full solution to exercise 2

There are 2 choices, in or out, for each of four named elements, hence 2⁴=16 subsets. Partitioning by subset size gives C(4,0)+C(4,1)+C(4,2)+C(4,3)+C(4,4). Therefore that row sum is 16. Numerically 1+4+6+4+1 confirms it.

What to carry forward

Pascal's identity comes from a disjoint split: contain one fixed element or do not. The proof repairs both the left and right edge cases when impossible selections are assigned count zero. Beyond triangle arithmetic, this is a reusable technique: divide a collection by a property, count each part accurately, then add because no object appears twice.
