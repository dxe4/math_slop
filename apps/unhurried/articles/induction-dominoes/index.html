Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

How do you prove every row length?

The sum 1 + 2 + 3 + ··· + n appears whenever a task adds one more item at each stage. For the first few lengths the totals are 1, 3, 6, and 10. They match n(n + 1)/2, but matching examples cannot certify every positive n. Mathematical induction supplies the missing bridge: prove the first case, then prove that any established case gives the next one.

Background capsule. The positive integers are 1, 2, 3, and so on. Let Sn mean 1 + ··· + n. An induction hypothesis is permission to use the target formula for one earlier index during the step; it is not permission to assume the formula at the index being proved. We need only algebra, the meaning of a sum, and that order of positive integers.

See what a new last term does

Worked example. At n = 4, the sum is 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10, while 4·5/2 = 10. Going from length 4 to length 5 adds exactly 5, giving 15 = 5·6/2. In symbols, the move is Sn+1 = Sn + (n + 1). This identity is true from the definition of the sum, before we substitute any formula. The question for an induction proof is whether the proposed expression transforms correctly under that move.

The optional builder can display several bases and steps. Any finite run is an illustration of the symbolic proof below. Its crucial output is the separation between “assume the formula at n” and “derive it at n + 1.” A falling-domino picture is similarly useful but cannot replace the arithmetic step.

Base, permitted hypothesis, step

Sn=∑j=1nj=n(n+1)2

A name for a sum

The large ∑ means “add up.” Its lower label starts j at 1, its upper label stops at n, and the j to its right is the term to add. At n = 3, it means 1 + 2 + 3. In Sn, the small n is a label telling us which sum we mean; it is not multiplication by n. You may read Sn as “the sum through n.”

Theorem. For every positive integer n, Sn = n(n + 1)/2.

  1. Verify the start. At n = 1, the sum is 1 and the proposed formula gives 1·2/2 = 1. Both sides agree.
  2. State the permitted assumption. Fix any positive integer n. For this step, suppose the formula is true at n: Sn = n(n + 1)/2. We must now derive the next case from this assumption.
  3. Add the new last term. By the definition, Sn+1 = Sn + (n + 1). Substituting the assumed formula gives n(n + 1)/2 + (n + 1).
  4. Combine and factor. Write the added term as 2(n + 1)/2, so both terms have denominator 2. Their numerators have the common factor n + 1. Factoring gives the expression below, exactly the proposed formula at n + 1.
n(n+1)+2(n+1)2=(n+1)(n+2)2

The base case and the step together prove the theorem by induction.

Why is assuming the earlier case allowed?

The step is an “if … then …” claim: if the formula holds at n, then it holds at n + 1. We temporarily take the “if” part as given to prove that implication. We are not assuming that every case is true. The separately verified base makes the implication useful: case 1 gives case 2, which gives case 3, and so on.

The logical induction rule is sound because any alleged first positive integer where the formula fails would be greater than 1, since the base succeeds. Its predecessor would satisfy the formula by being earlier than the first failure; the proven step would then force the alleged failure to satisfy it too. That contradiction explains why a base and a universal step reach all positive integers.

A false start illustrates the boundary. Writing “assume Sn+1 = (n + 1)(n + 2)/2” in the step assumes the very target. A valid step alone is also insufficient. Consider the claim “n ≥ 2” for every positive integer. Its step works: if n ≥ 2, then n + 1 ≥ 2. But its base at 1 fails, and the universal claim is false. Starting at 2 would prove only the cases from 2 onward. Always check both obligations and where the route starts.

Run both obligations

Exercise 1. Prove by induction that 2 + 4 + ··· + 2n = n(n + 1) for all positive integers n.

Hint for exercise 1

The next sum adds 2(n + 1). Factor n + 1 after substitution.

Full solution to exercise 1

For n = 1, both sides equal 2. Suppose the sum through 2n equals n(n + 1). Adding 2(n + 1) gives (n + 1)(n + 2), which is the target at n + 1. The base and step prove the claim for all positive n.

Exercise 2. A proposed induction for 1 + ··· + n = n² says: “At 1 it works. Assume the formula at n + 1; then it works at n + 1.” Find a counterexample and the logical error.

Hint for exercise 2

Test n = 2, then inspect which index is assumed in the step.

Full solution to exercise 2

At n = 2, 1 + 2 = 3 whereas 2² = 4, so the claim is false. The step assumes its own target at n + 1 instead of assuming the earlier case at n and deriving the next case. The true base cannot rescue a circular step.

One verified start, every justified step

The sum formula is proved for all positive integers by a base and a valid transition. Checking many rows, or watching many dominoes fall, would not supply that transition. This lesson uses ordinary induction with one immediately previous case. Strong induction permits all earlier cases when a construction needs more than one.
