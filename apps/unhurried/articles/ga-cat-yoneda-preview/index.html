What do arrows into an object reveal?

To probe an object A, record all maps X→A for each possible X. A map u:Y→X turns a probe X→A into a probe Y→A by precomposition. That reversal is the reason this construction is contravariant. We will prove the Yoneda correspondence for a two-object category, then state the general result with its exact typing.

Background capsule. A locally small category has a set HomC(X,A) of arrows for each pair of objects, even if the class of all objects is large. A contravariant functor F:Cop→Set sends u:Y→X in C to F(u):F(X)→F(Y). The represented functor HA=HomC(−,A):Cop→Set sends u to precomposition: h:X→A becomes h∘u:Y→A. A natural transformation HA⇒F is a compatible family of functions, one for each X.

A complete two-object probe

Worked example. Let C have objects 0 and 1, identities, and one nonidentity arrow a:0→1. Choose A=1. Then H1(0)={a} and H1(1)={11}. Precomposition along a maps 11 to a. Define a contravariant F by F(1)={r,s}, F(0)={x,y}, and F(a):F(1)→F(0) with r↦x, s↦y. Identities act as identities.

The component at 0 is forced: naturality at a requires its value on a to be F(a) applied to the value of the component at 1 on 11. Thus there are exactly two natural transformations, corresponding to the two members of F(1). Notice the direction F(a):F(1)→F(0); writing F(0)→F(1) here would reverse the wrong way.

Statement, finite proof and general boundary

Exact general statement. For a locally small category C, an object A and F:Cop→Set, the contravariant Yoneda lemma gives a bijection

Nat(HomC(−,A),F)≅F(A)

Full proof for our finite C. A component at 1 is a function from the singleton {11} into {r,s}, so it chooses t=r or t=s. Naturality for a says η0(a)=F(a)(η1(11)). This uniquely fixes the component at 0. Conversely, each choice t produces the component pair in the table; the equation holds at a, and identities commute automatically. Hence the association η↦η1(11) is a bijection in this finite category.

General proof sketch, not a full proof here. The same candidate map sends η to ηA(1A). Given t∈F(A), the expected inverse has component at X sending h:X→A to F(h)(t)∈F(X). Naturality follows from contravariant functoriality, and applying the formula to 1A recovers t. A complete general treatment checks the family and inverse maps across every object and arrow, including any set-theoretic size conventions for a large C. We have stated that route; the bounded proof above checks every case in one finite C.

A tempting but ill-typed variant is Nat(HomC(A,−),F) with this contravariant F. HomC(A,−) is covariant, so the displayed natural-transformation set is not defined as written: its two functors have different domains. The variance is mathematical content, not decoration.

Follow the variance

1. Calculation. Replace F(a) by the constant map r↦x, s↦x. List the two natural transformations H1⇒F.

Hint for question 1

Choose η1(11) first, then use naturality to force η0(a).

Full solution for question 1

For t=r, η1(11)=r and η0(a)=x. For t=s, η1(11)=s and η0(a)=x. They remain distinct because their components at 1 differ, even though the components at 0 coincide.

2. Explanation. Why is a proposed map F(a):F(0)→F(1) inconsistent with F:Cop→Set when a:0→1 in C?

Hint for question 2

In Cop, a points from 1 to 0.

Full solution for question 2

The arrow a:0→1 becomes aop:1→0. A covariant functor on Cop must send it to a function from F(1) to F(0). A function F(0)→F(1) has the opposite type and cannot act on t∈F(1) to make η0(a).

One precise window

In the two-object category, a natural map from arrows into 1 to F is wholly determined by its value on the identity of 1, and every such value works. The general Yoneda lemma asserts this for every locally small C and contravariant Set-valued F; its full general proof is beyond this article. When writing a new instance, check whether both functors live on C or Cop before trying a naturality square.
