What algebra lives on a parabola?

On the parabola y=x², the expressions y and x² give the same value at every point. A coordinate ring records exactly this relation by treating their difference as zero. We will construct the quotient ring and prove, explicitly, that its arithmetic is the same as ordinary one-variable polynomial arithmetic.

Background capsule. R[x,y] is the ring of polynomials in x and y with real coefficients. An ideal such as (y−x²) consists of all polynomial multiples H(x,y)(y−x²). In the quotient R[x,y]/(y−x²), two polynomials represent the same class when their difference is in this ideal. Brackets [P] denote the class of P. Ring addition and multiplication are performed on representatives, then classes are taken. The equation y=x² explains why [y]=[x²].

Reduce expressions by substitution

Worked example. Consider P(x,y)=y²+3xy−2. Along y=x², this becomes x⁴+3x³−2. In the quotient, [P]=[x⁴+3x³−2] because P−(x⁴+3x³−2)=(y−x²)(y+x²+3x). Thus the two expressions differ by an explicit multiple of the defining equation. At x=2 on the parabola, where y=4, both give 16+24−2=38. The single point check is reassuring, but the factorization proves equality of classes for every point and within the quotient algebra.

Define φ:R[x,y]→R[x] by φ(P)=P(x,x²). It replaces every y by x². It preserves sums and products because substitution does: φ(P+Q)=φ(P)+φ(Q), and φ(PQ)=φ(P)φ(Q). It is onto, since every polynomial q(x) already lies in R[x,y] and φ(q)=q. The main issue is its kernel: precisely which polynomials become zero after substitution?

Prove the isomorphism

Exact result. Substitution induces a ring isomorphism

ℝ[x,y]/(y−x2)≅ℝ[x]

To prove the kernel claim, treat P as a polynomial in y whose coefficients are polynomials in x. The elementary identity yn−an=(y−a)(yn−1+yn−2a+⋯+an−1) shows, term by term with a=x², that P(x,y)−P(x,x²) is divisible by y−x². Therefore P(x,x²)=0 exactly when P itself is a multiple of y−x². Hence ker φ=(y−x²). If two classes [P] and [Q] map to the same one-variable polynomial, then φ(P−Q)=0, so P−Q lies in that ideal and [P]=[Q]; the induced map is injective. Surjectivity was already checked, so it is an isomorphism. This proves the displayed statement without using the general first isomorphism theorem as a black box.

The coordinate ring is a device for retaining polynomial functions subject to relations. We worked over ℝ and used the principal ideal generated by y−x². For an arbitrary collection of equations, defining a quotient by their ideal is straightforward, but the relation between all polynomial functions and geometric points is subtler. For instance, quotienting by (x²) remembers a nonzero class [x] whose square is zero, even though x=0 and x²=0 define the same ordinary point set. Such an element is nilpotent. We will examine it in the affine scheme window. Our explicit isomorphism does not establish the full ideals–varieties correspondence.

Calculate in a quotient

1. Calculation. Reduce x²y+y² in R[x,y]/(y−x²), and give its value at (−1,1).

Hint for question 1

Use [y]=[x²] twice, then substitute x=−1.

Full solution for question 1

[x²y+y²]=[x²·x²+(x²)²]=[2x⁴]. At x=−1 this gives 2. Directly at (−1,1), x²y+y²=1·1+1=2, agreeing with the quotient reduction.

2. Explanation. Does equality of values at the single point (0,0) imply [P]=[Q] in the parabola's coordinate ring? Test P=x and Q=0.

Hint for question 2

Compare P and Q at another parabola point, and use the substitution map.

Full solution for question 2

No. At (0,0), both x and 0 evaluate to zero. But at (1,1), x evaluates to 1 while 0 evaluates to 0. Also φ(x)=x is not the zero polynomial, so x is not in the kernel (y−x²), and [x]≠[0]. One matching evaluation is much weaker than equality of polynomial classes.

A relation becomes arithmetic

The quotient R[x,y]/(y−x²) makes y and x² interchangeable, and the substitution proof identifies it exactly with R[x]. Stop once you can reduce a class and explain why the kernel is the defining ideal. The broader geometric meaning of ideals needs field and radical hypotheses that this example alone does not supply.
