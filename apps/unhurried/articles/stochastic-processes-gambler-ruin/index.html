Which edge arrives first?

A token moves independently left or right with probability 1/2 on integer positions 0,…,4, stopping at absorbing edges 0 and 4. Starting at 1, what is the chance of hitting 4 first? Paths can bounce arbitrarily long, so use a recurrence instead of listing them.

Background capsule. Conditional probability fixes known information. A first right move from 1 restarts the problem at 2; a left move reaches 0. Total probability multiplies each first-move chance by its subsequent success chance, then adds. The resulting equations use ordinary algebra.

Let hᵢ be the probability of eventually reaching 4 before 0 from i. We prove almost-sure absorption on a finite board and calculate expected duration. Removing the right edge in the optional investigation preserves almost-sure stopping but gives infinite mean duration. These are ideal token models.

Solve the four-space board

Worked example. The boundary values are h₀ = 0 and h₄ = 1. From each interior position a first step lands on a neighbor, giving h₁ = h₂/2, h₂ = (h₁+h₃)/2 and h₃ = (h₂+1)/2. Substitute the first and third equations into the second: 2h₂ = h₂/2 + (h₂+1)/2 = h₂+1/2, so h₂ = 1/2. Then h₁ = 1/4 and h₃ = 3/4. Starting one space from the left edge gives only one chance in four of reaching the far edge first.

Why the recurrence has a unique answer

hi=phi+1+(1−p)hi−1,h0=0,hN=1

For the fair case p = 1/2, rearranging gives hᵢ₊₁−hᵢ = hᵢ−hᵢ₋₁. Thus successive differences are a constant d. Starting from h₀ = 0 gives hᵢ = id; ending at hᴺ = 1 forces d = 1/N. This proves the displayed rule for every finite N ≥ 1, provided each interior move is independently fair and the process stops at the endpoints.

From any interior position, N consecutive left moves reach 0 within N steps, with chance at least 2⁻ᴺ. Each fresh N-step block therefore has conditional absorption chance at least 2⁻ᴺ. Survival through k blocks is at most (1−2⁻ᴺ)ᵏ→0. This proves almost-sure absorption and identifies the bounded recurrence solution as the eventual hitting probability.

Boundary. Unequal move chances break equal differences: on 0,1,2 with right chance 3/4, h₁=3/4. Reflecting only after the first edge hit leaves first-hit probabilities unchanged. Fixed-time location is a different question with different boundary data. Finite simulations cannot prove eventual absorption or exact fractions.

How many chimes should the finite game take?

Let T count chimes until first hitting 0 or N; on an edge T=0. Expectation weights durations by probability; see expected scores. For nonnegative integer T, E[T]=∑t≥0P(T>t), possibly infinite: a k-step path contributes one at t=0,…,k−1. Nonnegative terms justify the rearrangement without cancellation.

Prove finiteness first. The block bound above gives P(T>kN) ≤ (1−2−N)k. Within each block of N times, survival is no larger than at its beginning. Therefore E[T] ≤ N∑k≥0(1−2−N)k = N2N < ∞. This loose bound is enough to justify manipulating the finite means next; we have not assumed the answer we want to prove.

Write ei=Ei[T] for the mean from position i. A first step uses one chime, then leaves the same fair problem at a neighbor. Thus e0=eN=0 and ei=1+(ei−1+ei+1)/2 for 0<i<N. Rearranging gives ei+1−2ei+ei−1=−2. Direct substitution shows that ei=i(N−i) satisfies these equations and both boundaries. For uniqueness, subtract any two solutions: their difference has constant neighboring differences and zero values at 0 and N, so every difference is zero. Consequently Ei[T]=i(N−i) for a finite fair board.

On 0,…,4 the mean is 3 chimes from 1 and 4 from 2. Runs may finish immediately or bounce arbitrarily long. Unlike the hitting-probability recurrence, the duration recurrence has an extra 1 and zero values at both edges.

The calculation counts all possible fair coin histories to the finite horizon, including unused continuations after a token has stopped. It is not a set of sampled runs. On a finite board, condition on each still-active position x at time H: its future mean delay is x(N−x). This gives the exact check E[T] = E[min(T,H)] + ∑x interiorP(XH=x,T>H)x(N−x). The remaining delay is weighted by unconditional active mass, not by a distribution renormalized to survivors.

The seeded walk laboratory samples paths recorded for at most 2,000 steps. Its sample mean of min(T,2000) estimates the capped expectation, not necessarily E[T]. Censored paths are still active: calling them absorbed or discarding them changes the measured quantity.

Remove the right edge, not just its drawing

Now the state space is 0,1,2,…, with absorption only at 0. Start at a fixed positive integer i. Every move before absorption is independently left or right with probability 1/2. Let T0 be the first hit of 0. There is no rule that reflects or stops the token at the diagram’s right-hand window.

Almost-sure absorption. Choose any integer N>i and follow the same coin sequence both on this half-line and on a finite board stopped at 0 or N. On the event that 0 is hit before N, the half-line token also hits 0. The finite result therefore gives Pi(T0<∞) ≥ 1−i/N for every N>i. Taking N arbitrarily large forces this probability to be 1. “Almost surely” means probability one; it does not say that every conceivable infinite coin sequence hits 0.

Infinite expected duration. On that same shared coin sequence, the first hit of either 0 or N occurs no later than the first hit of 0 alone. Thus T0 ≥ T{0,N}, and nonnegative expectation gives Ei[T0] ≥ i(N−i) for every N>i. The right side grows without bound. Hence Ei[T0] = ∞, despite T0 being finite almost surely. We used inequalities for nonnegative durations, not an exchange of a random limit with expectation.

Find the invalid line in a tempting expectation argument

The stopped position Mn=Xmin(n,T₀) is the token’s location after n chimes, keeping it at 0 once it arrives. For every fixed finite n, its mean remains i. One elementary proof writes Mn=i+∑k=1n1{T₀≥k}ξk, where ξk is the fair ±1 move and the indicator is 1 only when a kth move is still needed. Whether that move is needed is determined by the earlier moves, independently of ξk. Each summand therefore has mean zero. Only a finite sum is being averaged.

Almost-sure absorption also gives Mn→0 almost surely. It is tempting to write E[lim Mn] = lim E[Mn], but here the two sides are 0 and i. That equality is the invalid step. The terminal position is integrable (it is zero), increments are bounded by one, and the stopping time is almost surely finite. None of those facts, alone or together in this example, supplies a common integrable bound or sufficient control of the whole family’s tails. In particular E[T₀] is infinite.

Let sn=P(T₀>n)↓0. Absorbed paths contribute zero while E[Mn]=i, so E[Mn|T₀>n]=i/sn→∞ whenever sn>0. Fewer survivors carry the same mean. Bounds Mn≤i+n depend on n; on a finite board, 0≤Mn≤N is one common bound.

The optional-stopping theorem has hypotheses; it has not failed. It concerns martingales: processes with integrable values whose conditional next mean, given the past, equals their current value. For a martingale, a bounded stopping time, or an almost-surely finite stop with a common bound on the stopped positions, are useful sufficient cases for preserving the mean. Another sufficient case combines finite expected stopping time with bounded increments. This half-line example lacks the relevant tail control. Full martingale theory is outside this short investigation. Roch’s author-hosted Notes 18, Theorem 18.8 and section 3 states the conditions and develops these stopping-time results. The finite recurrences and comparisons above give the local proofs used here.

Use the boundary conditions

1. Calculation. On a fair board with edges 0 and 6, find the chance of reaching 6 first from 2. Then give the chance of reaching 0 first.

Hint for question 1

Use hᵢ = i/N and the fact that one of the two edges must eventually be reached.

Full solution to question 1

With i=2 and N=6, h₂=2/6=1/3. Absorption occurs with probability one, and the two first-hit events are disjoint and exhaustive. Thus the chance of hitting 0 first is 1−1/3=2/3. Starting closer to 0 gives it the larger chance.

2. Explain a changed rule. On 0,1,2 a move from 1 goes right with chance 2/5. What is the success chance, and which line of the fair proof breaks?

Hint for question 2

Both destinations are already absorbing. Write the one-step recurrence.

Full solution to question 2

h₀=0 and h₂=1, so h₁=(2/5)h₂+(3/5)h₀=2/5. The fair proof used the average of neighboring values, leading to equal differences. Here the weights are 2/5 and 3/5, so h₁ is not halfway between the boundaries. The first-step recurrence remains valid under the new rule.

3. Duration is a different recurrence. For a fair board with edges 0 and 6, calculate the full mean duration from 2 and from 3. A friend uses ei=(ei−1+ei+1)/2 with zero endpoint means and gets zero everywhere. Which term is missing?

Hint for question 3

The first move itself takes time even if it lands on an edge.

Full solution to question 3

The means are 2(6−2)=8 and 3(6−3)=9 chimes. The correct recurrence is ei=1+(ei−1+ei+1)/2. The omitted 1 records the first chime; zero boundary values alone do not distinguish the two questions.

4. Changed start and horizon, on paper. Start at 2 on the fair board 0,…,5 and record at most H=2 chimes. List the four two-coin histories. Find survival at H, mean recorded duration, conditional surviving position, and the exact remaining-delay term. Verify that capped mean plus remaining delay equals the full mean.

Hint for question 4

The endpoints are 0,2,2,4; each two-coin history has chance 1/4. Use x(5−x) only for still-active positions.

Full solution to question 4

LL reaches 0, LR and RL reach 2, RR reaches 4. Only LL has stopped, so survival is 3/4. No path can reach an edge before time 2; recorded duration is always 2, so its mean is 2. Among survivors, positions 2,2,4 have equal conditional weights, giving mean 8/3. The unconditional future-delay sum is (1/2)·2(5−2)+(1/4)·4(5−4)=3+1=4. Thus 2+4=6=2(5−2), as required. Renormalizing the delay weights to survivors would instead give 16/3, which is the conditional future delay and must be multiplied by survival 3/4 before adding to the capped mean.

5. Optional half-line proof check. A fair half-line walk starts at 2 and stops at 0. Someone argues: “The stopped position is always nonnegative, every increment is bounded, the final position is zero, and stopping happens with probability one. Therefore its initial expected position must be zero too.” Locate the unjustified step. Give an arbitrarily large lower bound for the mean duration using a finite board.

Hint for question 5

Compare the expectation at a fixed finite horizon with the expectation of the limiting position. For duration, couple the half-line walk to a board with an arbitrarily distant right edge.

Second hint for question 5

At a finite stopped horizon, use fairness to keep the starting mean. For the duration bound, use the same coin sequence on 0,…,N and substitute the starting position into the finite-board exit-time formula before increasing N.

Full solution to question 5

Each finite-horizon stopped position has mean 2, while its almost-sure limit has mean 0. Nonnegativity, bounded increments, integrable terminal value and almost-sure stopping do not justify passing expectation through that limit. For every N>2 the half-line duration is at least the exit duration from 0,…,N, so its mean is at least 2(N−2). These bounds are arbitrarily large, proving infinite expectation. A finite expected-time condition needed with bounded increments is absent.

6. Identical finite records, different full means. Start at 1 with fair steps and record H=3 chimes. Compare absorption at 0 or 5, absorption at 0 or 12, and absorption only at 0 on the half-line. Show that every three-coin history has the same recorded positions and duration in all three models. Find each full mean duration. Can even an exact three-chime distribution identify that full mean without knowing the stopping rule?

Hint for question 6

The largest position reachable in three steps from 1 is 4. Neither finite right edge can yet be reached.

Full solution to question 6

All three rules behave identically until time 3: they absorb at 0, and neither 5 nor 12 is reachable. At H=3 the common surviving mass is 3/8, capped mean is 2, and conditional survivor position is 8/3. Nevertheless the finite full means are 1(5−1)=4 and 1(12−1)=11, while the half-line mean is infinite. An exact finite-prefix law alone does not specify the missing future rule. This does not prevent calculation once the full model is given; it shows why a finite record cannot establish that infinite-time model by itself.

What a small recurrence can settle

First-step equations settle hitting probabilities hᵢ=i/N and mean durations Eᵢ[T]=i(N−i) on finite fair boards, using different boundary data. A recording cap measures min(T,H). Removing the right edge preserves almost-sure absorption but destroys finite expectation, as the comparison proof shows. Rewrite recurrences when rules change; the free-walk lesson supplies path notation.
