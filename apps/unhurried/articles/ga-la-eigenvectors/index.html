Which directions survive a map?

A map may stretch a diagonal line while squeezing another. A vector on a special line can emerge on that same line, although its length or direction may change. Such a nonzero vector is an eigenvector. The word “nonzero” prevents every matrix from acquiring the uninformative zero vector as an eigenvector for every number.

Background capsule. A real 2×2 matrix A acts on column vectors. Multiplying by a scalar λ scales both coordinates; negative λ reverses the arrow, and λ=0 collapses it. An eigenpair consists of a real number λ and a nonzero real vector v with Av=λv. A nonzero solution of a homogeneous system exists precisely when its 2×2 determinant is zero. For [[a,b],[c,d]], that determinant is ad−bc.

Find two stable lines

Worked example. Take A=[[3,1],[0,2]], meaning A(x,y)=(3x+y,2y). The vector (1,0) goes to (3,0), so λ=3. The vector (−1,1) goes to (−2,2)=2(−1,1), so λ=2. Neither eigenvector remains fixed in length, but each output lies on its original line. The line rather than a particular arrow is the stable geometric object.

InputOutputEigenvector?
(1,0)(3,0)Yes, λ=3

To discover these systematically, form A−λI=[[3−λ,1],[0,2−λ]]. Its determinant is (3−λ)(2−λ). Setting it to zero gives candidate eigenvalues 3 and 2. Solving (A−3I)v=0 gives y=0; solving (A−2I)v=0 gives x+y=0. That recovers both lines. Every nonzero multiple of one listed eigenvector lies on the same eigenline and has the same eigenvalue; there are infinitely many arrows on each line, not just the representative in the table.

Use the stable directions for repeated steps

For the same matrix A, write an arbitrary vector (x,y) as (x+y)(1,0)+y(−1,1). Expanding the right side gives (x+y−y,y)=(x,y), and the second coordinate determines y uniquely. The two eigenvectors therefore form a basis in this particular example. Applying A repeatedly now multiplies the first coefficient by 3 at each step and the second by 2. After k steps, for any nonnegative integer k, the result is (x+y)3k(1,0)+y2k(−1,1).

For input (2,1) and k=2 this gives 27(1,0)+4(−1,1)=(23,4). Direct multiplication checks the result: A(2,1)=(7,2) and A(7,2)=(23,4). The decomposition replaces two rounds of matrix arithmetic by two independent scalar powers. It works because these eigenvectors are independent and cover the input plane. A repeated eigenvalue with only one eigenline would not support this calculation for every input; finding one stable direction is not enough.

Why the characteristic equation appears

Exact statement and proof for real 2×2 matrices. A real scalar λ is an eigenvalue of A exactly when

det(A−λI)=0,v≠0,Av=λv

Indeed Av=λv rearranges to (A−λI)v=0. A nonzero solution means that the two columns of A−λI are dependent; in two dimensions, that happens exactly when their determinant is zero. Conversely determinant zero gives dependent columns, hence a nonzero kernel vector. Expanding for A=[[a,b],[c,d]] produces (a−λ)(d−λ)−bc=λ²−(a+d)λ+(ad−bc). This quadratic is the characteristic polynomial. Its real roots, when present, are the real eigenvalues. The argument proves the 2×2 criterion; it does not establish a general theorem about eigenvalues in arbitrary dimension.

A real rotation through a quarter-turn R=[[0,−1],[1,0]] is a crucial failure case for an overbroad intuition. Its polynomial is λ²+1, which has no real root. Geometrically every nonzero real vector turns 90 degrees and leaves its line. Over the complex numbers roots i and −i exist, but that is a change of scalar field, not a real eigenline. Even when a matrix has a real eigenvalue, it may lack two independent eigenvectors: [[1,1],[0,1]] has only the line y=0 for λ=1. We have therefore not promised a basis of eigenvectors for every map.

Test the line, not just a number

1. Calculation. Find real eigenvalues and one eigenvector for each of B=[[2,0],[1,3]]. Verify by direct multiplication.

Hint for question 1

The determinant of B−λI is (2−λ)(3−λ). Solve one homogeneous system per root.

Full solution for question 1

Eigenvalues are 2 and 3. For λ=2, (B−2I)(x,y)=(0,x+y), so choose v=(1,−1); Bv=(2,−2)=2v. For λ=3, (B−3I)(x,y)=(−x,x), so choose w=(0,1); Bw=(0,3)=3w. Both vectors are nonzero.

2. Explanation. Someone says every real 2×2 matrix has a real eigenvector because its characteristic polynomial is quadratic. Identify the gap with an explicit matrix.

Hint for question 2

A quadratic can have a negative discriminant. Try a quarter-turn.

Full solution for question 2

For R=[[0,−1],[1,0]], det(R−λI)=λ²+1. This is positive for every real λ, so no nonzero real v can solve Rv=λv. Being quadratic guarantees complex roots, counted with multiplicity, but does not guarantee real ones.

Stable lines have hypotheses

Find eigenvalues by det(A−λI)=0, then solve for nonzero vectors and check them in Av=λv. A real matrix can have no real eigenline or too few to form a basis. The singular-value lesson finds useful axes even for a rotation, by studying lengths through AᵀA rather than demanding an eigenline of A itself.
