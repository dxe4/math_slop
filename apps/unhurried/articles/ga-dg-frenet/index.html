Question and background

Imagine carrying two perpendicular arrows along a plane curve. One arrow points forward along the path; the other points left. Even if the arrows keep unit length and remain perpendicular, their directions change. The Frenet equations describe exactly how they rotate.

Local capsule. A unit-speed curve γ(s) satisfies ||γ′(s)||=1, so s measures signed progress in units of arc length. Put T(s)=γ′(s). If T=(T₁,T₂), define the left unit normal N=(-T₂,T₁). The dot product T·N is zero and det(T,N)=1. Signed curvature κ is det(T,T′), equivalently the component of T′ along N. All derivatives in this article are with respect to s.

The word “normal” can mean a principal normal pointing toward the center of bending, which fails to be defined when κ=0. We instead use the oriented left normal J T, which exists wherever T exists. That convention keeps the equations meaningful even across an inflection point.

A worked example

Worked example. For a counterclockwise circle of radius R, parameterize by distance: γ(s)=(Rcos(s/R),Rsin(s/R)). Then T=(-sin(s/R),cos(s/R)) and N=(-cos(s/R),-sin(s/R)). Differentiating gives T′=(1/R)N and N′=-(1/R)T. Thus κ=1/R. The normal points inward here because the circle turns left.

For clockwise travel along the same circle, γ(s)=(Rcos(s/R),-Rsin(s/R)). At s=0, T=(0,-1) and left normal N=(1,0), which points outward. Now κ=-1/R, so T′=κN still points inward. The sign and normal direction change together. The product remains the physical turning of T.

A straight line γ(s)=(s,0) has T=(1,0), N=(0,1), and κ=0. Both frame arrows remain fixed. This case is useful because an approach that divides by κ or insists on a “center of curvature” would fail here, while the oriented-frame equations do not.

Catch a derivative taken with the wrong clock

Consider β(t)=(2cos 3t,2sin 3t). This is a radius-two circle traversed counterclockwise with speed six. Its unit tangent is T(t)=(−sin 3t,cos 3t), and its left normal is N(t)=(−cos 3t,−sin 3t). Differentiating with respect to t gives dT/dt=3N and dN/dt=−3T. The coefficient three is not the curvature: it measures rotation per unit clock time.

Arc length grows at ds/dt=6, so differentiation per unit distance gives dT/ds=(dT/dt)/6=(1/2)N and dN/ds=−(1/2)T. Thus κ=1/2, as expected for a radius-two circle. Equivalently, with an arbitrary regular clock the equations read dT/dt=κ(ds/dt)N and dN/dt=−κ(ds/dt)T for this oriented traversal. The arc-length version removes the speed factor. Before interpreting the coefficient of N as curvature, check which variable appears after the derivative sign. A faster walker changes that coefficient in time without changing the circle.

The statement and its boundary

Exact statement. For a C² regular plane curve parameterized by arc length, with N obtained from T by a counterclockwise quarter-turn, the frame equations are:

T′=κN,N′=−κT

Proof: differentiate T·T=1 to get 2T·T′=0. In the plane, a vector perpendicular to T is a scalar multiple of N, so T′=κN, with κ=T′·N=det(T,T′). The rotation J taking (x,y) to (-y,x) is fixed, so N′=J T′=κ JN=-κT. This proves both equations without assuming κ is nonzero. Differentiating T·N=0 provides the same second relation after we know N′ has no N component.

These are plane equations. A space curve may require a binormal and torsion, with further regularity and nonzero-curvature caveats. We do not transfer the two-vector plane frame unchanged to three dimensions. Reversing orientation of the parameter reverses signed curvature under this left-normal convention.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. read a frame. At one point of a unit-speed curve, suppose T=(3/5,4/5) and κ=-2. Find N, T′ and N′ there.

Hint for exercise 1

Rotate T left, then multiply by κ as the equations prescribe.

Full solution to exercise 1

N=(-4/5,3/5). Hence T′=κN=(-2)(-4/5,3/5)=(8/5,-6/5). Also N′=-κT=2(3/5,4/5)=(6/5,8/5). Checks: T·T′=24/25−24/25=0 and N·N′=-24/25+24/25=0, as required for unit arrows.

Exercise 2. an inflection. Can this left-normal frame exist at a regular point where κ=0? What do the equations say at that point?

Hint for exercise 2

The definition N=JT contains no division by κ.

Full solution to exercise 2

Yes. Regularity gives a unit tangent T, and rotating it produces N even when κ=0. The equations give T′=0 and N′=0 at that instant. They do not say the arrows are constant on a neighborhood; curvature may change sign across the point. A principal normal defined as T′/||T′|| would be undefined there, which is why the convention matters.

What to carry forward

The equations are a compact record of oriented turning. They follow from unit length, orthogonality and the chosen left rotation, rather than from a sketch of arrows. The lesson's full proof is restricted to C² unit-speed plane curves; space-curve torsion and global reconstruction lie beyond it.

The next part of differential geometry replaces a single moving parameter with two local coordinates on a surface patch. The same habit remains useful: calculate derivative vectors, check they are genuinely independent, and state where the chosen coordinates stop working.
