When every fixed move can be exploited

One player hides a token under a left or right cup; another guesses. The hider gains points when the guess is wrong and loses points when it is right; the stakes depend on the side. The guesser receives the opposite payoff. If both choose fixed sides, one of them wishes to change. Randomizing can make the opponent indifferent and remove that predictable weakness. We will solve a deliberately asymmetric two-action game and then check its support conditions.

Background capsule. A mixed strategy assigns probabilities to actions, totaling one. Expected payoff is the probability-weighted average of possible payoffs. If an action has positive probability in an equilibrium mix, it must be a best response to the opponent's mix. With two supported actions, this means equal expected payoffs. The rule is necessary, but equality alone is not sufficient if an omitted action does better; we check all available actions.

Worked example. Matching the hiding side gives the guesser a gain and the hider a loss. The size differs by side. Let p be the probability that the hider selects L. Against guess L, the hider expects −2p + 2(1−p) = 2−4p. Against guess R, the hider expects p − (1−p) = 2p−1. The guesser tries to minimize these hider values. To make the guesser indifferent, set 2−4p = 2p−1, giving p = 1/2. Both guess choices then give hider expected payoff 0.

Now let q be the probability of guess L. If hider chooses L, its expected payoff is −2q + 1(1−q) = 1−3q. If hider chooses R, it gets 2q − 1(1−q) = 3q−1. Setting these equal gives q = 1/3 and common payoff 0. Thus the equilibrium candidates are hider (1/2,1/2) and guesser (1/3,2/3). Each probability lies in [0,1], and each player has only two actions. Every pure action now gives that player the same payoff, so neither can improve unilaterally by changing its mix.

Why indifference identifies the mix

For player i's two supported actions a and b against opponent mixture σ−i, the equal-payoff condition is:

E[ui(a,σ−i)]=E[ui(b,σ−i)]

Why? If a had higher expected payoff, moving probability from b to a would improve the player's expected score, contradicting equilibrium. Conversely, when both possible pure actions are tied at the maximum, any mixture of them is a best response. In this two-action example there are no hidden third actions to inspect. The two indifference equations determine the opponent's probability, not one's own directly: hider's p makes guesser indifferent, and guesser's q makes hider indifferent.

Proof boundary. This derivation checks a particular finite zero-sum table. It is not a proof of the general existence theorem for all finite games. A proposed solution with p outside [0,1] is not a mixed strategy; in such a case the correct equilibrium may be on the boundary with a pure action. If a third action existed, it would need an inequality check against the common supported payoff.

Calculate and test a mixture

1. Calculation. For the displayed game, suppose the guesser uses q = 1/2. Find the hider payoff from each pure hide action and its preferred move.

Hint for exercise 1

Substitute q into 1−3q and 3q−1.

Full solution to exercise 1

Hide L gives 1−3(1/2) = −1/2. Hide R gives 3(1/2)−1 = 1/2. The hider should choose R rather than mix with positive probability on L. Thus q = 1/2 cannot be the guesser's component of a fully mixed equilibrium in this game; the balanced guess probability is 1/3.

2. Explanation. A student solves only 2−4p = 2p−1 and reports “the equilibrium is p = 1/2.” What remains to be found and checked?

Hint for exercise 2

Which player does this equation make indifferent?

Full solution to exercise 2

The equation equates the hider's payoffs against the two pure guesses, making the guesser indifferent between guesses because its payoffs are the negatives. It finds the hider's mixing probability p = 1/2, but it leaves the guesser's mixing probability q unknown. Equating the hider's pure-action payoffs gives 1−3q = 3q−1, so q = 1/3. Both are valid probabilities; all pure actions have payoff 0 at the pair. These checks establish the claimed equilibrium for the two-action game.

Randomness as an incentive condition

A mixture is not chosen because half-and-half seems fair. Its probabilities solve the other player's best-response problem. Here the hider uses 1/2 on L and the guesser uses only 1/3 on L because losses from a left-side match are larger. The expected zero payoff is a property of this scoring model.

Failure case. Merely drawing actions randomly does not guarantee equilibrium. At guess probability 1/2, the hider exploits the imbalance by always hiding R. Strategic randomization requires the indifference and probability checks together.
