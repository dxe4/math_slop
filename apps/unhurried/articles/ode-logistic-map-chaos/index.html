Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Powers and logarithms

For a positive integer n, aⁿ means a multiplied by itself n times. For example, 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. For a nonzero base, a⁰=1 and a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. A negative exponent describes a reciprocal.

Worked example. With base 3, the logarithm asks which exponent produces a positive input. Since 3⁴=81, log₃(81)=4. More generally, for a positive base b different from 1, log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with x positive.

34=81log3(81)=4

Multiplying powers with the same base adds their exponents: 2³×2²=2⁵. For positive inputs, this becomes the logarithm rule log₂(uv)=log₂(u)+log₂(v). It is a rule about a product.

Try one. Find log₂(1/8) and explain the negative sign.

Worked answer

Because 2⁻³=1/2³=1/8, the logarithm is −3. For a base larger than 1, inputs between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms.

Watch the operation. Logarithms do not generally turn a sum into a sum: log₂(2+2)=2, while log₂(2)+log₂(2)=2 happens to agree in this case. Using 2+4 instead gives log₂(6), which differs from 1+2=3 because 2³=8.

What changes when time advances in steps?

The logistic map repeats xn+1=r xn(1−xn). Here n is an integer step, xn is a fraction in [0,1], and r is a fixed parameter. This is a discrete dynamical system, not the logistic differential equation P′=rP(1−P/K). Similar formulas do not imply similar long-term behavior. The map is a compact setting for fixed points, loss of stability and sensitivity to initial conditions.

Local background. Iteration means applying the same function repeatedly: x1=f(x0), x2=f(f(x0)). A fixed point x* satisfies f(x*)=x*. For a differentiable map, a small error e near a fixed point is approximately multiplied by f′(x*) each step. A bound |f′|<1 in a whole neighborhood proves local attraction by the mean value theorem; the derivative at one point alone only supplies the nearby candidate test when it is strictly inside or outside the unit circle.

Two parameter values, different futures

Worked example. At r=2, the nonzero fixed point is x*=1−1/2=1/2. From x0=0.2, the first steps are 0.32, 0.4352 and about 0.4916. They approach one-half rapidly. At r=3.2, the same fixed-point formula gives 0.6875, but its derivative is 2−3.2=−1.2, so small displacements from it grow while alternating sides. A numerical sequence may instead approach a two-cycle; a finite list of decimals can suggest that but cannot prove its exact period or long-term behavior.

Prove the bounded fixed-point claims

For 0≤r≤4 and x∈[0,1], x(1−x) lies in [0,1/4], so fr(x)=rx(1−x) lies in [0,1]. Thus the state interval is invariant. Solve rx(1−x)=x: either x=0, or x=1−1/r when r≠0. The nonzero fixed point lies in (0,1) exactly when r>1. Differentiating gives f′r(x)=r(1−2x), hence

fr′(0)=r,fr′(1−1/r)=2−r

For 0<r<1, zero is locally attracting because |r|<1. In fact fr(x)≤rx for x∈[0,1], so xn≤rnx0→0. For 1<r<3, |2−r|<1, so the positive fixed point is locally attracting. For 3<r≤4, |2−r|>1, and nearby displacements from that point expand initially; it is repelling. At r=1 and r=3 the multiplier has absolute value one, so this derivative criterion is inconclusive. Those boundary values need higher-order or second-iterate analysis.

The local attraction argument is exact: if |f′(x*)|<1, continuity of f′ gives a small interval around x* on which |f′|≤q<1. The mean value theorem then gives |f(x)−x*|≤q|x−x*|, keeps nearby points in the interval, and yields geometric convergence. If |f′(x*)|>1, continuity gives local expansion away from the fixed point for nonzero nearby errors; that proves it cannot attract all nearby starts.

At r=4 there is an exact identity: if x=sin²(πθ), then 4x(1−x)=sin²(2πθ). Thus angle doubling generates particular exact trajectories of the map. Nearby angles can separate quickly before folding back through the sine-square projection. This identity gives a rigorous mechanism for complicated motion, but it does not by itself prove every formal definition of chaos for every starting value. A finite cobweb plot or a table of iterates is evidence about sampled behavior, not a proof of a period-doubling cascade or universality.

Computer arithmetic also rounds states. At r=4, x0=1/2 maps to 1 and then 0 forever; other rational or rounded starts can eventually fall onto short cycles. A long numerical trace must be interpreted alongside exact mathematics and precision checks.

Calculate, then qualify

1. Stability from a multiplier

Exercise 1. For r=5/2, find both fixed points, their multipliers, and the local conclusion for each.

Hint for 1

The multipliers are r at zero and 2−r at the positive fixed point.

Worked solution for 1

The fixed points are 0 and 1−2/5=3/5. At zero, f′=5/2>1, so it repels positive nearby starts. At 3/5, f′=2−5/2=−1/2; continuity of f′ gives a local contraction neighborhood, so 3/5 attracts nearby starts with alternating error signs.

2. A false transfer from continuous time

Exercise 2. A student says that because logistic differential equations approach carrying capacity, xn+1=4xn(1−xn) must approach 3/4. Refute the claim with one exact orbit.

Hint for 2

Start at x0=1/2.

Worked solution for 2

At r=4, the positive fixed point is 1−1/4=3/4, but x0=1/2 gives x1=1, x2=0, and then xn=0 for all n≥2. This exact orbit does not approach 3/4. Also f′(3/4)=−2, so the fixed point repels nearby starts. Continuous and discrete models have different update rules.

What is established

Fixed-point calculations and derivative bounds rigorously classify selected local behavior of the logistic map. The r=4 angle identity hints at sensitive behavior, while a finite numerical picture alone cannot certify chaos or universal period doubling. This is a bounded introduction to discrete dynamics; compare continuous logistic growth to see how the time rule changes the conclusion.
