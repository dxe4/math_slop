Question and background

Carry a tangent arrow around a closed route on a sphere while keeping it locally “as parallel as possible.” When you return, the arrow can point in a different direction. The change records curvature enclosed by the loop. We will calculate it for an octant triangle.

Local capsule. A tangent vector V(s) lies in the tangent plane along a surface path γ(s). Its covariant derivative ∇γ′V is the tangential part of its ordinary derivative in surrounding space. Parallel transport means that tangential part is zero. An arrow may still rotate in ℝ³ because tangent planes themselves turn. Holonomy is the angle between the returned arrow and its starting direction after transport around a closed loop.

Angles need orientation. Choose the sphere's outward normal and traverse the boundary so the enclosed region is on the left. With the associated positive orientation, the holonomy angle in our convention equals enclosed curvature modulo 2π. Reversing traversal changes the sign.

A worked example

Worked example. Let the sphere have radius R. Use three vertices on the positive coordinate axes: A=(R,0,0), B=(0,R,0), C=(0,0,R). Join them by shorter great-circle arcs in the first octant and traverse A→B→C→A. Each side is a quarter great circle of length πR/2. Each interior angle is π/2, because the corresponding coordinate planes meet orthogonally at their axis vertex.

The triangle has area one eighth of the whole sphere: (1/8)(4πR²)=πR²/2. The curvature of a round sphere is 1/R². Therefore ∫ over the triangle KdA=(1/R²)(πR²/2)=π/2. The arrow returns rotated by π/2 modulo 2π in the chosen positive sense.

There is a second check using spherical excess: three right angles sum to 3π/2, exceeding the Euclidean value π by π/2. On a sphere of constant K=1/R², the geodesic-triangle form of Gauss–Bonnet equates this excess with area/R². The equality explains why the radius cancels; it is not an independent proof of the triangle theorem.

Transport one arrow directly

The octant also allows a direct check using the definition. Start at A with tangent arrow (0,1,0). On A→B write γ(t)=R(cos t,sin t,0), 0≤t≤π/2, and transport V(t)=(−sin t,cos t,0). This arrow stays unit and tangent. Its derivative is −γ(t)/R, normal to the sphere, so its tangential derivative is zero. At B it equals (−1,0,0).

On B→C the route lies in the yz-plane. Keep V=(−1,0,0) constant. It is perpendicular to every radius along that arc and has zero derivative, so it is parallel. At C it is still (−1,0,0). On C→A take γ(t)=R(sin t,0,cos t) and V(t)=(−cos t,0,sin t). The initial arrow matches the preceding leg, and V′(t)=γ(t)/R is normal, giving parallel transport again. At A the returned arrow is (0,0,1).

In A's tangent plane, the outward normal is (1,0,0). Rotation from (0,1,0) to (0,0,1) is therefore a positive quarter-turn. This proves the octant's π/2 holonomy directly, including continuity of the arrow at the three corners. The general area–holonomy relation still needs its stated theorem; this special transport calculation does not establish that relation for arbitrary loops.

The statement and its boundary

Conditional relation. For a positively oriented piecewise smooth simple loop bounding an oriented region D of a sphere of radius R, with parallel transport defined using the sphere's Levi-Civita connection, the returned rotation is:

Δθ≡Area(D)R2(mod2π)

This is a holonomy consequence of curvature integration; proving it generally requires the connection form or a Gauss–Bonnet argument for regions with boundary. We invoke that theorem here. For the octant, the three sides are geodesics, so their geodesic curvature is zero. Gauss–Bonnet then relates area curvature to corner turns, producing the π/2 angle found above. A rigorous proof that the arrow's rotation equals that curvature integral is deferred; the area and angle calculations are explicit.

Why modulo 2π? A direction after a full turn is indistinguishable from its original direction. Different choices of which spherical region counts as “inside” a loop can differ by the whole sphere's curvature 4π, which is also zero modulo 2π. For a self-intersecting path, a simple unsigned area is inadequate; signed multiplicity and additional care are needed.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. change the radius. Repeat the octant calculation for R=3. Find its area, K and expected holonomy angle.

Hint for exercise 1

One octant occupies one eighth of 4πR².

Full solution to exercise 1

Area=(1/8)(4π·9)=9π/2. Curvature is K=1/9. Their product is (1/9)(9π/2)=π/2. Thus parallel transport around the positively oriented octant returns the arrow rotated by π/2 modulo 2π. Scaling the sphere changes both factors but not this dimensionless angle.

Exercise 2. reverse the route. Traverse the same octant boundary A→C→B→A. What happens to the holonomy angle, and why does its magnitude stay the same?

Hint for exercise 2

Think of reversing a rotation or the signed orientation of the enclosed region.

Full solution to exercise 2

The transport map around a reversed path is the inverse of the original transport map, so its angle is −π/2 modulo 2π. The same geometric region has the same ordinary area and constant curvature magnitude; only the orientation used to assign the signed rotation changes.

What to carry forward

The octant gives an exact area, an exact spherical excess and a directly verified holonomy calculation. The general transport-curvature relation has been invoked rather than proved. It is safe to use the formula for a simple oriented loop on a round sphere with the stated connection; arbitrary intersecting loops need more careful signed accounting.

To revisit the ingredients separately, return to great-circle geodesics and Gauss–Bonnet. The former explains why the octant edges are locally straight; the latter explains why enclosed curvature controls turning.
