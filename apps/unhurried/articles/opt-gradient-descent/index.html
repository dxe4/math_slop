Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

How far should “downhill” go?

The negative gradient points in a locally decreasing direction. Yet a step can overshoot the bottom and climb the other side. On a positive quadratic, exact linear algebra says which fixed step sizes converge and how fast. This article proves that narrow result and uses a one-dimensional case so every number can be checked.

Background capsule. For a differentiable f on ℝⁿ, ∇f is the vector of partial derivatives. The Hessian is the matrix of second partial derivatives. A symmetric positive-definite matrix Q has vᵀQv>0 for every nonzero v; its eigenvalues λᵢ are positive. For f(x)=½(x−x*)ᵀQ(x−x*)+c, the gradient is Q(x−x*). An iteration chooses a new x from the current x; convergence means its error tends to zero as the iteration count grows.

A step that alternates, then a step that explodes

Worked example. Let f(x)=(x−2)². Its gradient is 2(x−2). Starting at x₀=0 with α=3/4 gives x₁=0−(3/4)(−4)=3. Then x₂=3−(3/4)(2)=1.5, and x₃=1.5−(3/4)(−1)=2.25. The errors relative to 2 are −2, 1, −1/2, 1/4: each changes sign and halves in magnitude. The objective values fall from 4 to 1 to 1/4 to 1/16.

Now take α=5/4. From x₀=0 the first point is x₁=5, farther from 2, with objective 9. The error multiplier is 1−2α=−3/2, so later magnitudes grow. “Negative gradient” describes the initial direction, not the effect of an arbitrary finite stride.

The exact quadratic guarantee

The update is xk+1=xk−α∇f(xk)

Exact statement. For the positive-definite quadratic in the capsule, a constant α with 0<α<2/λmax(Q) makes every starting point converge geometrically to x*. To prove it, write eₖ=xₖ−x*. Substitution gives eₖ₊₁=(I−αQ)eₖ. A symmetric Q has an orthonormal eigenbasis; in each eigenvector direction the scalar error is multiplied by 1−αλᵢ. The stated interval makes −1<1−αλᵢ<1 for every i. Therefore the Euclidean error shrinks at most by ρ=maxᵢ|1−αλᵢ|<1 each step. Induction gives ‖eₖ‖≤ρᵏ‖e₀‖. This is geometric, also called linear convergence of errors; it is not Newton's quadratic convergence.

The interval is sharp for a guarantee from every starting point of this fixed positive quadratic: if α=2/λmax, an error component along a largest-eigenvalue direction alternates without shrinking; above it, that component grows. The case α=0 makes no progress. A gradient norm tending to zero here is meaningful because positive definiteness gives a unique minimizer; on a general nonconvex function, small gradient alone does not certify global optimality.

A broader smooth descent inequality says that if ∇f is L-Lipschitz, then f(x−α∇f(x))≤f(x)−α(1−Lα/2)‖∇f(x)‖² for 0<α<2/L. This follows by integrating the gradient along the step and bounding its change by L times travel distance. It ensures descent at nonstationary points, but without further assumptions it does not establish convergence to a global minimum. The eigenvalue proof above supplies the stronger conclusion only for its quadratic model.

Optional investigation: which minimum does descent select?

Allow 20–30 additional minutes. This is an editorial estimate, separate from the 20-minute core lesson; it has not been timed with readers. You will use differentiation, two linear equations and a geometric sequence. The nearest-point argument explains its dot products locally.

When several coefficient pairs fit the same data, minimizing a loss leaves a choice unresolved. Question 2 in least squares gives every exact fit on the line x+y=1. Can the descent procedure itself select one point on that line? Investigate the ordinary Euclidean gradient of

f(x,y)=12(x+y−1)2

Its minimizers form L={(x,y):x+y=1}. The Hessian has both rows equal to (1,1), with eigenvalues 2 and 0. The positive-definite theorem above therefore does not apply. That missing hypothesis alone does not tell us whether these iterates converge.

1. Same loss, different starts

Use the fixed step α=1/4. Starting at (2,0), calculate two iterates. Do you expect convergence to (1/2,1/2), another point of L, or no point? What changes from (0,0)? Derive a recurrence for the residual uₖ=xₖ+yₖ−1 and find a quantity that stays unchanged. Then use them to prove a limit and find the exact interval of real fixed steps that guarantees convergence to a minimizer from every start.

Also distinguish the loss, gradient norm, distance to L, and distance to the particular minimizer (1/2,1/2). Which must tend to zero?

Hint 1 for the two starts

Differentiate f. What stays unchanged when each coordinate has the same amount subtracted from it?

Hint 2 for the two starts

Add the two coordinate updates, then subtract 1. A scalar recurrence uₖ₊₁=ruₖ gives uₖ=rᵏu₀. When does it tend to zero for every u₀?

Full solution: residual, invariant and selected limits

Put uₖ=xₖ+yₖ−1. The gradient is (uₖ,uₖ), so the coordinate updates are

xk+1=xk−αukyk+1=yk−αuk

From (2,0), u₀=1. The first two iterates at α=1/4 are (7/4,−1/4) and (13/8,−3/8). Adding the updates gives the residual recurrence. Subtracting them shows that vₖ=xₖ−yₖ is an invariant: its value is unchanged at every step.

uk+1=(1−2α)ukvk+1=vk=v0

Thus uₖ=(1−2α)ᵏu₀. It tends to zero for every start exactly when |1−2α|<1, or 0<α<1. Solving xₖ+yₖ=1+uₖ and xₖ−yₖ=v₀ gives xₖ=(1+uₖ+v₀)/2 and yₖ=(1+uₖ−v₀)/2. Hence throughout this interval,

(x∞,y∞)=(1+v02,1−v02)

At α=1/4 the residual halves each time. From (2,0), v₀=2, so the limit is (3/2,−1/2). From (0,0), v₀=0, so the limit is (1/2,1/2). The direction in which the loss is flat retains information about the start.

The loss is uₖ²/2, the gradient norm is √2|uₖ|, and the distance to L is |uₖ|/√2. All three tend to zero for 0<α<1. Distance to a specified minimizer need not: from (2,0), the distance to (1/2,1/2) tends to √2. A statement about “distance to the minimum” must say which distance it means.

The interval is an all-start guarantee. At α=1, every nonzero residual alternates without shrinking, producing a two-cycle. At α>1 or α<0, its magnitude grows. At α=0, an off-line start never moves. The exception throughout is u₀=0: a start already on L has zero gradient and stays fixed for every real α.

2. Why that particular point?

For 0<α<1, show that the selected point is the unique point of L nearest the start z₀=(x₀,y₀) in Euclidean distance. Why does zero initialization then select the minimum Euclidean norm solution, even though no penalty was added to f?

Hint for the nearest-point claim

Write the limit as p=z₀−(u₀/2)(1,1). If q and p both lie on L, what is the sum of the coordinates of q−p? Compare that direction with z₀−p.

Full solution: perpendicular displacements and Pythagoras

The limit formula can be written p=z₀−(u₀/2)(1,1), where u₀=x₀+y₀−1. Its coordinates sum to 1, so p lies on L. For any other q on L, the coordinates of q−p sum to zero.

The dot product of two vectors (a,b) and (c,d) is ac+bd; a zero dot product means the vectors are perpendicular. Thus (q−p)·(1,1)=0. Since z₀−p is a multiple of (1,1), the displacements z₀−p and p−q are perpendicular. Expanding the squared length of their sum gives Pythagoras:

‖z0−q‖2=‖z0−p‖2+‖p−q‖2

The last term is nonnegative and is zero only when q=p. Therefore p is the unique point of L nearest z₀; f still has infinitely many minimizers. The distance from z₀ to L is ‖z₀−p‖=|u₀|/√2, which also justifies the distance formula used above.

For z₀=(0,0), distance from the start is the Euclidean norm ‖q‖=√(qₓ²+qᵧ²). The selected point (1/2,1/2) therefore has the smallest norm of all points on L. This selection comes from the update and initialization, without changing the objective.

3. Change the observation

Change only the observation in the loss to

f~(x,y)=12(x+2y−1)2

Start again at (2,0). Derive the gradient, a recurrence for uₖ=xₖ+2yₖ−1, and a linear combination of xₖ and yₖ that stays unchanged. Find the all-start convergence interval and the selected limit. Test α=1/4 and α=3/4, both of which were safe in the previous investigation. Include boundary exceptions and explain which point zero initialization selects.

Hint for the changed observation

The gradient points along (1,2). Which linear combination of the two coordinate updates cancels a displacement in that direction? Add the first update to twice the second to track the residual.

Full solution: a new invariant and a new step interval

The gradient is (x+2y−1)(1,2). Thus xₖ₊₁=xₖ−αuₖ and yₖ₊₁=yₖ−2αuₖ. The residual and the newly derived invariant vₖ=2xₖ−yₖ obey

uk+1=(1−5α)ukvk+1=vk=v0

Every start converges to a minimizer exactly when |1−5α|<1, giving 0<α<2/5. From (2,0), u₀=1 and v₀=4. At the limit, solve x+2y=1 and 2x−y=4: the second equation gives y=2x−4, so 5x−8=1. The selected point is (9/5,−2/5).

For any start the limit is p=z₀−(u₀/5)(1,2). It lies on the new minimizing line, and every displacement q−p along that line satisfies (q−p)·(1,2)=0. The same Pythagorean proof makes p the unique nearest point to z₀. In particular zero initialization selects the minimum norm point (1/5,2/5).

At α=1/4 the residual multiplier is −1/4: residuals alternate and shrink. The first two iterates are (7/4,−1/2) and (29/16,−3/8). At α=3/4 the multiplier is −11/4. From this start the residual magnitude grows, so the iterates diverge and the objective grows by the factor 121/16 each step. Changing the observation requires a new step interval.

At α=2/5 a nonzero residual alternates without shrinking. Above 2/5 or below 0 it grows; at α=0 an off-line start stays put. A start already satisfying x+2y=1 stays fixed for every real step size. Saying only that the Hessian is not positive definite would establish none of these conclusions.

Choose a stride from the curvature

1. Calculation. For f(x)=3(x+1)², find the fixed-step convergence interval, then calculate two iterates from x₀=1 using α=1/12.

Hint for question 1

The Hessian is 6; track error relative to −1.

Full solution for question 1

The Hessian eigenvalue is 6, so 0<α<2/6=1/3. Here 1−6α=1/2. Initial error is 1−(−1)=2, so successive errors are 1 and 1/2. Thus x₁=0 and x₂=−1/2. Directly, ∇f(1)=12 and 1−(1/12)12=0, agreeing with the error calculation.

2. Failure check. For the same f, what happens with α=1/3 from x₀=1? Is the failure numerical accident?

Hint for question 2

Compute 1−αλ exactly.

Full solution for question 2

The error multiplier is 1−(1/3)6=−1. Starting error 2 alternates between 2 and −2, so the iterates alternate between 1 and −3. Both have objective 3·2²=12. This exact cycle is the boundary case of the theorem, not rounding error.

What the model proves

For a positive-definite quadratic, curvature sets a precise safe interval and an exact contraction factor. Outside that class, a descent inequality may survive while global convergence does not. A step-size plot illustrates behavior but cannot replace the eigenvalue argument. The next challenge is when a feasible region blocks the negative-gradient direction; multipliers describe that geometry.
