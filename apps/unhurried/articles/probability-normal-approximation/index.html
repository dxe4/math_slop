The question and needed ideas

A long run of success counts often makes a mound of bars. A smooth bell curve can help estimate areas under those bars, but a discrete count cannot literally have a continuous normal distribution. The useful question is when the smooth curve captures a binomial count well enough for the task and when it misses important tail behavior.

Background capsule. For a Binomial(n,p) count, μ=np and σ=√[np(1−p)]. The standardized score Z=(X−μ)/σ tells how many standard deviations X lies from its mean. A standard normal variable has mean zero and variance one. Standardization requires 0<p<1 so that σ>0. A continuity correction treats an integer count k as the interval from k−0.5 to k+0.5 on the continuous scale.

Work through the model

Worked example. Take 100 independent trials with p=1/2. Then μ=50 and σ=5. For X≤60, the continuity-corrected normal sketch uses P(N≤60.5) with N having mean 50 and standard deviation 5. This is P(Z≤(60.5−50)/5)=P(Z≤2.1), approximately 0.9821 from a standard normal table. An exact binomial sum would be ∑ from k=0 to 60 of C(100,k)/2¹⁰⁰; the normal value is an approximation to that exact finite sum. Evaluating the sum gives about 0.9823999, while the corrected normal estimate is about 0.9821356; their difference is roughly 0.0002643 here. This close match belongs to this particular central event and parameter choice, not to every binomial tail.

The half-unit correction matters because a bar at 60 covers a width around 60; cutting the smooth curve at 60 would omit half of that represented bar. It still cannot make the approximation exact. A table or calculator can evaluate the finite binomial sum if a precise tail chance matters.

Try p=0.02 with n=20. Its mean is 0.4 and most mass sits at zero; the count cannot go negative. A symmetric normal curve centered at 0.4 assigns some area to negative values and misses the skew. That is a visible failure case, despite n being greater than one.

The statement and its limits

Qualified theorem. For independent Bernoulli trials with one fixed p strictly between zero and one, as n tends to infinity the standardized count converges in distribution to a standard normal variable. This is a special case of the central limit theorem. “Converges in distribution” means probabilities at continuity points of the limiting curve approach the corresponding normal probabilities; it does not make any finite-n distribution exactly normal.

Why the standardization? Write X as a sum of n success indicators. Each indicator has mean p and variance p(1−p). Independence makes the sum mean np and variance np(1−p), so subtracting and dividing yields mean zero and variance one. This algebra motivates the scale. A full proof of the central limit theorem requires an additional limiting argument and lies beyond this article; the theorem is stated with its conditions, not proved by a histogram.

Common rules of thumb ask both np and n(1−p) to be reasonably large, often around ten, before using a normal approximation. Such thresholds are heuristics, not guarantees of a particular numerical error, especially deep in a tail. If p changes with n toward zero, the fixed-p theorem does not directly settle the accuracy; a Poisson-type approximation may be more suitable.

Z=X−npnp(1−p)

Try two cases

Exercise 1. For X∼Binomial(64,1/2), find the mean, standard deviation, and continuity-corrected z cutoff for P(X≤36).

Hint for exercise 1

Use μ=np, σ=√[np(1−p)], and include the full bar at 36.

Full solution to exercise 1

μ=64/2=32 and variance is 64(1/2)(1/2)=16, so σ=4. The corrected upper boundary is 36.5; its z score is (36.5−32)/4=1.125. Thus a normal sketch estimates P(X≤36) by Φ(1.125), about 0.870. The exact chance remains a finite binomial sum.

Exercise 2. For n=30 and p=0.01, explain two reasons a bell curve centered at np is a poor sketch.

Hint for exercise 2

Calculate np and consider the left edge of a count.

Full solution to exercise 2

np=0.3, so success counts cluster at zero with a long right tail. The count has lower bound zero, while the symmetric normal curve centered at 0.3 gives area to negative values. Also np is far below common heuristic thresholds, indicating strong skew and sparse successes. The fixed-p limiting theorem does not promise accuracy for this small finite n.

What to carry forward

Use the normal curve as a labelled approximation to a well-specified binomial model, with the mean, spread and continuity correction shown. Exact binomial arithmetic remains the reference for finite counts. The extreme-p example is a boundary, not an exception to probability itself. Return to the exact count formula whenever tail accuracy or model assumptions are in doubt.
