Can a bounded sequence avoid settling anywhere?

The sequence 0, 1, 0, 1, … never converges, but its zero terms form a convergent subsequence. This is not an accident of having only two values. Every bounded sequence of real numbers has some convergent subsequence. We will construct one by repeatedly choosing an interval that contains infinitely many terms.

Background capsule. A subsequence keeps terms in their original order, with strictly increasing indices n₁ < n₂ < …. A sequence is bounded if all its values lie between some finite a and b. The nested-interval property says a decreasing sequence of nonempty closed real intervals whose lengths tend to zero has exactly one common point. It follows from completeness: the increasing left endpoints have a supremum, which remains inside every interval. We will use that property explicitly.

Keep the busy half

Worked example. Consider xn = (−1)n + 1/n for n ≥ 1. All its terms lie in [−1, 2]. Bisect that interval into [−1, 1/2] and [1/2, 2]. Infinitely many terms must lie in at least one half; otherwise the whole sequence would have only finitely many terms. For this sequence, the even terms approach 1 and eventually occupy the right half. Keeping that half, then repeatedly bisecting, concentrates on a cluster near 1. Choosing a term with an index larger than the last chosen index at every stage gives a genuine subsequence, not a rearrangement.

There can be a tie: both halves may have infinitely many terms. Either choice works. Closed halves overlap at their shared midpoint, and that causes no problem. The construction asserts a possible convergent subsequence, not a unique one; choosing the negative terms here leads to −1 instead.

Construct the convergent subsequence

Bolzano–Weierstrass in ℝ. If every xn belongs to [a,b] with real a ≤ b, there are indices n₁ < n₂ < … and a real c such that xnₖ → c.

[ a,b]⊃I1⊃I2⊃⋯,|Ik|=b−a2k→0

Proof. Put I₀ = [a,b]. Given a closed interval Ik containing infinitely many sequence terms, split it into two closed halves. At least one half contains infinitely many terms; choose it as Ik+1. The intervals are nested and their lengths are (b−a)/2k. By the nested-interval property they share one point c. For each k, choose an index nk larger than nk−1 with xnₖ ∈ Ik. Infinitely many terms in Ik ensure such an index exists even after finitely many earlier choices.

Because both xnₖ and c lie in Ik, |xnₖ−c| ≤ (b−a)/2k. Given ε > 0, choose K large enough that this length is below ε; then all k ≥ K satisfy the limit requirement. If a = b, every term is a and the result is immediate. This is a proof in the real line; we have not shown the analogous statement in an arbitrary metric space.

The real-number hypothesis matters. Rational approximations to √2 inside [1,2] form a bounded rational sequence whose convergent subsequences in ℝ must tend to √2. None has a limit in ℚ. That is another face of the rational gap. The theorem also does not claim the original sequence converges: alternating 0 and 1 is a simple failure case.

Choose and diagnose subsequences

1. Calculate. For xn = (−1)n + 1/n, give two explicit subsequences and prove their limits.

Hint

Take even and odd indices separately, then bound the reciprocal terms.

Worked solution

For n = 2k, x2k = 1 + 1/(2k) → 1 because 1/(2k) < 1/k → 0. For n = 2k−1, x2k−1 = −1 + 1/(2k−1) → −1. For any ε > 0, choose K > 1/ε; then the reciprocal errors are below ε for all k ≥ K. The two different limits also prove the full sequence does not converge.

2. Find the flaw. A proposed proof says, “Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence because it has an infinite set of distinct values.” Why does this fail, and what repairs it?

Hint

What if values repeat? Even infinitely many distinct values need a limit point in the domain.

Worked solution

The constant sequence 3,3,3,… has only one distinct value yet has a convergent subsequence. Conversely, having infinitely many distinct rational values does not ensure a rational accumulation point, as approximations to √2 show. The repair is the nested-interval construction in ℝ: it keeps infinitely many indexed terms at each stage and invokes real completeness for a common point.

Boundedness gives one settled thread

Repeated bisection and completeness find a convergent subsequence without claiming anything about the whole sequence. A useful stopping test is whether you can identify both the “infinitely many terms” invariant and the real-number property that supplies c. The optional next question is what stronger condition makes all terms approach the same c.
