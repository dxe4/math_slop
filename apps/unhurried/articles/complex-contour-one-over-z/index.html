A loop that remembers a missing point

The integrand 1/z is smooth along a circle centered at zero, yet its integral around that closed circle is not zero. The singularity inside the loop matters. A direct parameter calculation gives the answer and makes orientation visible without invoking a contour theorem prematurely.

Background capsule. A piecewise continuously differentiable path γ:[a,b]→ℂ has a contour integral ∫γf(z) dz=∫abf(γ(t))γ′(t)dt, with ordinary real-variable integration applied to real and imaginary parts. A counterclockwise circle of radius r>0 has γ(t)=reit, 0≤t≤2π, where eit=cos t+i sin t. Its derivative is ireit. The path must avoid points where the integrand is undefined.

Do the entire integral on the parameter

Worked example. For γ(t)=2eit, division gives γ′(t)/γ(t)=i for every t. Therefore ∮|z|=2 dz/z=∫02π i dt=2πi. Radius 2 disappears from the quotient. Radius 1 or 7 gives exactly the same result. Running backward, γ(t)=2e−it, gives γ′/γ=−i and the integral −2πi.

A circle centered at 3 with radius 1 does not surround zero. On it, 1/z has a logarithm branch on a neighborhood of the closed disk, so its integral is zero by the fundamental theorem for contour integrals. More elementarily, one can expand 1/(3+eit) as a uniformly convergent geometric series and integrate terms; every resulting nonconstant eint term averages to zero. This comparison shows that the value is not simply a property of circular shape.

Relate the calculation to a continuous angle. Along the centered circle we can track a real angle t from 0 to 2π without wrapping it back to zero. The quotient dz/z=i dt records its incremental turning. Integrating adds all those increments, giving 2πi. The point on the plane returns to its start, but the tracked angle has gained one full turn. A globally single-valued logarithm would have to return to its original value too; this calculation shows why its derivative 1/z cannot have such a primitive throughout the punctured plane.

Orientation and winding

For every r>0, the direct calculation proves the exact statement

∮|z|=rdzz=2πi

The orientation is counterclockwise. Reversing any parametrized contour changes the sign: substitute the reversed parameter into the defining real integral. Traversing the same centered circle twice counterclockwise gives 4πi, not 2πi, because the parameter interval covers two turns.

For a general closed piecewise smooth curve γ avoiding zero, the integer (1/2πi)∫γ dz/z is its winding number around zero. Informally, it counts net turns. The full theorem that this quantity is an integer for every such curve needs a continuous lifted angle or equivalent argument; we state that theorem here and prove only the circular cases by parameterization. A self-crossing curve can have winding 0, 1, −1, or larger; “inside” is not a reliable replacement for the integer on arbitrary curves.

The tempting claim “a holomorphic-looking integrand integrates to zero around a closed path” fails here because 1/z is not holomorphic at the point zero enclosed by the centered circle. Cauchy's theorem requires holomorphy on an appropriate region containing the contour and its interior, or an equivalent deformability condition. It cannot be applied across this hole. A numerical quadrature can illustrate the answer but cannot repair that missing hypothesis.

Use the parameter, then test the domain

1. Calculate. Let γ(t)=5e−2it for 0≤t≤2π. Evaluate ∫γ dz/z.

Hint for 1

Differentiate γ, divide by γ, and count both direction and number of turns.

Worked solution for 1

γ′(t)=−10ie−2it, so γ′(t)/γ(t)=−2i. Integrating from 0 to 2π gives −4πi. The path makes two clockwise turns; radius 5 has no effect.

2. Explain. Why is ∮ dz/z on the circle |z−1|=1 not a valid ordinary contour integral? Would “half of 2πi” be justified?

Hint for 2

Check whether z=0 lies on the parametrized path and whether 1/z has a finite value there.

Worked solution for 2

The circle passes through zero. At that path parameter the integrand is undefined and its parameter integrand has a singularity. The ordinary contour integral therefore is not defined. A principal value or indentation prescription would be a separate construction with extra choices; simply assigning half the centered-circle value has no basis in this definition.

Know where the function is defined

Parameterization proves the circular result, including its sign and repeated traversals. The general winding-number statement broadens the idea, but it requires its own theorem. A pole on the path invalidates the ordinary integral, and a pole inside prevents a naive use of Cauchy's zero-integral theorem.
