Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

When does a swinging sketch act like a sine wave?

A digital-clock illustration shows a weight swinging on a light string. It is tempting to animate its angle with a perfect sine wave. That can be a defensible approximation for small swings, but it is not the exact law of a pendulum. We can state the physical assumptions, derive the nonlinear equation, and see precisely where the linear shortcut enters.

Background capsule. Angle θ is measured in radians; 0 is straight down. A dot or prime indicates a time derivative: θ′ is angular velocity in radians per second and θ″ is angular acceleration in radians per second squared. For a sine wave θ(t)=θ₀cos(ωt), differentiating twice gives θ″=−ω²θ. The familiar small-angle relation sin θ≈θ works when |θ| is small in radians. Radians are dimensionless, so sin θ and θ can be compared.

Assume a point mass m on a massless rigid string of length L, no friction, constant gravitational acceleration g, and motion in one vertical plane. These idealizations omit air resistance, a bulky weight and support motion. We ask about the angle over time, not the sound of the clock.

Compare two amplitudes

Worked example. For L=1 m and g=9.81 m/s², the linear model has angular frequency √(g/L)≈3.13 radians per second and period 2π√(L/g)≈2.006 s. This period is independent of amplitude inside that approximation. The restoring acceleration in the exact model, however, uses sin θ rather than θ. At θ=0.1 rad, sin(0.1)≈0.09983, only about 0.17% below 0.1. At θ=1 rad, sin(1)≈0.84147, about 15.85% below 1. The difference in restoring acceleration is much larger at the bigger angle.

Derive the boundary of the approximation

The tangential gravitational force is −mg sin θ, where the minus sign points toward θ=0. Tangential acceleration on a circular path of radius L is Lθ″. Newton's second law gives mLθ″=−mg sin θ. Cancelling positive m and dividing by L yields the exact ideal-pendulum equation. Replacing sin θ by θ gives the linear approximation:

θ″+gLsin(θ)=0⇒θ″+gLθ≈0

The arrow marks approximation, not logical equivalence. Taylor's theorem gives sin θ=θ−θ³/6+R₅ with |R₅|≤|θ|⁵/120 for real θ. Thus the first omitted term is cubic; for |θ|≤0.2 rad, |sin θ−θ|/|θ|≤θ²/6≤0.00667, about 0.67%, using the alternating-series or standard Taylor bound. This bounds the restoring-term error at a position, not the trajectory error over arbitrarily long times.

The linear equation has solution θ(t)=C cos(ωt)+D sin(ωt), ω=√(g/L), because differentiating twice returns −ω² times the function. Constants C and D come from initial angle and angular velocity. The exact nonlinear pendulum generally has an amplitude-dependent period, so the linear formula is a purposeful local approximation.

Use and challenge the approximation

1. Calculate. With L=2 m and g=9.8 m/s², find the linear angular frequency and period. Check the units.

Hint for question 1

g/L has units s⁻²; its square root has units s⁻¹.

Full solution to question 1

g/L=9.8/2=4.9 s⁻², so ω=√4.9≈2.214 s⁻¹. The period is 2π/ω≈2.838 s. The radian angle is dimensionless; ωt must be dimensionless, which agrees with ω in inverse seconds. This period belongs to the linear small-angle model.

2. Explain a failure. Someone uses sin θ≈θ at θ=π/2 to predict restoring acceleration. Compare the exact and linear magnitudes in units of g/L and say whether the table's small-angle evidence justifies this use.

Hint for question 2

Compute sin(π/2) and compare with π/2.

Full solution to question 2

The exact magnitude is (g/L)sin(π/2)=g/L. The linear model gives (g/L)(π/2)≈1.571g/L, an overestimate of about 57.1% relative to exact. π/2≈1.57 rad is far outside the checked small range, so using the approximation here is unjustified. Both models still point toward the bottom, but their magnitudes differ substantially.

A precise place to stop

The ideal pendulum equation follows from Newton's law under stated mechanical assumptions. The sine-wave solution follows only after a small-angle replacement. We bounded the local restoring-term error; we did not prove a long-time path error bound.
