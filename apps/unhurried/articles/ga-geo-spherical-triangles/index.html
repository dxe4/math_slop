Can a triangle have three right angles?

On a sphere, start at the north pole, travel down the prime meridian to the equator, then a quarter of the way around the equator, then back to the pole along a second meridian. The three route segments form a triangle, and each corner is a right angle. The result challenges the habit of treating Euclidean angle sum as part of the word “triangle.”

Background capsule. A sphere of radius R consists of points (x,y,z) with x²+y²+z²=R². A great circle is the intersection with a plane through the centre. Its shorter arcs are spherical geodesics. A latitude circle away from the equator is a small circle, not a geodesic. The angle between two curves on a surface is the angle between their tangent directions in the tangent plane at their meeting point. The unit sphere has surface area 4π; radius R has surface area 4πR².

Build the octant explicitly

Worked example. Use N=(0,0,R), A=(R,0,0), and B=(0,R,0). NA lies in the plane y=0, NB lies in x=0, and AB is a quarter of the equator z=0. All three are great-circle arcs. At A, the equatorial tangent points in the y direction, while the meridian tangent points in the z direction, so the angle is π/2. The same coordinate argument works at B. At N, the two meridians arrive in perpendicular x and y tangent directions, again giving π/2.

AreaπR²/2One of eight congruent octants

The area statement can be checked without a surface integral: the three coordinate planes divide the sphere into eight congruent patches, and rotations or reflections exchange them. Each gets one eighth of 4πR². The triangle is one such patch on the positive x,y,z side. Its area is positive even though its angle sum exceeds π.

What the octant proves

Exact instance and full verification. For N, A and B as above, with shorter great-circle arcs,

∠N+∠A+∠B=3π2

Each arc is geodesic because its containing plane passes through the centre. At N the tangent directions to the two meridians are along x and y and are perpendicular. At A the tangent to NA lies in the vertical z direction, while the tangent to AB lies along y; at B the analogous pair is z and x. Dot products of each pair vanish, giving three exact right angles. This proves the displayed angle sum for the octant, not for every spherical triangle. The area follows by eightfold symmetry, as in the table.

For a general spherical geodesic triangle on a radius-R sphere, an angle-excess theorem gives Area=R²(α+β+γ−π) for a suitable simple triangle, with conventions about which region and arcs are chosen. Its general proof, often via spherical lunes or Gauss–Bonnet, is deferred here. Our octant confirms the formula in one case: R²(3π/2−π)=πR²/2. A single confirmation is not a derivation of the general theorem.

Great-circle segments are essential. If we replace AB with a latitude arc above the equator, that arc is not a geodesic and the simple excess relation no longer applies without a boundary-curvature term. Likewise a spherical “line” can have several shortest routes between antipodal endpoints; choosing the shorter arc avoids that ambiguity for our vertices, which are separated by only a quarter great circle.

Reason with tangents and scale

1. Calculation. A sphere has radius 2. Find the three angle measures, their sum, and the area of the octant triangle N-A-B.

Hint for question 1

Changing radius changes surface area but not angles. Use one eighth of 4πR².

Full solution for question 1

Each angle remains π/2, so the sum is 3π/2. The whole sphere has area 4π·2²=16π, and the octant has area 16π/8=2π. Equivalently πR²/2=π·4/2=2π.

2. Failure test. A traveller follows a circle of latitude at 60° north and calls it a spherical straight line because it looks horizontal on a map. Explain the flaw.

Hint for question 2

Does the plane of that latitude circle pass through the sphere's centre?

Full solution for question 2

The 60° latitude lies in a plane above the centre, so it is a small circle rather than a great circle. It is not a spherical geodesic. Map horizontality is a feature of the projection, not of shortest-path geometry. The equator is the one latitude that is a great circle.

One complete counterexample

The octant gives a fully checked spherical triangle with three right angles, angle sum 3π/2 and area πR²/2. It is an explicit counterexample to applying the Euclidean π-sum to all triangles. We have proved this instance from coordinate tangents and symmetry; the general spherical excess theorem remains outside the lesson. To analyze a new spherical route, first confirm that its sides are great-circle arcs. The word “straight” must be read within the surface's metric, not from a flat map.
