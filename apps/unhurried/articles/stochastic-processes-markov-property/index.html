Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

How much history must a state remember?

A music box displays Calm or Bright. The displayed state is Markov if, given today, earlier labels do not change tomorrow's distribution. A relevant hidden phase can invalidate that description. The property belongs to a state description and probability rule, not merely to randomness.

Background capsule. P(A|B) conditions on positive-probability B. Xₜ is the state at time t; a history records compatible X₀,…,Xₜ₋₁. Given Xₜ, the Markov equality says this history does not alter Xₜ₊₁'s distribution. Successive states need not be independent.

We use a discrete-time model: one tick per day. We can make the rule time-homogeneous by using the same next-state probabilities every day, but the Markov property itself does not require constant probabilities across days.

Two histories, one visible label

Worked example. First define a genuine two-state rule: after Calm, tomorrow is Bright with chance 1/4; after Bright, tomorrow is Calm with chance 1/2. The daily choice uses a new independent random draw. Whether yesterday was Calm or Bright, if today is Calm the chance of Bright tomorrow is 1/4. Past labels may help predict today's label before we see it, but once today is known, they add nothing under this stipulated rule.

Now consider a different, entirely deterministic music box whose hidden display sequence repeats Calm, Calm, Bright, Calm, Calm, Bright, and so on. The displayed label Calm occurs twice in each cycle. After the first Calm, tomorrow is Calm; after the second Calm, tomorrow is Bright. Knowing only “today is Calm” is insufficient, while knowing yesterday distinguishes the positions: Bright then Calm identifies the first Calm; Calm then Calm identifies the second. With a uniformly random initial phase, both histories occur with positive probability, so the conflicting conditional predictions are genuine.

“Calm” is the same visible label in both rows. Thus it cannot support one next-step probability that agrees with both histories. Sampling many sequences would reveal the difference, but the stipulated transition rule already proves it.

State the condition precisely

P(Xt+1=j|Xt=i, history)=P(Xt+1=j|Xt=i)

The equality must hold for every time, next state and compatible positive-probability history. Zero-probability histories do not supply ordinary conditional probabilities. In the first music box, the next random draw and today's label generate tomorrow, so yesterday cannot change its conditional chance. In the three-phase box, today's label leaves an unresolved phase, and yesterday changes the prediction. This is a direct counterexample, not an inference from a plot.

A repair is to call the state (visible label, phase), with phase 0, 1 or 2. The next phase is then determined by the current phase. An even smaller state can be “first Calm,” “second Calm,” or “Bright.” On these three states the rule is Markov and deterministic. Expanding the state can restore the property, but may make a model more complex than the question warrants.

Boundary. The Markov property describes one-step conditional distributions. It neither asserts independence of states nor guarantees convergence to a stationary distribution. The deterministic three-phase cycle is Markov after phase augmentation and still cycles forever. Also, a process may be Markov with a rule that changes with t; a single fixed transition matrix then would not represent every day.

Optional investigation: when can hidden states safely share a label?

One rule that works however the chain started

The music-box cycle shows that merging hidden states can create memory. Now ask the positive question: when does a grouped label keep enough information for one common transition matrix, regardless of the initial distribution?

Extra background. For a finite time-homogeneous Markov chain, P(x,z) is the probability of moving from hidden state x to hidden state z in one step. Every entry is nonnegative and every row sums to 1. Partition the hidden states into disjoint blocks B₁,…,Bᵣ that cover all states. The visible state Zₜ records only the block containing Xₜ. A grouped transition probability into a block adds all P(x,z) with z in that block. A starting distribution specifies the hidden state at time 0; it is not inferred from a report.

We seek one common, time-homogeneous matrix Q that describes Z for every initial hidden distribution. The same Q must remain valid after every positive-probability visible history. Finding a one-step average for one start is a weaker statement.

Compare two hidden states inside U

Group A and B into visible U, and C into visible V. In the equal-totals model, A's row is (1/2,0,1/2), B's is (0,1/2,1/2), and C's is (1/2,1/2,0). A and B have different hidden destinations but both send probability 1/2 into each visible block. Predict what happens to the next-V probability if the initial law is (w,1−w,0), first at w=0 and then at w=1. Now change only B's row to (0,3/4,1/4). Can one U row still work for both starts?

For equal block totals, the common matrix has U row (1/2,1/2) and V row (1,0). For unequal totals, starting at A gives next-V probability 1/2 while starting at B gives 1/4. Both visible starts are U, so one common U row cannot reproduce both. For the mixture (w,1−w,0), its one-step next-V probability is w/2+(1−w)/4=1/4+w/4. Choosing one value of w does not remove the disagreement across starts.

The block row-sum criterion

Claim. For a fixed finite stochastic matrix P and a fixed partition, one common time-homogeneous grouped matrix Q works for every initial hidden law if and only if, for each source block Bᵢ and target block Bⱼ, the following sum has the same value for every hidden x in Bᵢ:

Qij=∑z∈BjP(x,z)for every x∈Bi

Necessity: start at one hidden state. Suppose that one common Q works for every initial law. Start with probability 1 at x∈Bᵢ. The initial visible block is certainly i, so its probability of moving to j must be Qᵢⱼ. The hidden chain gives that probability as the displayed sum. Starting instead at any x′ in the same block must give the same Qᵢⱼ. Thus the block sums agree. This argument uses point-mass starts, not a stationary mixture.

Sufficiency: condition on any compatible history. Suppose the row sums agree, and use their common values to define Q. Its entries are nonnegative and each row sums to 1 because the target blocks partition all hidden destinations. Fix any initial law and any positive-probability visible history ending in block i. Conditional on that history, the hidden state has some probability weights aₓ inside Bᵢ. These weights can depend on the whole history. The hidden Markov property and total probability give

Pr(Zt+1=j|history ending at i)=∑x∈BiaxQij=Qij

The final equality uses ∑aₓ=1. Every possible hidden mixture gives the same next-block row, so the history adds no prediction beyond the current visible block. The matrix is independent of time and the initial law. Histories with probability zero are excluded before forming these conditional probabilities. No stationarity, irreducibility or convergence assumption is needed for this finite proof.

This property is called strong lumpability. Wolfer and Watanabe, §3.4, Theorem 3.1 states the row-sum characterization; footnote 8 distinguishes its independence of the starting law. Their paper works with irreducible chains. The elementary finite proof above establishes the precise common-Q statement used here without requiring irreducibility.

A special start can still work when the universal criterion fails

Failure of the criterion means that one common Q cannot work for every start. It does not say that the grouped process fails to be Markov for every particular start. To make that distinction concrete, use a third matrix P*, separate from both explorer presets:

A and B have next-V chances 1/4 and 3/4, so the universal row criterion fails. But start with hidden law (p/2,p/2,1−p), for any p∈[0,1]. Whenever the visible state is U at time 0 with positive probability, its hidden conditional mixture is half A, half B. Under P*, that mixture moves to hidden (A,B,C) with probabilities (1/4,1/4,1/2). Conditional on staying in U, it is again half A, half B. If the visible state was V, the hidden state was C, whose next U mixture is also half A, half B.

Induction over any positive-probability visible history now preserves this conditional half/half mixture whenever its last block is U. Therefore next V has chance (1/2)(1/4)+(1/2)(3/4)=1/2 after every such U history, and next U has chance 1 after V. The grouped process has Q with rows (1/2,1/2) and (1,0) throughout this family of starting laws. This is an exact all-history argument, not a conclusion from a finite sample of paths. It does not supply the common-Q guarantee for all other starts. Question 4 changes the start while keeping P* fixed.

Test the state you were given

1. Calculation. In the two-state random music box, today is Calm. What is the chance of Bright tomorrow? If yesterday was Bright, what is that chance under the same rule?

Hint for question 1

Use the stipulated Calm row; ask whether yesterday enters the rule.

Full solution to question 1

The Calm row assigns 1/4 to Bright tomorrow. The fresh draw uses today's state and does not consult yesterday, so the conditional chance remains 1/4 after adding “yesterday was Bright.” This equality is the Markov check. It would not follow merely from knowing a marginal chance of Bright on an arbitrary day.

2. Diagnose and repair. In the Calm, Calm, Bright cycle, why is the visible label not a sufficient state? Give a sufficient finite state.

Hint for question 2

Compare what happens after the two different Calm positions.

Full solution to question 2

A visible Calm can mean the first Calm, followed by Calm, or the second Calm, followed by Bright. Yesterday's label distinguishes them, so the next-state conditional distribution changes with the history. Labeling the three phases “first Calm,” “second Calm” and “Bright” repairs this: each phase has a determined next phase. The augmented process is Markov but periodic.

Hint for question 3

Compare a's and b's total probabilities of entering {a,b} and {c,d}. Then compare c and d. Moving mass within a target block leaves its total unchanged; moving it across blocks can alter that total.

Full solution to question 3

Both a and b have block totals (3/4,1/4), while both c and d have totals (1/2,1/2). Thus Q has U row (3/4,1/4) and V row (1/2,1/2), common to every initial distribution by the theorem. The first change replaces b's row with (1/4,3/8,0,3/8). Its next-V total is 3/8, but a's remains 1/4. Starting at a or b then gives conflicting next-block probabilities, so no common Q works for every initial law. This verdict does not by itself decide every particular start.

The second change replaces b's row instead with (3/8,3/8,0,1/4). Its block totals remain (3/4,1/4); the other rows are unchanged. Hence the same Q survives. The criterion compares totals into blocks, not equality of every hidden destination probability.

4. Change the start, keep P*. Use the special-start matrix P* above, but now start at hidden A with probability 1. Compare two visible histories at time 2: UUU and UVU, whose letters give Z₀,Z₁,Z₂. Find each history's probability, its conditional hidden law at time 2, and the conditional chance of V at time 3. Does this start give a Markov grouped process? Explain why the answer does not contradict the half/half family proved above.

Hint for question 4

Propagate unnormalized A/B/C probability masses along each history, discarding paths whose visible block does not match. Divide by the history's total mass only at the end. For UVU, the middle hidden state must be C, so its return row helps. Compare predictions for two histories ending in the same visible state at the same time.

Full solution to question 4

After UUU, the unnormalized hidden masses at time 2 are (1/4,3/16,0). For example, the B mass comes from A→A→B with probability (1/2)(1/4)=1/8 and A→B→B with probability (1/4)(1/4)=1/16. The history probability is 7/16, so its conditional hidden law is (4/7,3/7,0). Its next-V probability is (4/7)(1/4)+(3/7)(3/4)=13/28.

For UVU, A first reaches C with chance 1/4; C then returns to A or B with probability 1/2 each. The terminal masses are (1/8,1/8,0), the history probability is 1/4, and its conditional hidden law is (1/2,1/2,0). Next V therefore has chance 1/2. Both histories have positive probability and end in U at time 2, yet 13/28≠1/2. Earlier visible labels change the next-step prediction, so this start does not give a Markov grouped process, even if a rule were allowed to depend on time. Starting at A is outside the family (p/2,p/2,1−p); the family's hidden-mixture induction is unaffected. No conditional distribution has been formed on an impossible history.

What a state promises

A Markov state contains enough information for the next-step probability calculation under the chosen model. The test compares histories that reach the same proposed state. A successful one-step test does not by itself validate the model against data, and it says nothing about long-run settling. The transition matrix packages a fixed Markov rule into rows after a state is chosen.
