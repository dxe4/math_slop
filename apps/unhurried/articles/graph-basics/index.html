Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

What survives when a map is redrawn?

A transit map often straightens a winding line and moves stations to make labels fit. The drawing may change radically while the list of directly connected stations stays fixed. Graph theory studies that list of connections. It can answer whether a route exists, but the drawing alone cannot tell us travel time, compass direction, or distance.

Background capsule. A finite set is a collection in which each named object occurs once. An unordered pair {a,b} records two objects without choosing a first one. For this lesson a simple undirected graph has a finite vertex set V and an edge set E consisting of unordered pairs of distinct vertices. There are no loops from a vertex to itself and no duplicate edges. We call two vertices adjacent when their pair belongs to E. These conventions matter: other graph models allow arrows, loops, or several edges between the same two vertices.

Suppose a tiny museum has rooms A, B, C, D, with doors AB, AC, BC, and CD. Can we describe exactly the same door network without a floor plan? Yes: record the four pairs and nothing about where a room sits on paper.

Turn doors into a checkable ledger

Worked example. For the museum, let V={A,B,C,D} and E={{A,B},{A,C},{B,C},{C,D}}. The adjacency list says A: B,C; B: A,C; C: A,B,D; D: C. This is a worked conversion: every edge appears in the lists of both endpoints. Conversely, read only the entries after A, then the previously unseen entries after B and C, and recover exactly the four unordered pairs. Recording both directions does not create eight doors; AB and BA name the same edge.

Place A at the top of a page or at the bottom: the ledger stays the same. Even a line crossing in a sketch need not be a station or junction. Only an explicitly named vertex creates a meeting point. This is why an adjacency list is often safer than an artistic map when deciding what the mathematical object is.

A precise object and its limits

Definition. For a finite simple undirected graph, its edges satisfy the following containment, where the notation on the right means all two-element subsets of V:

G=(V,E),E⊆{{u,v}:u,v∈V,u≠v}

Representation claim. An adjacency list that names every vertex, lists only vertices in V, contains no self entry, and lists v beside u exactly when it lists u beside v determines one such graph. To justify it, form E by taking the unordered pair {u,v} from each list entry. Symmetry makes the reverse entry the same pair; excluding self entries prevents loops. Every pair in E appears in the list by construction, so moving from pairs to lists and back loses no connection information. This is a complete argument under the finite, simple, undirected convention.

Consider instead two different corridors between A and B. The simple graph records only {A,B}, so it cannot distinguish one corridor from two. A multigraph gives those corridors separate edge identities. Likewise, a one-way escalator requires a directed edge. If walking length matters, assign edge weights as additional data. The choice of model follows the question; adding distance to a drawing without declaring weights does not make a shortest-distance claim valid.

A graph can still carry an isolated vertex. If room E has no doors, E belongs to V and its adjacency list is empty. Leaving E off the list changes the graph, even though no edge mentions it. This small detail becomes consequential when counting components or asking whether every room is reachable.

Build and challenge a model

Exercise 1. A club has four members P,Q,R,S. P knows Q and R; Q knows S; R and S know no one else. Treat knowing as mutual. Give V, E, and a complete adjacency list. How many members have no contacts?

Hint for exercise 1

Write each unordered pair once, then put each partner in both endpoint lists. A member can have an empty list.

Full solution to exercise 1

V={P,Q,R,S} and E={{P,Q},{P,R},{Q,S}}. The lists are P: Q,R; Q: P,S; R: P; S: Q. All four members have at least one contact, so the number with no contacts is zero. The phrase about R and S rules out an RS edge; it does not erase their listed contacts.

Exercise 2. A diagram shows two lines crossing without a dot at the crossing. One person reports five vertices, another reports four. Explain the disagreement and give one piece of model data that would settle it.

Hint for exercise 2

Ask whether the crossing is meant to be a place where a route may change from one line to the other.

Full solution to exercise 2

The geometric crossing alone does not define a vertex. If it is a junction, name it, add it to V, and split each passing edge into edges incident with it; five vertices may then be correct. If the lines merely cross on the page, there are four vertices and no transfer there. An explicit vertex list and edge list, or a statement that the crossing is a junction, settles the model. A graph drawing by itself can be ambiguous.

Keep the connections, state the convention

A simple graph is its named vertices and unordered edge pairs. The adjacency list is another exact record of those pairs when it is symmetric and includes isolated vertices. A drawing helps the eye, but shape and apparent intersections do not add mathematical connections. Stop here if you can translate between edge sets and adjacency lists and can say which real-world facts this simple model discards. To inspect a route next, use walks, trails, and paths.
