Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Why can an arc be straight?

In the Poincaré disk model of the hyperbolic plane, many geodesics look like circular arcs on the page. “Straight” means locally shortest according to the model's distance, not straight according to a ruler laid over the screen. The boundary circle marks ideal points infinitely far away; it is excluded from the space.

Background capsule. A point is z=(x,y) with x²+y²<1. Write |z|=√(x²+y²), and |dz| for a tiny Euclidean displacement. A metric assigns its length: here the scale factor depends on location. A path's length is the integral of these tiny lengths, or a limit of fine sums. The symbol ln means natural logarithm, the inverse of ex. You can use the exact radial-distance formula below without following its optional integral derivation. A geodesic is a locally distance-minimising path for that metric. Familiar Euclidean circles meet orthogonally when their tangents make right angles at intersection.

Walk radially toward the rim

Worked example. Follow a radius from the origin to Euclidean radius r. Along that path |dz|=dt, so its hyperbolic length is ∫₀ʳ 2/(1−t²) dt = ln((1+r)/(1−r)). At r=1/2 this is ln 3, about 1.099. At r=9/10 it is ln 19, about 2.944. The Euclidean gaps to the rim are respectively 1/2 and 1/10, yet the hyperbolic distances from the centre grow without bound as r approaches 1.

The metric and the geodesic rule

Exact model definition. On the open unit disk, the curvature −1 Poincaré metric is

ds=21−|z|2|dz|,|z|<1

The radial calculation is a full derivation for that one path: 2/(1−t²)=1/(1−t)+1/(1+t), so integrating gives −ln(1−r)+ln(1+r). Its limit is infinite as r→1. This shows the boundary is infinitely far along radial paths. The model's distance to the boundary is infinite more generally, but that broader statement is not proved solely by one radial calculation.

Geodesic identification, with proof boundary. Complete geodesics in this metric are diameters and arcs of Euclidean circles that meet the unit boundary at right angles. A diameter such as the horizontal axis is visibly symmetric under reflection, and the radial length formula shows its natural distance scale. More general orthogonal arcs arise from disk isometries carrying a diameter to an arc. Establishing that these transformations preserve the metric and that every geodesic is of this form requires a derivation beyond this lesson; we use the standard model theorem here rather than claiming the picture proves it. The result concerns complete geodesics; a short portion of one is also geodesic locally.

Angles in the disk drawing are Euclidean angles because the metric multiplies every direction at one point by the same positive scale factor. Lengths are not Euclidean: the factor 2/(1−|z|²) changes from point to point. This combination makes the model visually useful and easy to misread. The drawn boundary is neither a geodesic nor a set of ordinary points of the hyperbolic plane.

Calculate, then inspect a false shortcut

1. Calculation. Find the hyperbolic radial distance from the origin to radius 3/5. How much farther is that than the distance to radius 1/2?

Hint for question 1

Use ln((1+r)/(1−r)); subtract logarithms by dividing their arguments.

Full solution for question 1

At r=3/5, the ratio is (8/5)/(2/5)=4, so distance is ln 4. At r=1/2 it is ln 3. The added hyperbolic distance is ln 4−ln 3=ln(4/3), even though the added Euclidean radius is only 1/10.

2. Explanation. A student says a point at radius 0.99 is only Euclidean distance 0.01 from the boundary, so the boundary is nearby in hyperbolic distance. Diagnose the error.

Hint for question 2

What happens to the scale factor as |z| approaches one?

Full solution for question 2

The student has changed metrics mid-argument. The hyperbolic scale factor 2/(1−r²) diverges near r=1. From the centre to 0.99 the radial distance is ln(199), and as r→1 the radial distance diverges. The remaining radial distance from 0.99 to the boundary is infinite, despite its Euclidean coordinate gap of 0.01.

Read the drawing through the metric

The disk displays a complete infinite geometry inside a bounded Euclidean circle. Its geodesics are diameters or boundary-orthogonal arcs, and its radial distances grow logarithmically without bound toward the rim. The radial formula was derived here; the general geodesic classification was stated with its model-theorem dependency. A screen circle that merely looks orthogonal is not an exact proof of geodesicity. Before interpreting any diagram, identify its line definition and length rule; then its apparent curvature can be separated from the geometry it represents.
