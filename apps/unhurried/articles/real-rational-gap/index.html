A sequence that asks for a missing number

Imagine working only with rational numbers: fractions of integers with nonzero denominators. You can bracket a positive number whose square is 2 as tightly as you like using decimals, yet no rational number fills the gap. This is a useful way to see what the completeness of the real numbers supplies. We will prove the missing-number claim, then say exactly which extra property the real line has.

Background capsule. An upper bound of a set is a number at least as large as every member. Its least upper bound, or supremum, is an upper bound smaller than every other upper bound. A sequence is Cauchy if its late terms get arbitrarily close to one another, even before we know its limit. We need only integer divisibility, elementary inequalities, and the fact that between distinct real numbers lies a rational number. We take the existence and least-upper-bound property of the real numbers as axioms here; constructing them from rationals is a separate project.

Build rational brackets

Worked example. Start with 1² < 2 < 2². Testing tenths gives 1.4² = 1.96 < 2 < 2.25 = 1.5². Testing hundredths gives 1.41² = 1.9881 < 2 < 2.0164 = 1.42². These calculations certify an interval, not an exact decimal representation. At the next stage, divide the current bracket into ten equal rational pieces and keep the pair that straddles the square value 2. After stage n, the bracket width is 10−n. Its left endpoints increase, its right endpoints decrease, and any two later choices lie at most 10−n apart.

The brackets are a concrete worked example of Cauchy behavior in the rationals. Their widths tend to zero, but that fact alone cannot conjure a rational limit. To see why, we need an exact obstruction.

Where completeness enters

Exact statement. Let S be the set of nonnegative rational x with x² < 2. It is nonempty and bounded above. It has no least upper bound within the rationals. In the real numbers, its supremum exists and is the unique positive real whose square is 2:

sup{x∈ℚ:x≥0,x2<2}=2∉ℚ

First prove irrationality. Suppose the positive square root were p/q in lowest terms, with positive integers p and q. From p² = 2q², p² is even, hence p is even: an odd integer has odd square. Put p = 2k. Then 4k² = 2q², so q² = 2k² and q is also even. Both p and q would share a factor 2, contradicting lowest terms. Thus no rational square is 2.

For any rational a ≥ 0 with a² < 2, a slightly larger rational still has square below 2. One explicit choice is a+h with positive rational h smaller than both 1 and (2−a²)/(2a+1), because (a+h)² ≤ a²+h(2a+1) < 2. So no member of S is its maximum. If a rational upper bound b had b² > 2, a sufficiently small rational step left would still have square greater than 2, and would remain an upper bound: for positive x, x² < 2 < (b−h)² implies x < b−h. Thus b would not be least. No rational has square exactly 2, which exhausts the possibilities. This proves the rational failure without assuming the desired real limit.

In ℝ the least-upper-bound axiom gives c = sup S. The same small-step arguments show neither c² < 2 nor c² > 2 is possible, so c² = 2. Strict increase of x² on nonnegative numbers makes c unique. The bracket endpoints above converge to c because both surround c and their gap tends to zero. The completeness axiom does real work; the decimal table merely guides it.

Test the boundary

1. Construct a bracket. Find two hundredths that bracket the positive solution of x² = 3, and check both squares exactly.

Hint

Try neighboring hundredths near 1.73; convert each to a fraction with denominator 100 before squaring.

Worked solution

We have 1.73² = 29929/10000 = 2.9929 < 3 and 1.74² = 30276/10000 = 3.0276 > 3. Thus the solution lies strictly between 1.73 and 1.74. This calculation alone does not prove that its decimal expansion has any particular later digits.

2. Repair a claim. Someone says, “Every bounded increasing sequence of rationals converges in ℚ.” Identify the missing assumption or counterexample.

Hint

Use lower endpoints of successively finer brackets for √2. Ask what a rational limit would have to square to.

Worked solution

The lower endpoints are rational, bounded above by 2, and nondecreasing. In ℝ they converge to √2: the bracket gap tends to zero and always contains √2. Were their limit rational, uniqueness of limits in ℝ would make that rational equal √2, contradicting the parity proof. The correct monotone-convergence statement needs completeness of the ambient real numbers; boundedness inside ℚ alone is insufficient.

The property behind the gap

A list of rational approximations can become arbitrarily self-consistent while its limit lies outside ℚ. Completeness says bounded nonempty real sets have real suprema; it does not say every rational approximation eventually equals its target. The next optional connection is why bounded real sequences contain convergent subsequences, where completeness appears again in nested intervals.
