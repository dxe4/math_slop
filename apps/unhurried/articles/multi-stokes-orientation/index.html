One loop, different spanning surfaces

A circle can bound a flat disk or a curved cap. Stokes' theorem says circulation around the oriented loop equals the normal component of curl integrated over a suitable spanning surface. The boundary orientation must match the surface normal, and the field must be smooth where the chosen surface lies.

Background capsule. For F=(P,Q,R), curl is (Ry−Qz, Pz−Rx, Qx−Py). A surface normal n gives a positive side. Looking from the tip of n toward the surface, the compatible boundary direction is counterclockwise. A surface integral of curl·n dS adds local rotation through tiny oriented area patches. The cross product of tangent vectors gives an oriented area element for a parametrized surface.

Compute the disk and its rim

Worked example. Let F(x,y,z)=(-y/2,x/2,0), and let S be the unit disk z=0 with upward normal (0,0,1). Its boundary is r(t)=(cos t,sin t,0), 0≤t≤2π, traversed counterclockwise from above. On the boundary, F=(-sin t/2,cos t/2,0) and r′=(-sin t,cos t,0). Their dot product is 1/2, so circulation is ∫₀²π1/2 dt=π.

The curl is (0,0,1), constant everywhere. On the upward disk curl·n=1, and the disk has area π, so the surface integral is π. Reverse the normal to downward and the induced positive rim direction becomes clockwise; both integrals become −π. If one changes only the normal but keeps the old rim traversal, the signs disagree because the orientations are incompatible.

Calculate the cap's flux directly as a check. Away from its equator, the upper hemisphere is the graph z=√(1−x²−y²). Its upward oriented area vector is (−zx,−zy,1) dx dy. Dotting this with curl F=(0,0,1) leaves dx dy, so integration over the projected unit disk gives π. The graph derivatives become unbounded at the equator; one can calculate first on projected radius ρ<1 and let ρ increase to 1. The result depends on projected oriented area, explaining why the hemisphere's larger surface area does not produce a larger flux for this vertical curl.

The theorem and what this example establishes

General statement. For an oriented piecewise smooth surface S with piecewise smooth boundary and a C¹ field on a neighborhood of S, Stokes' theorem equates circulation around the induced oriented boundary with the flux of curl through S. For our planar disk, the identity reduces to Green's theorem because the vertical component of curl is Qx−Py. The direct calculations above verify this example; they are not a proof for every curved surface.

∮∂SF·dr=∬S(∇×F)·ndS

To see a second spanning surface without integrating a difficult normal, use the upper hemisphere x²+y²+z²=1, z≥0, with outward/upward normal. Its boundary is the same unit circle, counterclockwise when viewed from above. Since F is smooth throughout both surfaces and Stokes applies to each, both curl fluxes equal the same boundary circulation π. For arbitrary admissible fields, this same-boundary conclusion invokes the general theorem for the curved cap; the graph calculation above additionally verifies the cap value directly for our selected constant curl. The two surfaces need not have equal area or matching normals point by point.

A failure case is a field singular on one proposed spanning surface. The punctured-plane vortex can be integrated along a circle, but an attempt to span it with a disk through the origin violates smoothness. Choosing a different surface does not allow a hidden singularity to be ignored; verify a valid field neighborhood and compatible orientation first.

Trace the orientation

1. Calculation. Replace F by G=(-y,x,0) on the upward unit disk. Compute circulation and curl flux.

Hint for question 1

G is twice the worked field.

Worked solution for question 1

Along the rim, G(r(t))=(-sin t,cos t,0)=r′(t), so the integrand is 1 and circulation is 2π. Curl G=(0,0,2), so upward flux is 2 times disk area π, also 2π.

2. Explanation. A student chooses the downward normal but walks the rim counterclockwise from above, then reports opposite signs as a failure of Stokes. Repair the setup.

Hint for question 2

Use the right-hand orientation rule to choose the boundary direction.

Worked solution for question 2

Downward normal induces clockwise rim travel when viewed from above. Reverse the boundary path, giving circulation −π for the worked field. Curl flux with downward normal is also −π. Keeping counterclockwise travel pairs the boundary of one orientation with the surface of the other and does not match the theorem.

The loop carries its orientation

For the smooth field and upward disk, both sides equal π. A different valid spanning surface inherits the same boundary total through the general theorem, not because its area equals the disk's. Stop by checking three items in order: the field is defined and C¹ near the surface, the surface normal is chosen, and the boundary traversal matches it. Those checks prevent most apparent sign paradoxes.
