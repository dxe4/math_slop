How long until the next chime?

In a toy arrival process, visitors appear at constant rate λ and counts in disjoint intervals are independent. Let T be the time from now until the next arrival. Counting zero events before time t is the same event as waiting longer than t. This simple equivalence turns a Poisson count formula into an exponential waiting-time formula.

Background capsule. A survival probability P(T>t) is the chance a nonnegative waiting time exceeds t. For a Poisson process, N(t) counts events in the next t time units and P(N(t)=0)=e⁻λᵗ. The rate λ is events per time unit, so 1/λ has units of time. The cumulative probability P(T≤t) is one minus survival. An integral is an area under a curve; here integrating the exponential density would recover cumulative probability, but the zero-count argument needs no integration.

We specify the start of waiting independently of future arrivals. A random calendar time in a changing-rate process need not have the same rule.

Wait for a model museum visitor

Worked example. Suppose λ=2 per hour. The chance of waiting more than half an hour is e^(−2 × 1/2)=e⁻¹≈0.3679. The chance of an arrival within half an hour is 1−e⁻¹≈0.6321. The average waiting time in this model is 1/λ=1/2 hour, but the chance of waiting longer than that average is still about 36.8%. An average is not a deadline.

Now suppose half an hour has passed with no arrival. The chance of waiting at least another quarter hour is P(T>3/4 | T>1/2)=e⁻²(3/4)/e^(−2 × 1/2)=e^(−1/2)≈0.6065. It equals the chance of a new wait exceeding a quarter hour. The elapsed half hour changes no future Poisson increment; this is the memoryless property. It can feel odd, but it is an exact consequence of the specified process.

Derive survival and memorylessness

P(T>t)=P(N(t)=0)=e−λt

For s,t≥0, the event T>s+t is contained in T>s. Therefore P(T>s+t | T>s)=P(T>s+t)/P(T>s)=e⁻λᵗ. This also equals P(T>t). The derivation uses the Poisson zero-count law and λ constant; the independent-increments axiom explains why a fresh interval follows the same process rule. It is not a universal property of all waits.

The expected wait is 1/λ. One way to see it uses the tail-area identity E[T]=∫₀∞P(T>t)dt for nonnegative continuous T. Substituting e⁻λᵗ yields ∫₀∞e⁻λᵗdt=1/λ for λ>0. The integral evaluates because the antiderivative is −e⁻λᵗ/λ and the exponential tends to zero. This calculation justifies the stated mean under the model; it does not estimate λ from any data.

Failure case. Suppose a chime rings at exactly each whole hour and you start just after one. The wait is nearly one hour, whereas starting just before one gives a nearly zero wait. Elapsed clock position matters. Even if the number of chimes per hour averages one, the wait is not exponential and the memoryless equation fails. A variable arrival rate likewise changes the survival calculation to a rate-dependent expression. The simple formula requires the homogeneous Poisson process, not merely an average count.

Calculate a wait and test a claim

1. Calculation. Chimes form a Poisson process at rate four per hour. Find P(T>15 minutes) and P(T≤15 minutes). What is the mean waiting time?

Hint for question 1

Convert 15 minutes to 1/4 hour before multiplying by the rate.

Full solution to question 1

λt=(4/hour)(1/4 hour)=1. Thus P(T>15 minutes)=e⁻¹≈0.3679 and P(T≤15 minutes)=1−e⁻¹≈0.6321. The mean is 1/λ=1/4 hour, or 15 minutes. The mean wait and the 15-minute survival threshold happen to coincide here, but the survival probability is not one half.

2. Conditional wait. At rate four per hour you have already waited 30 minutes. Under the same process, what is the chance of waiting at least 15 more minutes? Explain why “already waited longer than average” does not change it.

Hint for question 2

Use the ratio of survival probabilities at 45 and 30 minutes.

Full solution to question 2

The ratio is e^(−4 × 3/4)/e^(−4 × 1/2)=e⁻¹≈0.3679. Equivalently, the next 15-minute increment has zero arrivals with chance e⁻¹, independent of the earlier empty interval. A long past wait does not increase or decrease the chance of a chime in the next interval under the homogeneous Poisson assumptions. A scheduled or time-varying process would not support this answer.

The count-wait connection

“Wait exceeds t” and “zero arrivals in t” name the same event. That is why a Poisson process produces an exponential first wait. The calculation is exact for the model and fragile outside it: rate units, constant intensity and independent increments matter. Read Poisson counts again if the zero-count formula or its assumptions are unclear.
