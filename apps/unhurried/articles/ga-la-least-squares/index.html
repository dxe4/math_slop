What is the best answer when the equations disagree?

Repeated measurements rarely lie exactly on a proposed line. We can still choose coefficients that make the total squared discrepancy as small as possible. The key geometric fact is that, at the best fit, the leftover error points perpendicular to every change the model can make.

Background capsule. For a real m×n matrix A and data column b in Rᵐ, a model prediction is Ax. The residual is r=b−Ax. Its squared Euclidean length is ||r||²=r·r, the sum of squared coordinate errors. Aᵀ is the transpose: rows and columns interchange, and (Au)·v=u·(Aᵀv). The column space of A is all possible model predictions. Full column rank means its n columns are independent, so Az=0 forces z=0. This article proves the normal-equation criterion and uniqueness under that hypothesis.

Fit one value to three observations

Worked example. Suppose measurements are b=(1,2,6) and the model predicts the same constant c at all three times. Then A is the column (1,1,1)ᵀ, x=c, and Ax=(c,c,c). The squared discrepancy is (1−c)²+(2−c)²+(6−c)². Setting the normal equation AᵀAc=Aᵀb gives 3c=9, hence c=3. The prediction is (3,3,3) and residual is (−2,−1,3). Its dot product with the model direction is −2−1+3=0.

The total is 14. At c=4, residuals are (−3,−2,2), giving 9+4+4=17. The example suggests that c=3 is better, but checking two candidates is not proof of a global minimum. The argument below supplies that proof for every real c and every full-column-rank matrix.

Orthogonality proves the minimum

Exact theorem, with proof. If real A has full column rank, there is a unique x̂ minimizing ||b−Ax||² over all x in Rⁿ; it is the unique solution of

ATAx^=ATb

First, full column rank makes AᵀA invertible: if AᵀAz=0, dot with z to obtain ||Az||²=0, so Az=0 and then z=0. A square matrix with trivial kernel has an inverse, hence the equation has a unique solution x̂. Put r̂=b−Ax̂. The equation says Aᵀr̂=0, equivalently r̂·Az=0 for every z. For any alternative x=x̂+z, its residual is r̂−Az. Expanding its squared norm gives ||r̂−Az||²=||r̂||²+||Az||²−2r̂·Az=||r̂||²+||Az||². This is at least ||r̂||², and equality under full column rank requires z=0. That proves the unique global minimum.

The orthogonality statement actually survives when columns are dependent: any minimizer has residual perpendicular to the column space, and a fitted prediction is unique. Coefficients may then be nonunique. For A with two identical columns (1,1)ᵀ, coefficients (1,0) and (0,1) give the same prediction; AᵀA cannot be inverted. Thus “solve by (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb” requires full column rank. In serious numerical work, squaring the condition number by forming AᵀA can amplify roundoff; QR or SVD methods are preferred. Our exact derivation does not make a numerical stability claim.

Check fit and failure

1. Calculation. Fit a constant c to data (2,4,9). Find c, the residual, its squared norm, and show the residual is orthogonal to (1,1,1).

Hint for question 1

The normal equation is 3c=2+4+9.

Full solution for question 1

3c=15, so c=5. The prediction is (5,5,5), residual b−Ax=(−3,−1,4), and squared norm 9+1+16=26. The residual dot (1,1,1) is −3−1+4=0. If c changes by t, the new squared error is 26+3t², so 5 is the unique minimizer.

2. Explanation. Let A=[[1,1],[1,1]] and b=(1,1). A student computes AᵀA and tries to invert it to prove a unique coefficient pair. Give all exact fits and explain why this does not contradict the theorem.

Hint for question 2

The two columns are equal. Express Ax in terms of x₁+x₂.

Full solution for question 2

Ax=(x₁+x₂,x₁+x₂). Every pair with x₁+x₂=1 fits exactly, including (1,0) and (0,1). The columns are dependent and AᵀA=[[2,2],[2,2]] has determinant zero, so it is not invertible. The theorem's full-column-rank hypothesis fails; the prediction (1,1) is still unique, but the coefficient pair is not.

Best fit means perpendicular leftover

Under full column rank, the normal equations choose one coefficient vector and the Pythagorean identity proves it is the global minimum. The perpendicular residual is an exact geometric certificate, not just a visual impression. If columns repeat or nearly repeat, check rank before claiming unique coefficients or stable floating-point answers. Singular values help reveal which directions a matrix compresses and why fitting can become numerically fragile.
