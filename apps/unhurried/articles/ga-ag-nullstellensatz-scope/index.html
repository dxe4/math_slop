Can points recover equations?

The equations x=0 and x²=0 have the same points over ℂ, yet the ideals (x) and (x²) differ. The Nullstellensatz says precisely what point data can recover: the radical of an ideal, under an algebraically closed field hypothesis. We will state that result, prove its one-variable case, and exhibit a failure over ℝ.

Background capsule. In k[x1,…,xn], an ideal I is closed under addition and multiplication by any polynomial. Its zero set Vk(I) consists of all k-coordinate tuples where every element of I vanishes. For a set S of points, I(S) consists of all polynomials vanishing on every point of S. The radical √I contains polynomials f for which some positive power fm belongs to I. A field is algebraically closed if every nonconstant one-variable polynomial has a root in it; ℂ is the standard example, while ℝ is not.

A single point, two ideals

Worked example. Work in ℂ[x]. Both I=(x) and J=(x²) vanish exactly at 0, because x²(a)=0 if and only if a=0 in a field. Yet x∉(x²), so I≠J. Their radicals agree: √(x²)=(x). To see this directly, if a polynomial f has f(0)=0, it is divisible by x, so f² is divisible by x² and f∈√(x²). Conversely, if f(0)≠0, then every power fm also has nonzero constant term and cannot be divisible by x². Thus the radical retains exactly the vanishing condition in this example.

Now switch to ℝ[x]. The ideal K=(x²+1) has no real zero, because x²+1>0 for all real x. Therefore Vℝ(K)=∅, and every polynomial vanishes vacuously on all points of that empty set: I(∅)=ℝ[x]. But √K is a proper ideal; for example 1 has no power inside K, since K contains no nonzero constant multiple of 1. In fact √K=K: the quotient ℝ[x]/(x²+1) is the field ℂ, so K is prime and therefore radical. Hence I(Vℝ(K))≠√K. This is the promised failure of the algebraically closed-field formula over ℝ.

The theorem and the proof boundary

Hilbert's Nullstellensatz, geometric form, stated. If k is algebraically closed and I is an ideal of k[x1,…,xn] for finite n, then

I(Vk(I))=I

The inclusion √I⊆I(Vk(I)) is elementary over any field: if fm∈I and a is a common zero of I, then f(a)m=0. A field has no nonzero nilpotents, so f(a)=0. The reverse inclusion is the deep direction. It says that if a polynomial vanishes at every common k-point, a power of it lies in I. We prove that direction for one variable below; the several-variable direction remains an invoked Nullstellensatz theorem. The Stacks Project gives the theorem as the intersection of maximal ideals containing a radical ideal. Over an algebraically closed field, maximal ideals in a polynomial ring correspond to k-points, giving the displayed geometric form.

One should distinguish “same set of classical points” from “same ideal.” The theorem identifies radical ideals with algebraic zero sets through mutually reversing operations V and I. It does not claim that every ideal equals I(V(I)): (x²) is the smallest counterexample. An ideal can retain nilpotent or multiplicity information invisible to point sets. Conversely, the real-field example shows that even taking a radical may not recover an ideal from real points. Real algebraic geometry has a different, stronger radical notion; it is outside our scope.

For a zero-set calculation, always say which field supplies the coordinates. The notation V without a subscript can obscure the issue; here Vk makes it explicit. Continue to Spec to see how a ring retains the (x²) distinction beyond its visible point set.

Prove the theorem in one variable

Weak Nullstellensatz for k[x]. If k is algebraically closed, every proper ideal I of k[x] has a common zero in k. Here is a complete proof. If I=(0), every point is a common zero. Otherwise choose a nonzero g∈I of least degree and divide it by its leading coefficient to make it monic. For any f∈I, polynomial division gives f=qg+r with either r=0 or deg r<deg g. Since r=f−qg belongs to I, minimality forces r=0. Thus I=(g). Properness excludes a nonzero constant g, since such a constant is invertible and would put 1 in I. Algebraic closure gives a root a of the nonconstant g. Every f∈I is a multiple of g, so every such f vanishes at a. This proves the claim.

The same calculation also proves the full radical formula in this one-variable setting. For a proper nonzero ideal, factor its monic generator as g=∏(x−ai)eᵢ, with distinct ai and positive integers ei. Such a factorization follows by repeatedly removing a root using polynomial division; the degree decreases at each removal. The common zero set of I is exactly {ai}. Put q=∏(x−ai), using each distinct factor once.

A polynomial f vanishes at every ai exactly when q divides f. To see the product divisibility, remove one factor using the factor theorem. At any other root aj, the removed factor has nonzero value, so the remaining quotient must still vanish there. Continue through the distinct roots. Consequently I(V(I))=(q).

If q divides f, choose M at least every ei. Then g divides fM, so f lies in √I. Conversely if fM∈I, evaluating at each ai gives f(ai)M=0, hence f vanishes there and q divides f. Therefore √I=(q)=I(V(I)).

The boundary ideals also fit. For I=k[x], the zero set is empty and both sides are k[x]. For I=(0), the zero set is k and both sides are zero: an algebraically closed field is infinite, and a nonzero polynomial has at most its degree many roots by successive division. For completeness, a finite field could not be algebraically closed, since ∏a∈k(x−a)−1 is nonconstant and has no root in that field. Thus no hidden finiteness exception is being used. This completes the one-variable radical formula; the several-variable hard direction remains a separate theorem.

Test ideals against points

1. Calculation. In ℂ[x], find Vℂ((x−2)³), its radical, and I of that zero set.

Hint for question 1

A power vanishes exactly when its base vanishes. Divide polynomials by x−2 to detect vanishing at 2.

Full solution for question 1

The only zero is 2, so Vℂ((x−2)³)={2}. A polynomial vanishes at 2 exactly when x−2 divides it, giving I({2})=(x−2). The radical of ((x−2)³) is also (x−2): if f has that factor then f³ lies in ((x−2)³); if f(2)≠0 then no power is divisible by x−2. The three results agree with the theorem.

2. Explanation. Why does (x²+1) in ℝ[x] disprove the same geometric formula with k=ℝ?

Hint for question 2

First find real roots, then compute I(∅) from its definition.

Full solution for question 2

It has no real roots, so Vℝ((x²+1))=∅ and I(∅)=ℝ[x]. Yet 1∉√(x²+1), because no power of 1 is a multiple of x²+1. Thus the right side is proper while the left side is the whole ring. ℝ lacks the algebraically closed hypothesis.

Recover the radical, under a hypothesis

Over an algebraically closed field, classical zero sets recover radical ideals, not arbitrary ideals. The easy containment follows from evaluating powers; the hard containment is proved here in one variable, while its several-variable form remains an invoked theorem. Stop once you can explain the (x) versus (x²) comparison and produce the real-field counterexample without relying on a picture.
