Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

What changes when growth meets a limit?

Exponential growth assumes a fixed growth rate per individual, no matter how large a population becomes. A logistic model makes that rate shrink linearly with population P: P′=rP(1−P/K), where r>0 has units per time and K>0 has population units. K is called a carrying capacity in the model. It is a fitted parameter under stable conditions, not a universal biological constant.

Local background. P′ is the population's instantaneous rate of change. A zero of the right-hand side is an equilibrium. The sign of P′ says which way a solution moves. Separation of variables means rearranging a nonzero solution so one side involves P and the other t; any division by P or K−P must leave the omitted equilibria for separate checking.

A population with room to grow

Worked example. Take r=0.4 per year, K=1000 and P(0)=200. The formula derived below gives P(t)=1000/[1+4e−0.4t]. Initially P′=0.4·200·0.8=64 individuals per year. At t=(ln 4)/0.4≈3.47 years, e−0.4t=1/4 and P=500. The population is then halfway to K, where this model predicts its largest growth rate, rK/4=100 individuals per year.

Compare equal fractions at different scales. At P=K/4 the per-individual growth rate is P′/P=3r/4, while at P=3K/4 it is r/4. The larger population has the smaller rate per individual. Its total growth is nevertheless the same in both cases, 3rK/16, because the larger number of individuals offsets their slower individual rate. Distinguishing total growth from per-individual growth prevents a misleading interpretation of the capacity factor.

Derive the curve and its limits

For P>0 with P≠K, rearrange the equation: K dP/[P(K−P)]=r dt. Since K/[P(K−P)]=1/P+1/(K−P), integration yields ln|P|−ln|K−P|=rt+C. Solving and applying P(0)=P0>0 gives, on the branch where its denominator remains nonzero,

P(t)=K1+(KP0−1)e−rt

Substitution verifies the formula and initial value. The excluded constant solutions P=0 and P=K also satisfy the original equation. For 0<P0<K, the denominator exceeds 1 for all real t and the curve rises from near zero to K. For P0>K, the forward-time denominator stays positive and the curve falls to K; extending it backward may meet a zero denominator, so no global-in-time claim follows.

Differentiate the rate rule along a solution: P″=r(1−2P/K)P′. On a rising solution with 0<P<K, P′>0, so concavity switches from upward to downward exactly as P crosses K/2. This identifies the S-curve's inflection. A solution starting above K is already decreasing; the same verbal S-curve description is inappropriate there.

The sign table proves forward attraction to K for positive starting populations when combined with the explicit formula. Near P=0, a small positive population grows away, so zero is unstable on the nonnegative state space. Near K, small positive or negative departures move back, and the formula shows convergence. This scalar conclusion is about the logistic differential equation, not the discrete logistic map, whose stepwise iteration can behave very differently.

Local uniqueness also follows because f(P)=rP(1−P/K) is a polynomial and therefore locally Lipschitz. The separation calculation by itself would be incomplete if it silently discarded P=0 or K. In empirical use, changing resources, migration and random births can break the rule; fitting r and K from a short run does not prove long-run capacity.

Use signs and formulas

1. Find the midpoint time

Exercise 1. For K=600, r=0.3 per day and P(0)=150, find the first time P reaches 300 and its rate then.

Hint for 1

The coefficient K/P0−1 is 3. At P=K/2 the denominator is 2.

Worked solution for 1

P(t)=600/[1+3e−0.3t]. Setting P=300 gives 3e−0.3t=1, so t=(ln 3)/0.3≈3.66 days. At P=300, P′=0.3·300·(1−1/2)=45 individuals per day.

2. Check a generalization

Exercise 2. A student claims that every positive logistic solution is an increasing S-curve. Give a counterexample and explain its forward behavior.

Hint for 2

Choose P0>K and inspect the sign of 1−P/K.

Worked solution for 2

With P0=2K, P′(0)=r(2K)(1−2)=−2rK<0. The explicit solution is P(t)=K/[1−(1/2)e−rt] for t≥0, so it decreases toward K from above. It is neither increasing nor the usual rising S-curve.

What is learned

The logistic rule gives two equilibria, a solvable positive trajectory and a clear maximum growth rate at K/2 for a rising population. Its value lies in a transparent assumption about density-dependent growth, not in a guarantee about every population. The fold model shows a different way equilibria can change as a parameter moves.
