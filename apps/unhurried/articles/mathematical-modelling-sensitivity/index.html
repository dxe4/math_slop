Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Which mural assumption moves the answer?

Two people disagree about a mural estimate. One worries that tile side length was measured inaccurately; the other worries about wasted pieces. “Both matter” is true but too vague for a purchasing decision. A sensitivity check changes an input by a declared amount while holding the other inputs fixed, then compares the output. It is a question about the chosen model, so its conclusions must retain that scope.

Background capsule. A function y = f(x) gives an output for an input. A percentage change divides a change by its starting value: from 20 to 22 is 2/20 = 10%. A derivative is a limiting rate of change for very small input changes. We can do finite comparisons without derivatives and use a derivative only to explain a local approximation. Counts can be rounded for purchasing after the model calculation.

Let a rectangular panel have fixed area A in m². Square tiles have side a in metres. Let w be the fraction of extra tiles ordered for breakage, with w ≥ 0. Our continuous procurement estimate is N(A,a,w) = (A/a²)(1+w). We assume full coverage, a stable waste fraction, and the same tile size throughout.

Compare changes on the same scale

Worked example. Set A = 6 m², a = 0.20 m, and w = 0.10. The baseline is 6/0.04 × 1.10 = 165 estimated tiles. Raise waste from 10% to 20% while keeping a fixed: the output is 150 × 1.20 = 180, an increase of 15 tiles or about 9.09% of the baseline. Raise tile side from 0.20 to 0.22 m while keeping waste at 10%: the output is 6/(0.22²) × 1.10 ≈ 136.36, a decrease of 28.64 or about 17.36%.

These changes are not equally sized in relative terms. Waste rate doubles, but the whole factor (1+w) changes from 1.1 to 1.2, only 9.09%. Tile side grows 10%, and the count falls by about 17.36%. The table does not show which input is more uncertain in reality; it shows model response to these specific scenarios.

Local sensitivity and its boundary

For a positive differentiable output y = f(x), its local elasticity to x is (x/y)(dy/dx). It approximates the percentage change in y caused by a small percentage change in x. Holding A and w fixed gives derivative dN/da = −2A(1+w)/a³ and therefore

Ea=aNdNda=−2

The result is exact for this continuous formula wherever A, a and 1+w are positive. It predicts that a very small 1% increase in side length gives about a 2% decrease in estimated count. For a 10% increase, the exact factor is 1/(1.1²) ≈ 0.8264, a 17.36% decrease rather than 20%. Local derivatives need not match large moves. Actual purchases also require integer counts, layout and availability. Those introduce jumps that the smooth formula omits.

For waste, it is clearer to vary the multiplier q = 1+w. Its elasticity is +1: a 1% change in q gives a 1% change in N exactly. An extra ten percentage points of w is not a 10% change in q. Naming the denominator prevents a misleading sensitivity statement.

Choose a useful check

1. Calculate. With A = 8 m², a = 0.25 m and w = 0.05, find N. Then raise a by 4% and find the exact count ratio, holding A and w fixed.

Hint for question 1

New a is 1.04 times old a; the ratio cancels A and 1+w.

Full solution to question 1

Baseline N = 8/0.0625 × 1.05 = 128 × 1.05 = 134.4. The new side is 0.26 m. The ratio new/old is (0.25/0.26)² = 1/1.04² ≈ 0.9246. Thus new N ≈ 124.26. The continuous estimate falls about 7.54%, compared with the local elasticity prediction of about 8%.

2. Interpret. A supplier can only sell packs of 20 tiles. Is the smooth elasticity −2 still the exact sensitivity of packs purchased? Use the baseline 165 tiles and a small decrease to 163 as a counterexample.

Hint for question 2

Convert each tile estimate to a whole number of packs by rounding up.

Full solution to question 2

For 165 tiles, ceiling(165/20) = 9 packs. For 163, ceiling(163/20) = 9 packs. The continuous estimate changes, but pack count does not. At a threshold such as 160 to 161 tiles, packs jump from 8 to 9. A derivative of the smooth N does not describe this stepped purchasing rule; use scenario calculations around thresholds.

Answer a scoped question

Sensitivity ranks the consequences of stated input changes within a stated model. It cannot tell us input uncertainty unless we measure or specify that uncertainty. The tile-side elasticity is a precise local property of A(1+w)/a²; the finite table reveals its limits and the pack example exposes a discontinuity. The next practical step is to check the layout and waste assumptions against a small trial, then update the model and repeat the comparison.
