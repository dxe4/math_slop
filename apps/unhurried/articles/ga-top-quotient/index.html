What does it mean to glue two edges?

Imagine marking matching heights on the left and right edges of a rectangle, then identifying each pair. The result is a cylinder. A quotient space turns this instruction into a set of equivalence classes with a precise topology. We will check the topology rule, construct the cylinder map, and distinguish a genuine mathematical identification from a visual animation.

Background capsule. An equivalence relation ∼ on a set X is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. The class [x] is all points equivalent to x; X/∼ is the set of classes. The quotient map q:X→X/∼ sends x to [x]. A preimage q−1(V) consists of all original points whose classes lie in V. A topology declares which subsets are open; arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets must be open, and ∅ and the whole space must be open.

Make the cylinder from a square

Worked example. Let X=[0,1]×[0,1]. Identify (0,t) with (1,t) for every t∈[0,1], and make no other identifications except those forced by equivalence. For instance, [(0,1/3)] has two representatives, while [(1/2,1/3)] has one. The bottom edge becomes a circle, as does the top edge. The top and bottom are not identified with one another, so the object has two boundary circles rather than being a torus.

Define F([s,t])=(cos 2πs,sin 2πs,t), a point of S¹×[0,1]. It is well-defined: the only distinct equivalent representatives have s=0 and s=1 at the same height t, and both give (1,0,t). It is onto because every circle point has an angle s∈[0,1]; it is one-to-one because equal heights and equal circle angles in that range give either equal s or the endpoint pair, already identified. Thus the class set is bijective with the usual cylinder.

A neighborhood of a seam point on the cylinder pulls back to two thin strips, one at each vertical edge of the square. Those strips are open relative to the square. This is why a quotient-open set can surround a seam point even though no single interior strip crosses the square’s printed boundary.

The quotient topology and its exact guarantee

Exact definition. For any topological space X and equivalence relation ∼, declare V⊆X/∼ open precisely when its full preimage is open in X:

V open in X/∼⇔q−1(V) open in X

Verification. The inverse image of ∅ is ∅ and the inverse image of all of X/∼ is X, both open. Preimages commute with arbitrary unions and finite intersections. Therefore the declared open sets obey all three topology axioms. By the same defining rule, q is continuous: every open V downstairs has open preimage upstairs. This is a full verification of the quotient rule, independent of the rectangle example.

There is a useful test for maps out of the quotient. If g:X/∼→Y is any map, then g is continuous exactly when g∘q:X→Y is continuous. For an open O⊆Y, the preimage (g∘q)−1(O) equals q−1(g−1(O)); the quotient rule says this is open exactly when g−1(O) is open. In particular, the map F above is continuous because F∘q is the continuous formula (s,t)↦(cos 2πs,sin 2πs,t).

To conclude that F is a homeomorphism, its inverse must also be continuous. One standard route uses two theorems: a closed square is compact, and a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space has continuous inverse. Continuous images of compact spaces are compact, and S¹×[0,1] is Hausdorff. Those general theorems are cited as inputs here; this lesson proves the quotient rule and the explicit bijection, while a full proof of the compact-to-Hausdorff theorem is beyond its scope. The cylinder claim has these hypotheses visible rather than relying on a sketch.

Check what is actually identified

1. Under the cylinder relation, list the classes of (0,1/2), (1,1/2), and (1/2,1/2). Decide whether (0,0) and (0,1) are equivalent.

Hint for question 1

Only points on opposite vertical edges at the same height are paired.

Full solution for question 1

[(0,1/2)]=[(1,1/2)]={(0,1/2),(1,1/2)}. The interior point has singleton class [(1/2,1/2)]={(1/2,1/2)}. The points (0,0) and (0,1) have different heights, so they are not equivalent. Their images under F are (1,0,0) and (1,0,1), on different boundary circles.

2. Suppose someone declares a subset V of X/∼ open whenever it has one open representative region in X. Explain why checking only one representative near a seam is insufficient; state the correct test.

Hint for question 2

A class may contain both (0,t) and (1,t). A subset of classes pulls back to all representatives.

Full solution for question 2

A set of classes must include or exclude each class as a whole. If V contains [(0,t)], its preimage includes both (0,t) and (1,t); openness must be checked around both in the square’s relative topology. A neighborhood only around (0,t) says nothing about the other representative. The correct condition is that the full saturated preimage q−1(V), including both seam strips, is open in X.

Keep the seam and the topology together

A quotient is a set of classes plus the topology determined by full preimages. For the rectangle relation, a continuous bijection to the standard cylinder is explicit, and the usual compact-to-Hausdorff theorem supplies inverse continuity. Identifying the top and bottom as well would define another quotient. No surface-classification theorem follows from this one construction. Return to open sets if relative seam neighborhoods need another look.
