Take a detour above the real line

The real integral ∫−∞∞ dx/(1+x²) has a familiar arctangent answer. A complex contour offers a second explanation: an upper semicircle encloses one pole, and its curved part contributes less and less as the radius grows. The arc estimate is essential; without it, the contour answer would not imply the real integral.

Background capsule. For a function with a simple pole at a, its residue is limz→a(z−a)f(z), when that limit exists. A counterclockwise contour integral ∮f(z)dz is computed from a piecewise smooth parameter path. The ordinary improper integral over the real line means the sum of the separately convergent integrals from −∞ to 0 and from 0 to ∞. For 1/(1+x²), both tails converge by comparison with 1/x² when |x|≥1, so its symmetric limits give that ordinary integral.

Find the enclosed pole

Worked example. Factor 1+z²=(z−i)(z+i). For radius R>1, let CR run along the real interval −R to R and then counterclockwise on the upper semicircle back to −R. The pole i lies inside; −i lies below. At i, the residue of f(z)=1/(1+z²) is limz→i1/(z+i)=1/(2i).

The residue theorem gives ∮CRf(z)dz=2πi·1/(2i)=π. Split this into the straight and curved pieces. If the curved integral tends to zero, the straight integral tends to π. This is the precise point where a real improper integral emerges from the complex calculation.

Make the arc estimate quantitative. For R≥2, R²−1≥3R²/4, so the omitted upper-arc contribution has modulus at most 4π/(3R). Consequently the symmetric finite real integral differs from π by at most this quantity. This bound is sufficient rather than sharp: the real tail can be estimated directly by 2/R. Both estimates tend to zero and justify passage to an infinite real interval. The important feature is a bound that holds for every sufficiently large R, rather than a small value observed for one radius.

Account for the arc

Residue theorem, used here. Let C be a positively oriented simple closed piecewise smooth contour. If f is holomorphic on an open neighborhood of C and its bounded interior except for finitely many isolated poles ak strictly inside, then

∮Cf(z)dz=2πi∑kRes(f,ak)

The general theorem is stated, not proved in this lesson. Its hypotheses exclude a pole on the contour, and the orientation fixes the sign. Our rational function is holomorphic away from ±i, so CR is admissible for R>1.

On the upper arc |z|=R. The reverse triangle inequality gives |1+z²|≥||z²|−1|=R²−1. Hence |f(z)|≤1/(R²−1). The arc length is πR, so the contour length estimate gives

|∫arcdz1+z2|≤πRR2−1→0

The estimate proves the arc disappears. Therefore the straight integral tends to π. This does not license dropping arbitrary semicircle arcs: the bound depends on the decay of this particular integrand. For 1/z on an upper semicircle, the arc integral is iπ, independent of radius. The same contour shape can have very different limits.

As an independent real check, ∫dx/(1+x²)=arctan x, and arctan R−arctan(−R)→π. This confirms the answer but does not replace the arc proof in the complex method.

Check a residue and challenge an arc

1. Calculate. Evaluate ∫−∞∞ dx/(x²+4) by the same upper semicircle method. State the pole and an arc bound.

Hint for 1

The upper pole is 2i. On |z|=R>2, use |z²+4|≥R²−4.

Worked solution for 1

The residue at 2i is 1/(4i), so the contour integral is 2πi/(4i)=π/2. The upper arc has modulus at most πR/(R²−4), which tends to zero for R→∞. Thus the real integral is π/2. The contour avoids both poles when R>2.

2. Explain. Why does the claim “a semicircle gets far away, so every arc integral vanishes” fail for f(z)=1/z?

Hint for 2

Parameterize the upper arc z=Reit, 0≤t≤π.

Worked solution for 2

On that arc dz/z=i dt, so its integral is iπ for every R>0. Length grows like R while |1/z| shrinks like 1/R, leaving a constant product. Distance alone does not establish a vanishing contour contribution.

The detour earns its answer

One residue fixes the whole contour at π, and an explicit O(1/R) arc bound connects that contour to the real integral. The residue theorem is the cited general tool; the pole calculation and arc limit are derived here. For a different integrand, repeat all three checks: admissible contour, enclosed singularities, and an actual estimate of the extra path.
