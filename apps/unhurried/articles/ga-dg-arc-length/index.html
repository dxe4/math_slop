Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

Question and background

How can a ruler measure a curved road? A chord between two points underestimates the trip whenever the road bends. We can break the trip into many short chords, then ask what their lengths approach as their endpoints become close. This article gives that limit a calculable form for a continuously differentiable curve.

Local capsule. A parametrized curve is a vector-valued function γ(t)=(x(t),y(t)), with t in an interval [a,b]. Its derivative γ′(t)=(x′(t),y′(t)) is the velocity vector. The Euclidean norm ||(u,v)||=√(u²+v²) is the vector's length. A definite integral adds continuously varying contributions. “Regular” means γ′(t) is never zero; that condition matters for a unit tangent, although a C¹ curve can have finite length even with a stationary point.

Time and distance are distinct. Equal changes in t need not cover equal pieces of a path. Indeed, speed is the norm ||γ′||, so the clock has to be weighted by speed when we calculate distance. The method works for a curve in three dimensions as well: add a z′(t)² term under the square root.

A worked example

Worked example. Take γ(t)=(3cos t,3sin t), from t=0 to t=π/2. This draws one quarter of a circle of radius 3. Differentiation gives γ′=(-3sin t,3cos t), so ||γ′||=√(9sin²t+9cos²t)=3. Every unit of parameter covers three units of distance. Integrating over a parameter interval of length π/2 gives L=3π/2.

A two-chord approximation uses the points at 0, π/4 and π/2. Each chord has length 3√(2−√2), giving 6√(2−√2), approximately 4.592. The true arc length 3π/2 is approximately 4.712. Refinement shortens the gaps between the polygon and the curve. The numerical comparison illustrates the limit; it is not by itself a proof of convergence for every curve.

Notice that the endpoint displacement is √18, about 4.243. It is smaller than the two-chord route and the arc. The endpoint difference answers a different question from distance traveled. A curve that doubles back may even have zero displacement while accumulating positive length.

The statement and its boundary

Exact statement. If γ:[a,b]→ℝⁿ is continuously differentiable, its arc length, defined as the supremum of lengths of inscribed polygonal paths, equals the speed integral below. Regularity is not required for this equality. The formula does require the derivative and enough continuity for the usual integral argument.

L(γ)=∫ab‖γ′(t)‖dt

Why does speed enter? On a short interval [tᵢ,tᵢ₊₁], differentiability says γ(tᵢ₊₁)−γ(tᵢ) is approximately γ′(tᵢ)Δt. The corresponding chord is therefore approximately ||γ′(tᵢ)||Δt. Summing suggests a Riemann sum for the integral. A complete convergence proof needs uniform control of these errors over all sufficiently fine partitions; continuous differentiability on a closed interval supplies it. We use that standard result here and do not pretend the one-line approximation proves it.

The triangle inequality explains a useful lower bound: L≥||γ(b)−γ(a)||. Straight travel attains it. Curving or reversing generally makes the inequality strict, even though the same endpoints are retained.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. a slanted path. For γ(t)=(2t,3t), 0≤t≤2, compute its length and compare with endpoint displacement.

Hint for exercise 1

Differentiate each coordinate; the speed is constant.

Full solution to exercise 1

γ′=(2,3), so speed is √(2²+3²)=√13. Therefore L=∫₀²√13 dt=2√13. The endpoints are (0,0) and (4,6), whose difference has norm √(16+36)=2√13. Equality is expected for a straight path traversed without backtracking.

Exercise 2. a deceptive clock. Let η(t)=(t²,0), −1≤t≤1. Why is the endpoint difference insufficient, and what is the path length?

Hint for exercise 2

Speed is |2t|. Split the integral at zero.

Full solution to exercise 2

Both endpoints are (1,0), so displacement is zero. But η moves from x=1 to x=0 and back. Its speed is |2t| and L=∫₋₁⁰(−2t)dt+∫₀¹2t dt=1+1=2. The velocity vanishes at t=0, so this curve is not regular there; nevertheless the C¹ length formula still applies.

What to carry forward

Keep the pairing of a curve and its speed: the integral records accumulated travel, while the endpoint vector records displacement. A zero-speed instant need not destroy length, but it prevents defining a unit tangent at that instant. Piecewise smooth paths can be measured by adding lengths of pieces; corners require separate treatment of tangent directions.

For a next step, change the clock on the same geometric trace. The geometry should stay put even when the speed formula changes. Curvature will ask a second question: how quickly does the direction of motion rotate per unit of distance?
