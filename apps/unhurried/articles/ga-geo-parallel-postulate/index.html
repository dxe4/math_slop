How many parallels pass through one point?

In a school drawing, a point outside a line has one parallel line through it. That familiar assertion describes Euclidean geometry. Change what counts as a line and how distance is measured, and a different answer can be coherent. The purpose here is to keep each model's objects explicit before comparing counts.

Background capsule. In the Euclidean plane, a line extends in both directions without end. Two distinct lines are parallel when they never meet. A geodesic is a locally shortest route for the chosen metric; in the models below it plays the role of a straight line. The sphere uses great circles, intersections of the sphere with planes through its centre. A standard hyperbolic disk model uses circular arcs orthogonal to its boundary circle, plus diameters. The disk boundary is not part of that plane.

Three precise cards

Worked example. In the Euclidean plane, choose ℓ as y=0 and P=(0,1). Lines through P have equations y=mx+1 except the vertical x=0. If m≠0, y=0 occurs at x=−1/m; the vertical line also meets ℓ at (0,0). Only m=0 gives y=1, which stays disjoint. That is a worked coordinate instance of uniqueness, not a proof of the complete Euclidean axiom from weaker axioms.

On a sphere, take the equator as a line and the north pole as P. Every great circle through the poles crosses the equator at two opposite points. In the Poincaré disk, the corresponding picture looks less ordinary: several geodesic arcs through P reach different ideal boundary pairs and can miss a fixed geodesic. Boundary endpoints are infinitely far away in the model metric, so touching a boundary point in the drawing is not an interior intersection.

What is an axiom, and what is model evidence?

Exact Euclidean claim. In Euclidean geometry, given a line ℓ and P not on ℓ, there exists exactly one line through P disjoint from ℓ. This is Playfair's formulation of the parallel postulate. In the coordinate plane it follows from the slope argument after a rigid change of coordinates places ℓ at y=0. Treating that coordinate plane as a model already assumes Euclidean structure; it does not prove the postulate from Euclid's other axioms.

∃!m:P∈m∧m∩ℓ=∅

The symbols say “there exists exactly one line m” with the stated properties. On the sphere any two great circles come from planes through the origin. Two distinct such planes intersect in a line through the origin; that line hits the sphere at two antipodal points. So no pair of great circles is disjoint. As an incidence comparison this is full reasoning. Yet ordinary spherical great circles have two intersections, unlike some incidence axioms for an abstract line; identifying antipodal points repairs that feature. Also, spherical shortest paths between opposite points are not unique. Those details matter when someone calls the sphere simply “Euclid with a changed postulate.”

The hyperbolic claim requires a model theorem. In the Poincaré disk, complete geodesics are orthogonal circular arcs or diameters; there are infinitely many through an exterior point that avoid a given geodesic in the open disk. One can vary their ideal endpoints through an interval. This is model evidence and a sketch, not a proof that the hyperbolic axiom is independent of Euclid's other axioms. Establishing formal independence and consistency needs a precise axiom system and an interpretation theorem.

Test the definition before counting

1. Calculation. Let ℓ be y=0 and P=(2,3). Which line through P is Euclidean-parallel to ℓ? Where does y=2x−1 meet ℓ?

Hint for question 1

A line parallel to a horizontal line has slope zero.

Full solution for question 1

The unique horizontal line through P is y=3, and it never has y=0. For y=2x−1, setting y=0 gives x=1/2, so it meets ℓ at (1/2,0). It does pass through P, since 2·2−1=3, but it is not parallel to ℓ.

2. Explanation. A student says two great circles are parallel because they look nearly horizontal on a map. Give a decisive reason this fails.

Hint for question 2

Think of the planes through the sphere's centre that define the two circles.

Full solution for question 2

Two distinct centre planes meet in a line through the centre. Its two intersections with the sphere lie on both great circles. Thus the circles meet, regardless of how a map projection draws them. The statement “parallel” requires the spherical line definition, not a resemblance to flat ink lines.

A useful comparison boundary

Euclidean, spherical and hyperbolic geometry give one, zero and infinitely many disjoint lines through an exterior point under the stated line models. The spherical model needs its antipodal incidence caveat, and the hyperbolic count rests here on the disk-model description. A common failure is to count intersections of drawn arcs at the disk boundary as intersections of hyperbolic lines; boundary points are excluded. The lesson's conclusion is about these specific models, not a derivation of all non-Euclidean geometry from a picture.
