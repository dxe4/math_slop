Can infinitely many blankets be reduced to finitely many?

Covering each point of a closed interval with a little open neighborhood sounds like an infinite task. Compactness says that, no matter how the neighborhoods were chosen, finitely many already suffice. The statement is about every open cover, not a particular convenient drawing. We will prove the closed-interval case by using the least-upper-bound property of the real numbers.

Background capsule. A family {Ui} covers a set K if every point of K belongs to at least one Ui. An open cover uses sets open in the surrounding space, here ℝ. A finite subcover is a finite selection from that family which still covers K. A bounded nonempty set S of real numbers has a supremum c: c bounds S above and each number below c fails to be an upper bound. This completeness property of ℝ is assumed, not proved here.

A cover with endlessly many members

Worked example. For each positive integer n, let Un=(−1/n,1+1/n). Every Un contains [0,1], so this infinite family has a one-set subcover. That easy cover does not establish compactness. A more instructive cover of [0,1] is Un=(−1,1−1/n) for n≥2, together with W=(1/2,2). The Un approach 1 without ever containing it, while W covers the right end. In fact U3=(−1,2/3) and W together cover [0,1]: their overlap from 1/2 to 2/3 leaves no gap.

Contrast (0,1), covered by Un=(1/n,1) for n≥2. Each x in (0,1) lies in some Un, because 1/n eventually falls below x. Any finite selection has a largest index N, hence its union is just (1/N,1) and misses, for example, 1/(2N). This open interval is not compact. The decisive issue is not simply that the original family is infinite; it is whether every such family admits finite reduction.

Push a finite cover from left to right

Exact statement. A set K is compact when every open cover of it admits a finite subcover. In particular, for real a≤b,

∀𝒰 open cover of [a,b]∃U1,…,Un∈𝒰:[a,b]⊆⋃j=1nUj

Proof. Fix an arbitrary open cover 𝒰 of [a,b]. Let S consist of those x∈[a,b] for which [a,x] can be covered by finitely many members of 𝒰. Some member covers a, so a∈S. The set S is nonempty and bounded above by b; let c=sup S.

Choose U∈𝒰 containing c. Because U is open, there is r>0 with (c−r,c+r)⊆U. By the defining property of a supremum, there is x∈S with x>c−r/2, unless c=a; in that endpoint case take x=a, already in S. A finite selection covers [a,x], and adding U covers [a,c] because the intervals [x,c] lie within (c−r,c+r). Thus c∈S.

If c<b, choose y with c<y<min(b+1,c+r/2) and y≤b. The same finite selection plus U covers [a,y], so y∈S, contradicting that c is an upper bound. Therefore c=b, and the finite selection covers [a,b]. The proof works also when a=b: any cover member containing the sole point suffices. Its only substantial external input is the least-upper-bound property. We have not proved arbitrary closed and bounded subsets of higher-dimensional spaces compact.

Compactness is preserved by continuous maps: if {Vi} covers f(K), then their open preimages cover K; select finitely many and map forward. This short proof shows why compactness matters beyond intervals. The converse fails: a constant map from noncompact ℝ has compact one-point image.

Find the finite selection

1. Let U=(−1,0.7), V=(0.4,1.2), W=(0.9,3). Show these three open sets cover [0,1] and find a smaller subcover.

Hint for question 1

The overlap of U and V bridges the middle; check both endpoints explicitly.

Full solution for question 1

U covers [0,0.7), while V covers (0.4,1]. Their overlap includes (0.4,0.7), so U∪V contains every point of [0,1]. Thus {U,V} is a two-set subcover, and W is unnecessary. Neither U alone covers 1 nor V alone covers 0, so this subcover cannot be shortened further.

2. Give an open cover of [0,1) with no finite subcover, and explain why omitting the endpoint matters.

Hint for question 2

Use sets extending slightly left of zero but stopping short of one.

Full solution for question 2

Take Un=(−1,1−1/n) for integers n≥2. Any x<1 eventually satisfies x<1−1/n, so the family covers [0,1). A finite selection has largest index N; its union is UN and misses x=1−1/(2N), which still belongs to [0,1). Thus no finite subcover exists. Adding point 1 to the set would also require a cover member reaching that endpoint; in a cover of [0,1], the supremum proof exploits exactly such a neighborhood.

Separate the definition from the interval theorem

Compactness quantifies over all open covers. The finite-subcover theorem for [a,b] follows from real-number completeness, not from a sketch of finitely many strips. A bounded set can still fail compactness when a limiting endpoint is missing. This global property can help test whether a proposed homeomorphism is possible.
