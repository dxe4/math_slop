When can a nearby best point be best everywhere?

A convex landscape has no hidden valley beneath a line segment connecting two points. That geometric condition turns a local minimum into a global one. It is a strong statement about the whole feasible set and the whole function; a bowl-like sketch alone is not enough.

Background capsule. A set C is convex if for any x,y in C and 0≤t≤1, the point (1−t)x+ty also lies in C. This is the straight segment from x to y, in one or many dimensions. A function f on C is convex if its value at each segment point does not exceed the weighted average of endpoint values. A local minimum is best in some small neighborhood relative to C; a global minimum is best throughout C.

Test a simple bowl exactly

Worked example. Take f(x)=(x−3)²+2 on the real line. Its minimum value is visibly 2 at x=3 because a square cannot be negative. For x=1 and y=5, the midpoint is 3: f(3)=2 while [f(1)+f(5)]/2=6. The graph lies below that chord. This one check illustrates the inequality, but does not prove convexity for all pairs.

To prove it, set a=x−3 and b=y−3. The identity (1−t)a²+tb²−[(1−t)a+tb]²=t(1−t)(a−b)²≥0 gives the inequality for every x,y and t in [0,1]. The added constant 2 cancels. When 0<t<1 and x≠y, the final term is positive: f is strictly convex. The exact proof works on any interval as well as the whole line.

Test a constrained endpoint. Keep the same function but restrict its domain to [4,6]. The unconstrained minimum x=3 is unavailable. For feasible x, x−3≥1 and squaring increases as x increases, so the unique constrained minimizer is x=4 with value 3. Its derivative is 2 rather than zero. This fits the local-to-global theorem because the interval and objective remain convex, while illustrating why an unconstrained stationary equation is insufficient for a constrained problem.

Local-to-global, with its hypotheses visible

The defining formula is f((1−t)x+ty)≤(1−t)f(x)+tf(y) for x,y∈C and 0≤t≤1.

Theorem. If C is convex and f:C→ℝ is convex, then every local minimizer x* is global. Proof: suppose some feasible y has f(y)<f(x*). Every segment point zt=(1−t)x*+ty is feasible. Convexity gives f(zt)≤(1−t)f(x*)+tf(y)<f(x*) for every t>0. As t tends to zero, zt approaches x*, contradicting local minimality. The proof uses both convexity assumptions; it does not require differentiability.

If f is strictly convex, it has at most one global minimizer on a convex set: two distinct minimizers would have a midpoint with strictly smaller value. Existence is a separate matter. For example, f(x)=x² on C=(0,1) is strictly convex, but its infimum zero is unattained because 0 is excluded. Even an ordinary convex function can have many minimizers, as f(x)=0 on [0,1] shows.

For differentiable convex f on an open convex set, the supporting-line inequality f(y)≥f(x)+∇f(x)·(y−x) makes ∇f(x)=0 sufficient for global optimality. That claim assumes convexity and an unconstrained interior point. A constrained optimum can have nonzero gradient, because feasible directions are restricted.

Use the promise carefully

1. Calculation. Show f(x)=2(x+1)²+5 is strictly convex and find its global minimizer on [−3,4].

Hint for question 1

Scale the square identity in the example by two; then use nonnegativity.

Full solution for question 1

The chord gap is 2t(1−t)(x−y)², positive for distinct x,y and interior t, so f is strictly convex. The square term is nonnegative and vanishes at x=−1, which lies in [−3,4]. Thus f(−1)=5 is the unique global minimum. Convexity also means any established local minimum would have been global.

2. Boundary of the theorem. Give a strictly convex function on a convex feasible set that has no minimizer, and say why this does not contradict uniqueness.

Hint for question 2

Remove the point where a square reaches zero.

Full solution for question 2

Use f(x)=x² on C=(0,1). The set is convex and the square identity proves strict convexity there. Values approach zero as x→0 from the right, but every feasible x is positive and has f(x)>0. There is no minimizer to be unique; “at most one” allows zero.

One guarantee, two separate questions

Convexity of both domain and objective makes local optimality global. Strict convexity rules out two distinct minimizers, while compactness or coercivity and suitable continuity may be needed to ensure one exists. Do not apply this conclusion to a disconnected feasible set or a nonconvex objective merely because the nearby graph looks bowl-shaped. Gradient descent asks how to approach a minimizer once the landscape has useful structure.
