Why does a flat triangle total 180°?

Measure enough paper triangles and their corner angles seem to add to a straight angle. Measurements can motivate the claim, but cannot cover every triangle. A short proof works because Euclidean parallel lines give equal alternate interior angles. Seeing that dependency prepares us to understand why triangles on a sphere or in a hyperbolic plane behave differently.

Background capsule. A triangle is formed by three noncollinear points A, B and C joined by straight segments. Write its inside angles as α at A, β at B, γ at C. A straight angle measures π radians, or 180°. When a transversal crosses two parallel Euclidean lines, its alternate interior angles are equal. A line through a point parallel to a given line exists and is unique in Euclidean geometry. The argument below uses existence and the angle equality; both belong to the Euclidean setting.

Transfer two corners to the third

Worked example. Draw a line through C parallel to AB. Follow that line from left to right. The side CA cuts both the new line and AB, so an angle beside C on the new line equals α by alternate interior angles. Similarly CB makes an angle beside C equal to β. Between them sits γ. These three adjacent angles fill one side of a straight line, so they add to π.

For a numerical right triangle with vertices A=(0,0), B=(3,0), C=(0,4), α=π/2. At B, the acute angle has tangent 4/3; at C, the remaining acute angle has tangent 3/4. The two acute angles are complementary because the horizontal and vertical sides are perpendicular, so their sum is π/2. With the right angle the total is π. This instance checks the theorem but the parallel-line argument handles triangles that are not right angled.

The exact Euclidean theorem

Statement and full proof. For every nondegenerate triangle whose sides are Euclidean straight segments, measured with ordinary Euclidean angles,

α+β+γ=π

Construct through C a line parallel to AB. Alternate interior angles make its two outer angles on the side containing the triangle respectively α and β. The middle angle is γ. They partition a straight angle at C, whose measure is π. Adding the three parts proves the equation. No particular side lengths or a special drawing orientation enter the reasoning.

The proof's logical hinge is the parallel-line angle theorem. In familiar Euclidean foundations it is derived using the parallel postulate, or taken as an equivalent fact within a suitable axiom system. Merely sliding α and β as paper cutouts would hide that hinge. If the “lines” become great-circle arcs on a sphere, there is no parallel through C to AB; the transfer step fails. The octant triangle formed by three mutually perpendicular great circles has three right angles, totalling 3π/2. In constant curvature −1 hyperbolic geometry, a nondegenerate geodesic triangle has angle sum less than π; proving that general result requires more than this Euclidean diagram.

Even in Euclidean geometry, the hypothesis “triangle” rules out collinear vertices. If A, B and C lie on one line, the usual three interior angles of a nondegenerate triangle are not defined in the same way. The theorem is not repaired by assigning convenient angles to a collapsed picture.

Use the theorem and identify its hinge

1. Calculation. Two angles of a Euclidean triangle are 43° and 68°. Find the third, and decide whether a triangle with 43°, 68°, 80° can be Euclidean.

Hint for question 1

Subtract known angles from the straight angle.

Full solution for question 1

The third is 180°−43°−68°=69°. The proposed angles 43°+68°+80°=191° do not total 180°, so they cannot be the three interior angles of a Euclidean triangle.

2. Explanation. Someone applies the same transferred-angle proof to a spherical octant triangle. Point to the exact step that fails and give its actual angle sum.

Hint for question 2

Can a great circle through one vertex avoid a given great circle?

Full solution for question 2

The proof asks for a line through C parallel to AB. Every great circle meets every other great circle, so no such spherical line exists. The octant's three corners each measure π/2, making its sum 3π/2 rather than π.

A theorem with an address

In a flat Euclidean plane, a parallel through one vertex lets the other two angles be read against a straight angle. The equation α+β+γ=π is exact for nondegenerate Euclidean triangles. It is not a universal definition of triangle: the spherical octant is an explicit failure of that wider claim. When changing geometry, ask what counts as a line and whether the parallel-line angle relation survives. That question is more informative than asking whether the new drawing “looks curved.”
