Counting choices

Begin by naming one complete outcome. If you choose one of three drinks and then one of two snacks, an outcome is a drink–snack pair. Each drink has two snack choices, so there are 3×2=6 pairs. This counts combinations of choices; it does not require a probability model.

Worked example. Arrange three distinct postcards in a row. There are three choices for the first place, two remaining choices for the second, and one for the last. The result is 3×2×1=6 arrangements, written 3!.

3!=3×2×1=6

For a positive integer n, n! multiplies every integer from 1 through n. The convention 0!=1 counts the single empty arrangement. Factorials count arrangements of distinct objects when each is used exactly once.

Try one. How many two-letter codes can you make from A, B and C when repetition is forbidden?

Worked answer

Choose the first letter in three ways and the second in two ways, giving six codes: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA and CB. If repetition were allowed, the second position would have three choices too, giving nine.

Watch the rule. If different first choices allow different numbers of second choices, count those branches separately and add. A single fixed product needs the stated number of options at every stage.

A bag of coins can be encoded by multiplying short polynomials.

Suppose a box holds one token marked 1, one marked 2 and one marked 3. You may take or leave each token. How many selections have total mark 3? The two selections are {3} and {1,2}. The polynomial (1+x)(1+x²)(1+x³) records every selection: choose 1 from a factor when its token is left, and choose its x-power when taken. The exponent of the resulting term is the selected total. Different selections can produce the same exponent, which makes the coefficient count them.

Prerequisite capsule. For a polynomial P(x), the notation [xᵏ]P(x) means the coefficient multiplying xᵏ after like terms are collected. We treat x as a bookkeeping symbol, not a numerical amount of money. Multiplying polynomials uses the distributive rule: choose one term from each factor, multiply the chosen terms, then add contributions with equal powers. A finite product therefore encodes a finite collection of choices without any convergence questions.

Count a concrete case

Worked example. First multiply (1+x)(1+x²)=1+x+x²+x³. Multiplying by (1+x³) gives 1+x+x²+2x³+x⁴+x⁵+x⁶. The coefficient 2 at x³ is a ledger of two choices: x³ from the third factor with 1 from the others, or x·x² from the first two factors with 1 from the third. The coefficient at x⁴ is one, for {1,3}; at x⁰ it is one, for taking no tokens. The sum of all coefficients is 8, also found by evaluating the polynomial at x=1: each of three binary choices doubles the number of subsets. This is an audit of the encoding, not a new assumption.

The exact rule and its proof

number of subsets with weightk=[xk]∏i=1r(1+xwi)

Finite subset generating function. For r individually distinguishable tokens with nonnegative integer weights w₁,…,wᵣ, the displayed coefficient counts subsets whose weights sum to k. Proof: expanding the product chooses one of two terms from each factor, so each choice sequence corresponds to exactly one subset. Multiplying selected terms adds exponents, producing x to the total weight. Polynomial addition then collects one contribution per subset of that total. This is a complete finite-product argument; no infinite power series or analytic convergence is required. If two distinct tokens have the same weight, keep both factors: (1+x)²=1+2x+x² counts two different one-token selections. If the tokens are indistinguishable, that interpretation changes and the coefficient two is inappropriate.

Where the rule fails. A tempting error is to read the exponent as the number of selected tokens when weights differ. In (1+x)(1+x²)(1+x³), the term x³ can arise from one token or two; it records total weight, not subset size. If negative weights are admitted, ordinary polynomials in nonnegative powers no longer cover every product term, so the stated model needs adjustment. If unlimited copies of a token are permitted, a factor 1+xʷ captures only zero or one copy; the allowed multiplicities must appear explicitly, perhaps as a finite longer factor.

Worked example: a zero-weight token still makes a choice

Take three distinguishable tokens with weights 0, 1, and 2. Their polynomial is (1 + x⁰)(1 + x)(1 + x²) = 2(1 + x)(1 + x²). The coefficient of x² is 2. The two subsets of total weight 2 are the weight-2 token alone and that token together with the weight-0 token. Their numerical totals agree, but the subsets differ.

Replacing the first factor by 1 would lose the choice to take the zero-weight token. Conversely, evaluating the complete product at x = 1 gives 8, the full count of subsets of three distinguishable tokens. The theorem allows nonnegative weights, including zero, because its expansion argument tracks selections rather than requiring every selected item to raise the total. It is the coefficient that records multiplicity when different choices share one weight.

Try two counts

Exercise 1. Expand (1+x²)(1+x³)(1+x⁴) only as far as needed to count subsets with weight 5. Name the contributing selections.

Hint for exercise 1

Find pairs or singletons among weights 2,3,4 that sum to 5. Each token can be used once.

Full solution to exercise 1

Only the pair {2,3} totals 5. In the product this is x²·x³·1=x⁵, and no other selection gives x⁵, so [x⁵] of the product is 1. A singleton has weight 2,3 or 4; the other pairs total 6 or 7, and all three total 9.

Exercise 2. Two separately labeled red tokens each have weight 1. Write the polynomial and interpret its coefficient of x. Then say what changes if the tokens are truly indistinguishable.

Hint for exercise 2

Use one factor per labeled token. Ask whether selecting the first token differs from selecting the second.

Full solution to exercise 2

For labeled tokens, (1+x)(1+x)=1+2x+x², so two one-token subsets produce coefficient 2. If the tokens are indistinguishable, choosing one red token has just one visible outcome. The appropriate inventory polynomial is 1+x+x², with coefficient 1 at x. The distinction is about what counts as a distinct selection, not about algebra failing.

What to carry forward

A finite generating polynomial translates choices into exponents and counts into coefficients. Its proof is a term-by-term correspondence between subsets and product expansion terms. Specify whether tokens are distinct, how many copies are permitted, and what the exponent measures. Then coefficients provide an exact count, while a small explicit list checks the interpretation.
