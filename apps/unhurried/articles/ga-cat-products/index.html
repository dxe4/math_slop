Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

What makes ordered pairs universal?

Suppose two measurements of the same input are needed: f:Z→X and g:Z→Y. We can package them into one function Z→X×Y, sending z to (f(z),g(z)). The useful claim is stronger than “pairs exist”: this package is the only function whose two coordinate projections recover f and g.

Background capsule. X×Y is the set of ordered pairs (x,y) with x∈X and y∈Y. The projections π1:X×Y→X and π2:X×Y→Y select the first and second coordinates. Equality of ordered pairs means equality of both coordinates. In the category Set, objects are sets and arrows are functions. A universal property characterizes an object and its arrows by how other arrows factor through it, including both existence and uniqueness of the factor.

Pair two finite observations

Worked example. Let Z={0,1,2}, X={even,odd}, Y={small,large}. Set f(0)=even, f(1)=odd, f(2)=even; set g(0)=small, g(1)=small, g(2)=large. The paired function m:Z→X×Y is m(0)=(even,small), m(1)=(odd,small), m(2)=(even,large).

Prove the universal property in Set

Exact statement and full proof. For any sets X,Y,Z and functions f:Z→X, g:Z→Y, there exists exactly one function ⟨f,g⟩:Z→X×Y such that

π1∘⟨f,g⟩=f,π2∘⟨f,g⟩=g

For existence define ⟨f,g⟩(z)=(f(z),g(z)). The first projection returns f(z), and the second returns g(z), for every z∈Z, proving both function equations. For uniqueness, suppose h:Z→X×Y obeys them. Write h(z)=(x,y). Then x=π1(h(z))=f(z), and y=π2(h(z))=g(z). Ordered-pair equality gives h(z)=(f(z),g(z)) for every z; therefore h=⟨f,g⟩. This also works when one of the sets is empty: when f and g exist, the same pointwise argument applies, and a function from an empty Z is unique.

Notice the roles: X×Y is the product object, π1 and π2 are fixed arrows out of it, and the mediator points into it from Z. Reversing that mediator would express a different problem. Another product object can have a different coordinate presentation yet satisfy the same property. This article proves the canonical ordered-pair construction in Set; product existence in every category is not claimed.

For a change of presentation, let X=Y={0,1} and label the four pairs with α, β, γ, δ in the order (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1). Call the symbol set Q. Define q1(β)=0 and q2(β)=1, and similarly read both coordinates from the other labels. The relabeling e:X×Y→Q is a bijection. Given f and g, the unique mediator into Q is e∘⟨f,g⟩. The two projection equations follow by construction. This shows why the universal property recognizes a product without requiring its elements to be written as coordinate pairs.

Use existence and uniqueness

1. Calculation. Let Z={a,b}, X={0,1}, Y={red,blue}. If f(a)=1, f(b)=0 and g(a)=g(b)=red, write ⟨f,g⟩ and both projection composites.

Hint for question 1

The mediator sends each z to the pair of its two observed values.

Full solution for question 1

⟨f,g⟩(a)=(1,red) and ⟨f,g⟩(b)=(0,red). Applying π1 gives 1 at a and 0 at b, exactly f. Applying π2 gives red at both a and b, exactly g.

2. Explanation. A proposed h sends a to (1,blue) and b to (0,red). Which requirement fails, and why does the first coordinate alone not establish a product mediator?

Hint for question 2

Apply both projections at a.

Full solution for question 2

π1∘h=f, since the first coordinates are 1 and 0. But π2(h(a))=blue whereas g(a)=red. Thus π2∘h≠g. The mediator must satisfy both equations; the first projection leaves the second coordinate undetermined.

The arrow pattern survives new contexts

The product of two sets is characterized by a unique way to pair any two maps sharing a domain. The ordered-pair proof is complete for Set, while products in other categories may use different objects. If two inputs must additionally agree after mapping to a shared target, the appropriate construction is a pullback.
