How much of one direction lies along another?

A wind blowing northeast helps a cyclist traveling east, but only part of that wind points down the road. The dot product measures alignment and turns that question into a signed number. Positive means a component along the chosen direction, negative means against it, and zero means perpendicular.

Background capsule. A plane vector u=(u₁,u₂) is a displacement. Its length is ||u||=√(u₁²+u₂²), by the right-triangle theorem. For nonzero vectors u and v, let θ be the smaller angle between them, from 0 to π. The dot product is the coordinate calculation u·v=u₁v₁+u₂v₂. A unit vector has length one. We will distinguish the scalar component of u along v from the projected vector itself.

A road and a wind

Worked example. Let wind u=(3,4) and road direction v=(2,0). The dot product is 3·2+4·0=6. Since ||v||=2, the signed scalar component along the road is (u·v)/||v||=3. The projected vector is ((u·v)/(v·v))v=(6/4)(2,0)=(3,0). Thus the northward component (0,4) contributes nothing to motion along an eastward road. If the road instead points west, v=(−2,0), the scalar component becomes −3: the wind opposes the directed road.

The westward direction yields a negative signed component but the same eastward projected vector: multiplying a negative number by a west-pointing unit vector gives an east-pointing vector. This distinction prevents sign mistakes.

From coordinates to angles

Exact statement. For nonzero u,v in the Euclidean plane, with angle θ between them,

u·v=‖u‖‖v‖cosθ

Here is a complete derivation using the cosine rule, assuming that familiar Euclidean triangle result. Component arithmetic gives ||u−v||²=(u₁−v₁)²+(u₂−v₂)²=||u||²+||v||²−2(u·v). The triangle with sides u, v, and u−v has cosine-rule expression ||u−v||²=||u||²+||v||²−2||u||||v||cos θ. Comparing the two formulas gives the displayed identity. The only deferred part is a proof of the cosine rule itself.

Divide by ||v|| to get the signed length of u along v: u·(v/||v||). Divide again by ||v|| to get the coefficient multiplying v, namely (u·v)/(v·v). Therefore projvu=((u·v)/(v·v))v. Subtract it from u. Its dot product with v is u·v−((u·v)/(v·v))(v·v)=0, so the residual is perpendicular to v. This algebra proves orthogonality without trusting a drawing. The denominator v·v is positive exactly when v is nonzero.

If u or v is zero, the coordinate dot product still exists and equals zero. But the angle with a zero vector and projection onto zero are undefined by these formulas. Likewise, a zero dot product with nonzero vectors proves perpendicularity; zero dot product involving a zero vector does not describe a physical right angle.

Separate a component from a vector

1. Calculation. Let u=(2,5) and v=(1,2). Find u·v, the signed scalar component along v, the projected vector, and the perpendicular residual.

Hint for question 1

Compute v·v before dividing. Keep the square root only in the signed scalar component.

Full solution for question 1

u·v=2+10=12 and v·v=1+4=5, so ||v||=√5. The signed scalar component is 12/√5. The projected vector is (12/5)v=(12/5,24/5). Subtraction gives residual (2−12/5,5−24/5)=(−2/5,1/5). Its dot product with v is −2/5+2/5=0, checking perpendicularity.

2. Failure case. Someone writes projvu=((u·v)/(v·v))v for v=(0,0), then cancels the denominator and concludes the projection is zero. Explain exactly why the calculation fails.

Hint for question 2

Evaluate v·v and ask what line the phrase “onto v” identifies.

Full solution for question 2

For v=(0,0), v·v=0. The coefficient would require division by zero, so it is undefined. A zero vector also specifies no direction or one-dimensional line onto which to project. Although u·v=0, a zero numerator does not authorize 0/0. One could separately define projection onto the zero subspace to be zero, but that is a different definition and does not rescue this formula.

Alignment has a denominator

Compute u·v from coordinates, then use the cosine rule to read its sign as alignment. To project onto a direction, first check that the direction vector is nonzero. The scalar component and the projected vector are different outputs; their signs can look different when the chosen direction is reversed. This idea returns in least squares, where the residual is perpendicular to every direction available to a model.
