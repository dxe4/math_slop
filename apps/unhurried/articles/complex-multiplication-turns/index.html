Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

A multiplication that moves the whole plane

Multiplying real numbers stretches or reverses a number line. Complex multiplication does something equally concrete to the plane: a nonzero multiplier stretches distances from the origin and turns every nonzero vector through the same angle.

A complex number has the form a + bi, where a and b are real numbers and i satisfies i² = −1. Represent it by the point (a, b). The real part a is its horizontal coordinate, and the imaginary part b is its vertical coordinate. The word “imaginary” is a historical name; both coordinates are ordinary real numbers.

We use the usual distributive rule for multiplication, then replace i² with −1. No calculus is needed. Here is the trigonometry needed later: on the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin, the point at angle θ has horizontal coordinate cos θ and vertical coordinate sin θ. Measure positive angles counterclockwise from the positive horizontal axis. A full turn is 360 degrees, or 2π radians; π/4 radians is 45 degrees. Stretching that unit-circle point by a positive factor r gives coordinates (r cos θ, r sin θ).

Start with a quarter turn

Worked example. Multiply 2 + i by i. Distributing gives 2i + i² = −1 + 2i. The point (2, 1) becomes (−1, 2). Its squared distance from the origin remains 5, and the vector has turned a quarter turn counterclockwise.

Try another multiplier: (2 + i)(1 + i) = 2 + 2i + i + i² = 1 + 3i. The new point is (1, 3). This combines a turn with a stretch, because 1 + i has length √2 rather than 1.

Derive the rule, then see the geometry

Exact algebraic statement. For any real a, b, c and d, complex multiplication obeys:

(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i

Derivation. Distribute to get ac + adi + bci + bdi². Substitute i² = −1 and collect the real and imaginary terms. The product's coordinates are therefore (ac − bd, ad + bc). This rule also works when one or both numbers are zero.

The length, or modulus, of a + bi is |a + bi| = √(a² + b²). Squaring the product's length gives (ac − bd)² + (ad + bc)². Expanding cancels the two mixed terms and leaves (a² + b²)(c² + d²). Taking nonnegative square roots proves that the product's length is the product of the lengths.

Geometric statement, with hypotheses. When both numbers are nonzero, write them as r(cos θ + i sin θ) and s(cos φ + i sin φ), with r, s > 0. Here an angle is measured from the positive horizontal axis; adding a full turn does not change the point.

Using the multiplication rule, the product is rs[(cos θ cos φ − sin θ sin φ) + i(sin θ cos φ + cos θ sin φ)]. The trigonometric addition identities identify the bracket as cos(θ + φ) + i sin(θ + φ). Thus lengths multiply and angles add, with angles understood modulo a full turn.

This last step uses the standard sine and cosine addition identities, stated here rather than proved. For a fixed nonzero multiplier, s is its stretch factor and φ its turning angle. Zero has no defined angle, so the angle statement excludes it; the coordinate formula and length identity still apply.

Calculate a turn and explain a special case

1. Calculate. Find (1 + 2i)(2 − i), then check the product's length against the two original lengths.

Hint

Use a = 1, b = 2, c = 2 and d = −1. Check squared lengths to avoid unnecessary square roots.

Worked solution

The real part is 1·2 − 2(−1) = 4. The imaginary part is 1(−1) + 2·2 = 3. The product is 4 + 3i, whose length is 5. Each original number has length √5, and √5·√5 = 5 as required.

2. Explain. Why does multiplying by i four times return every point to its starting position? What happens to the origin?

Hint

Compute i² and i⁴. Separate the coordinate calculation from any claim about angles at zero.

Worked solution

Since i² = −1, i⁴ = (−1)² = 1. Four successive multiplications therefore multiply by 1. Nonzero vectors undergo four quarter turns, one full turn. The origin stays at zero at every step, although no angle is assigned to it.

One rule, two descriptions

The coordinate rule gives an exact calculation; the turn-and-stretch description explains its shape. They describe the same operation. A nonzero complex multiplier never squashes just one direction, unlike some general plane transformations. An optional next idea is to ask what changes when the multiplier itself is the point being moved: the map z ↦ z².
