Why a polar cell widens as it moves outward

In a polar grid, an angular change of one degree covers a longer arc far from the center than near it. That geometric fact becomes the factor r in polar area integrals. We will calculate it locally, check it with an exact annular sector, and mark the origin where polar coordinates cease to be one-to-one.

Background capsule. Polar coordinates describe a point by distance r≥0 and angle θ: x=r cosθ, y=r sinθ. A determinant of two planar vectors gives their signed parallelogram area; its absolute value is area. A double integral adds values times tiny area cells. The one-variable identity ∫r dr=r²/2 will suffice for the disk example.

Check an annular sector exactly

Worked example. Take radii a and a+Δr, with a>0, and an angle width Δθ measured in radians. The sector area of radius R is R²Δθ/2, because it is Δθ/(2π) of a circle of area πR². Subtract two sectors: the annular area is [(a+Δr)²−a²]Δθ/2 = (aΔr+(Δr)²/2)Δθ. Divide by ΔrΔθ; as Δr shrinks, the ratio tends to a. Thus an infinitesimal rectangle in (r,θ) coordinates corresponds to physical area approximately rΔrΔθ, with a precise limiting factor.

For a disk of radius 2, integrate the factor: ∫ from θ=0 to 2π and r=0 to 2 of r dr dθ = (2π)(2)=4π. If we omitted r, we would get 4π coincidentally for this radius? The incorrect integral would be ∫1 dr dθ=4π too! Choose radius 3 to expose the error: correct area 9π; omitting r yields 6π. Agreement at radius 2 is an accidental numerical collision, not evidence for the wrong element.

Check a ring rather than a disk. The annulus 1≤r≤2 with a full turn has area ∫₀²π∫₁²r dr dθ=2π(4−1)/2=3π. This agrees with subtracting the inner disk area π from the outer disk area 4π. The angular interval has unchanged length, but the radial integral weights outer strips more heavily because each strip wraps around a longer circle. Using just dr dθ would incorrectly assign area 2π.

Compute the local area multiplier

For Φ(r,θ)=(r cosθ,r sinθ), the derivative sends a small radial displacement to (cosθ,sinθ) and a small angular displacement to (−r sinθ,r cosθ). Their determinant is r(cos²θ+sin²θ)=r. Therefore the local area multiplier is |det DΦ|=r for r≥0.

dxdy=|detDΦ|drdθ=rdrdθ

Proof boundary. The determinant calculation proves the local linear area factor, and the sector calculation verifies it exactly in this geometry. A sufficient change-of-variables statement is this: if Φ:U→V is a C¹ diffeomorphism between open planar sets, D is a compact Jordan-measurable subset of U, and g is continuous on Φ(D), then ∫∫ over Φ(D) of g(x,y) dA equals ∫∫ over D of g(Φ(r,θ))|det DΦ(r,θ)| dr dθ. We invoke that theorem rather than prove its general form. The polar map is a diffeomorphism only after restricting to r>0 and an open angular interval with no seam duplication. At r=0 every angle names the same point; at a full-turn seam two angles name the same ray. To integrate a bounded disk, apply the valid change of variables away from those exceptional sets and pass to the limit. The origin and seam have area zero, so continuous bounded integrands are unaffected. This does not claim a diffeomorphism through either exception.

Orientation matters for signed determinants. If angle runs clockwise, the signed determinant changes sign; physical area still uses an absolute value. For any integrand, r multiplies the integrand after substituting x and y. For example x²+y² becomes r², so its disk integral is ∫∫r³ dr dθ, not ∫∫r² dr dθ.

Integrate with the right cell area

1. Calculation. Compute ∬ over the disk x²+y²≤4 of (x²+y²) dA.

Hint for question 1

Use r² for the function and another r for the area factor.

Worked solution for question 1

The disk is 0≤r≤2, 0≤θ<2π. Thus the integral is ∫₀²π∫₀² r³ dr dθ = (2π)[r⁴/4]₀²=(2π)(4)=8π. The origin causes no singularity in this integrand.

2. Explanation. A student argues that because det DΦ(0,θ)=0, the disk's area must be zero. Explain the confusion.

Hint for question 2

A local factor at one radius does not describe all radii.

Worked solution for question 2

The determinant vanishes only at r=0. For every r>0 the local factor is positive and grows with r. Integrating r from 0 to the disk radius accumulates those nonzero annular contributions. The single origin has zero area; it does not make the entire disk collapse.

What to keep

The extra r measures how a unit angular increment stretches with distance from the origin. It is a Jacobian factor, supported both by a determinant and by annular-sector area. The limitation is coordinate singularity at the origin and seam duplication at a full turn. For a routine disk integral, use a half-open angular range, include r, and check dimensions: dr has length, r dθ has length, so their product has area.
