Can a vector have two addresses?

A traveler can be located by street coordinates or by distances along two slanted avenues. The location is unchanged; its numerical address depends on the chosen directions. Vector coordinates work the same way. A basis gives a unique address for every vector, and a change-of-basis matrix translates between address systems.

Background capsule. In the real plane, vectors add and scale coordinate by coordinate. Two vectors b₁ and b₂ are linearly independent if s b₁+t b₂=(0,0) forces s=t=0. In the plane, two independent vectors form a basis: each vector v can be written uniquely as s b₁+t b₂. Its B-coordinate column is [v]B=(s,t)ᵀ. Standard coordinates [v]E use e₁=(1,0), e₂=(0,1). The column [v]B is an address, not a new physical vector.

Translate one address

Worked example. Choose b₁=(1,1) and b₂=(1,−1). For v=(4,2), solve s(1,1)+t(1,−1)=(4,2). This gives s+t=4 and s−t=2, so s=3 and t=1. Thus the same v is (4,2) in standard coordinates and (3,1) in basis B. The numbers differ because they count different unit directions.

v=3b₁+b₂(4,2)(3,1)

Put the basis vectors into the columns of P=[[1,1],[1,−1]]. Then P(3,1)ᵀ=(4,2)ᵀ. In this example the reverse conversion solves s=(x+y)/2, t=(x−y)/2. Applying it to (4,2) returns (3,1). That reversible formula is possible because the two column vectors are independent.

What the conversion formula says

Exact statement and proof. Let B=(b₁,b₂) be an ordered basis of R², let E be the standard basis, and let P be the matrix whose columns are the E-coordinates of b₁ and b₂. Then P is invertible and

[v]B=P−1[v]E

Proof: if [v]B=(s,t)ᵀ, the definition of coordinates says v=s b₁+t b₂. Since the columns of P are b₁ and b₂ in standard coordinates, matrix multiplication gives [v]E=P[v]B. Independence says Pz=0 only for z=0. In the 2×2 case this means its determinant is nonzero, so P has an inverse. Apply that inverse to both sides. The formula translates from standard coordinates to B coordinates; reversing the arrow uses P, not P⁻¹.

A basis must be ordered: swapping b₁ and b₂ swaps coordinate entries. It must also be independent. If one chooses c₁=(1,1) and c₂=(2,2), then (2,2)=2c₁+0c₂=0c₁+1c₂, so the coordinates are not unique. P has proportional columns and no inverse. That is a concrete failure of the formula's hypothesis.

For a linear map T, an extra distinction appears: the matrix for T in B-coordinates is P⁻¹AP if A is its standard matrix and input and output both use B. This follows by converting B-input to standard with P, applying A, and converting back with P⁻¹. It does not say A and P⁻¹AP act identically on raw coordinate pairs; their inputs use different address systems.

Switch addresses deliberately

1. Calculation. For B=((2,0),(1,1)), find [v]B when v=(5,3), then reconstruct v from your answer.

Hint for question 1

Write s(2,0)+t(1,1)=(5,3). The second coordinate gives t immediately.

Full solution for question 1

The equations are 2s+t=5 and t=3. Thus 2s=2 and s=1, so [v]B=(1,3)ᵀ. Reconstruction gives 1(2,0)+3(1,1)=(5,3), exactly the original vector. The column matrix P=[[2,1],[0,1]] is invertible since its determinant is 2.

2. Explanation. A student chooses C=((1,2),(−1,−2)) and asserts every v has one C-coordinate pair. Find a counterexample to uniqueness and a vector that cannot be represented at all.

Hint for question 2

The second candidate basis vector is the negative of the first. Every combination lies on one line.

Full solution for question 2

For v=(1,2), both (1,0) and (0,−1) are C-coordinate pairs, since c₂=−c₁. Meanwhile (0,1) cannot equal s(1,2)+t(−1,−2)=(s−t,2(s−t)): a zero first coordinate would force a zero second coordinate. Thus C neither provides unique addresses nor reaches every vector.

Keep object and address separate

A basis translates geometry into numbers. Put basis vectors in columns to convert new coordinates to old; use the inverse for the return trip. The formula requires an independent pair, and its direction matters. When reading another matrix, ask which basis names its input and output before comparing entries.
