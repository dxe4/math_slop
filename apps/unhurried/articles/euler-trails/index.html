Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Can one walk use every bridge exactly once?

An inspector must cross each bridge once. Revisiting an intersection is allowed; reusing a bridge is not. An Euler trail uses every edge once; if it ends where it started, it is an Euler circuit. Isolated vertices have no bridge to inspect.

Background capsule. We use a finite simple undirected graph. Degree counts incident edges; a trail repeats none. Two vertices are connected when a walk joins them. An Euler route requires all nonisolated vertices in one connected component, because one continuous walk must reach every edge.

Consider bridges AB, BC, CA, and CD: a triangle with a tail. Degrees are A:2, B:2, C:3, D:1. Route D,C,A,B,C uses every edge once and ends at the odd-degree vertices. When is that pattern enough?

Pair arrivals with departures

Worked example. At an internal vertex of a trail, every arrival along an unused edge is followed by departure along another unused edge. The used incident edges therefore come in pairs. At a start different from the end, the first departure is unpaired. At the final vertex, the last arrival is unpaired. Since an Euler trail uses all edges, these used-edge counts equal the graph degrees.

Worked trail D,C,A,B,C
VertexDegreeRole in trail
D1Start: one departure
C3Arrive from D, depart for A, finally arrive from B
A2One arrival and one departure
B2One arrival and one departure

The table checks one graph; a construction must establish the general case. Two separate triangles have all degrees even but cannot share one continuous edge-covering route. Connectivity matters.

The precise Euler criterion

Theorem. Suppose a finite undirected graph has at least one edge and all its nonisolated vertices belong to one connected component. It has an Euler trail exactly when its number of odd-degree vertices is zero or two. It has an Euler circuit exactly when that number is zero. In the two-odd case every Euler trail begins and ends at those odd vertices.

Eulertrail⇔#{v:deg(v)odd}∈{0,2}

Necessity, proved here. Pair every arrival with a departure at each internal occurrence of a vertex. If start and finish differ, only their first departure and final arrival remain unpaired; hence those two vertices are odd and all others even. If start and finish agree, their unmatched-looking first and last edges pair too, so every degree is even. A route using all edges also forces all nonisolated vertices into one connected component.

Sufficiency, construction proof. If all degrees are even, start at a nonisolated vertex and follow unused edges until stuck. A different endpoint is impossible: earlier visits there used arrival–departure pairs, and the current arrival makes used degree odd. Even total degree leaves an unused exit. Thus the trail closes. Its edges remove pairs of incidences, leaving all unused degrees even. If edges remain, connectedness gives an unused edge incident with the closed trail: take one already touching it, or follow a path toward an unused edge and take the first edge leaving the traced vertices. Starting there, build another closed trail from unused edges and splice it into the first. Each splice consumes edges, so finiteness ends the process. With two odd vertices s,t, add one temporary edge between them (a parallel edge if needed), construct the all-even circuit, then remove that edge to open it into an s–t trail. To spell out the splice, write the existing closed trail as a prefix ending at the chosen shared vertex followed by a suffix back to its start. Insert the entire new closed trail between that prefix and suffix. Its last vertex equals its first, so both joins are legal, and it uses only previously unused edges. The combined sequence is therefore a closed trail containing every edge of both old sequences exactly once. If edges remain, repeat; each repetition consumes at least one, so the finite edge set guarantees completion. In the two-odd construction, rotate the resulting circuit to begin with the temporary edge and then delete that first edge. All remaining edges form a trail between the odd endpoints. The temporary parallel edge has its own identity, so removing it leaves any original edge between the same endpoints intact.

Zero edges form a degenerate case: depending on whether a length-zero route counts, an edgeless graph may be called Eulerian. We excluded it in the theorem to keep the inspection question meaningful. Degree parity alone remains insufficient when nonisolated components are disconnected.

Decide and certify

Exercise 1. Edges are AB, BC, CD, DA, AC. Decide whether an Euler trail exists and give one if it does. Can it be a circuit?

Hint for exercise 1

Count degrees of A,B,C,D, then begin at an odd vertex.

Full solution to exercise 1

A and C each have degree three; B and D each have degree two. The graph is connected, so a trail exists but no circuit does. A,B,C,D,A,C uses AB, BC, CD, DA, AC exactly once and ends at C. A circuit would start and end together, forcing all degrees even, so it is impossible.

Exercise 2. A network consists of triangle PQR and separate triangle XYZ. A planner says all degrees are two, so there is an Euler circuit. Diagnose the claim. If the only permitted edit is adding simple edges between the two triangles, find the minimum number of additions that creates an Euler circuit.

Hint for exercise 2

The cross edges must connect the components while adding an even number of incidences to every affected vertex. What is the smallest nonempty even-degree simple graph using only cross edges?

Full solution to exercise 2

The triangles are disconnected, so no route uses all six original edges. Added cross edges must give every vertex even additional degree. Any nonempty finite graph with all nonisolated degrees even contains a cycle: follow edges without repeating one until a vertex repeats. Cross edges form a simple bipartite graph, whose shortest cycle has four edges. Thus at least four additions are necessary. Add PX, PY, QX, QY: they form a four-cycle of cross edges, giving P,Q,X,Y two extra incidences each. The resulting graph is connected and all-even, so it has an Euler circuit. Four additions suffice and are minimal.

Parity and connection work together

For a nonempty finite edge set in one connected nonisolated component, zero odd vertices permit a closed edge-once route; two permit an open one; any other number forbids both. The parity proof explains the obstruction, and edge splicing establishes existence. A drawing of two disconnected even-degree cycles is the simplest reminder to check connectivity. For routes minimizing edge count rather than covering every edge, continue to shortest paths.
