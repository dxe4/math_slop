How much room does a point need?

The interval (0,1) has no smallest positive point, but every point in it has some room on both sides. That local fact, rather than the appearance of a drawn boundary, is what “open” means for a subset of the real line. We will test it with explicit distances and see why one troublesome point can spoil a set.

Background capsule. The real line ℝ contains the usual numbers in order. An interval (a,b) consists of numbers strictly between a and b; [a,b] includes its endpoints. Absolute value |x−y| measures distance. For ε>0, the ε-neighborhood of x is (x−ε,x+ε), the points less than ε away. A subset U of ℝ is open in ℝ when every x in U has an ε-neighborhood contained in U. The ε may depend on x.

Work at a point near an edge

Worked example. Take U=(2,5) and x=4.9. The left gap is 2.9 and the right gap is 0.1. Choose ε=0.05; then (4.85,4.95) lies in U. The same recipe works for any x with 2<x<5: choose half the smaller of x−2 and 5−x. Both gaps are positive, so this is a positive ε and no point in its neighborhood reaches an edge.

Now take [2,5). Its point 2 belongs to the set. For every ε>0, the number 2−ε/2 lies in (2−ε,2+ε) but outside [2,5). No ε can work at 2. The set is therefore not open in ℝ, even though all its other points have room. A single witness is enough to disprove a statement beginning “for every point.”

The empty set is open: there is no member at which the condition could fail. All of ℝ is open: ε=1 works at every point. Neither conclusion says that the empty set has a hidden interval or that ℝ has finite edges.

The quantifiers and what they permit

Exact statement. For U⊆ℝ with its ordinary distance,

U open⇔∀x∈U∃ε>0:(x−ε,x+ε)⊆U

This is the metric definition, so the equivalence sign records terminology, not a theorem needing a separate proof. Notice the order: choose x first and then choose its ε. Requiring one ε to work at all x would reject (2,5), because points can approach 2 arbitrarily closely.

Two useful proofs. Every ordinary open interval (a,b) is open by the half-gap construction above. Conversely, if U is open and x belongs to U, the definition supplies an open interval around x lying in U. Taking the union of one such interval for each x recovers exactly U. Thus every open subset of ℝ can be written as a union of open intervals. No finiteness condition is imposed on that union.

A set is closed in ℝ when its complement in ℝ is open. The interval [2,5] is closed: its complement is (−∞,2)∪(5,∞), an open union. “Not open” does not mean “closed.” The half-open interval [2,5) is neither: it fails openness at 2, and its complement fails openness at 5. Some sets, including ∅ and ℝ, are both open and closed. The ambient space matters: [2,5) is open relative to [2,∞), because it equals that space intersected with (1,5).

For a more irregular set, ℝ without 0 is open. At any x≠0, choose ε=|x|/2; then every point in the neighborhood has the same sign as x. The method is an exact proof; sampling a collection of points would not settle the universal condition.

Choose a witness or a counterexample

1. Prove (−3,1)∪(4,∞) is open in ℝ. Give an explicit ε for a point x in either component.

Hint for question 1

Stay inside whichever interval already contains x. For the bounded component use both endpoint gaps.

Full solution for question 1

If −3<x<1, set ε=min(x+3,1−x)/2; both gaps are positive and the neighborhood stays in (−3,1). If x>4, set ε=(x−4)/2; then x−ε>4, so the neighborhood stays in (4,∞). Every member lies in one of these cases, proving the union open.

2. Is the intersection of all intervals (−1/n,1/n), for positive integers n, open? Compare this with intersections of two open sets.

Hint for question 2

Which numbers survive every increasingly narrow interval? Test a positive candidate by choosing n large enough.

Full solution for question 2

The intersection is {0}. Zero lies in every interval. If x≠0, choose a positive integer n>1/|x|; then 1/n<|x|, so x is absent. The singleton {0} is not open: for every ε>0, ε/2 belongs to its ε-neighborhood but not to {0}. Two open sets do have open intersection: at a point in both, take the smaller of their two positive neighborhood radii. The failure here involves infinitely many sets.

Keep the local test

Openness is a claim about every included point and a radius chosen for that point. Endpoints often supply counterexamples, but an open set may have many components or missing points. The discussion used the ordinary real-line distance; in another space, first identify its open neighborhoods. This local test becomes the input for continuity through preimages.
