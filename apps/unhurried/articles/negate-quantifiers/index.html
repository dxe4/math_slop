One missed train is enough

An announcement says, “Every train on the board departed on time.” To show it false, you need one listed train that did not depart on time. Saying “every train was late” would be far stronger. This small difference is the heart of negating quantifiers.

Let D be the set of trains on a particular board and P(x) mean “train x departed on time.” ∀x∈D P(x) says every listed train has the property; ∃x∈D P(x) says at least one does. Negation, written ¬, reverses the truth of the whole sentence. A predicate such as P(x) is not a complete proposition until x is assigned or quantified. This is the local prerequisite capsule.

We will prove the two basic negation laws from meanings, then apply them to a sentence with two nested quantifiers. The board is a finite worked model; the laws themselves do not rely on finiteness. We assume ordinary classical true-or-false semantics throughout.

Worked example. The universal claim fails because B is a counterexample. Its negation, “there exists a listed train that was not on time,” is true with witness B. But “no listed train was on time” is false because A and C were on time. If someone instead claims “at least one train was not on time,” its negation says “every train was on time”; there is no need to find a single on-time train to negate existence.

For a nested example, let P(x,y) mean person x received message y, with x ranging over members and y over messages. Negating “each member received some message” requires a member for whom every possible message fails. One missed message for one member is too weak; that person could have received a different message.

Switch the quantifier, negate the inside

Exact laws. For any specified domain D and predicate P, ¬∀x∈D P(x) is equivalent to ∃x∈D ¬P(x), and ¬∃x∈D P(x) is equivalent to ∀x∈D ¬P(x). Both laws also work when D is empty under classical semantics.

¬∀xP(x)≡∃x¬P(x)

Semantic proof. “Not all members have P” is true exactly when the universal sentence has a failing member. Such a member is a witness to ¬P, proving the first equivalence in both directions. “There is no member with P” is true exactly when each member fails P, proving the second. For an empty domain, every universal sentence is true and every existential sentence false; the two equivalences still agree. The proof is about quantified truth conditions, not a mere pattern of swapping symbols.

Apply the laws one layer at a time: ¬[∀x∈X ∃y∈Y R(x,y)] ≡ ∃x∈X ¬[∃y∈Y R(x,y)] ≡ ∃x∈X ∀y∈Y ¬R(x,y). A common invalid negation is ∃x∃y ¬R(x,y). That says only that someone lacks one relation to one object. In a two-person, two-book club where each has read exactly one different book, such a missing pair exists, yet every person has read some book. The invalid negation would report failure where there is none.

If the domains themselves are constrained by extra conditions, keep those conditions inside the translation. For example “every train on the board” quantifies over that board, not over every train in the world. Changing the domain changes the claim even if the symbols look similar.

Negate all the way inward

1. Negate “some integer is both even and positive” in symbols and plain English.

Hint for question 1

Negate ∃n∈ℤ [E(n)∧P(n)]; then use the propositional De Morgan law.

Full solution to question 1

The negation is ∀n∈ℤ ¬[E(n)∧P(n)], equivalently ∀n∈ℤ [¬E(n)∨¬P(n)]. In words, every integer is either not even or not positive. The original statement is true, for example at n=2, so its negation is false.

2. A club claims ∃b∈B ∀m∈M Read(m,b). Write its exact negation and explain a witness pattern in a two-book club.

Hint for question 2

First deny the outer existential, then the inner universal.

Full solution to question 2

The negation is ∀b∈B ∃m∈M ¬Read(m,b): for each book there is a member who has not read it. If Ada alone read Atlas and Ben alone read Beacon, Ben witnesses the failure for Atlas and Ada witnesses it for Beacon. The member may depend on the book.

Check that your final negation has no ¬ in front of a quantifier and no unnegated predicate when the original was positive. That mechanical check helps, but the witness explanation is what establishes meaning.

A negation names a failure

Negating ∀ seeks one failure; negating ∃ says all candidates fail. For nested claims, move inward one quantifier at a time and preserve the order of choices. The timetable witness and club countermodel show why “some missing pair” is weaker than “one person lacks every pair.”

A good stopping point is a correct symbolic negation plus a plain-language witness pattern. This lesson proves the classical semantic laws; it does not replace the need to state a domain or clarify an ambiguous everyday sentence.
