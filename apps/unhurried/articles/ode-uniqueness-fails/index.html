Can a motion law allow waiting?

Consider the initial-value problem y′=2√|y| with y(0)=0. At height zero the assigned slope is zero, so the constant curve y=0 works. Surprisingly, the rule also permits a solution to rest for any chosen delay and then rise. This is an exact mathematical example of nonuniqueness, not a physical prediction that a particle can choose a departure time.

Local background. A solution must be differentiable and satisfy the equation at every time in its interval, including a join between pieces. A function f(y) is locally Lipschitz near zero if there are L and a neighborhood such that |f(u)−f(v)|≤L|u−v| for all u,v there. This condition is stronger than continuity and is a sufficient ingredient in the standard local uniqueness theorem.

Verify two futures

Worked example. For any a≥0 define ya(t)=0 when t≤a and ya(t)=(t−a)² when t≥a. Choose a=0: at t=1 the solution is 1. Choose a=2: at t=1 it is still 0. Both pass through (0,0), so uniqueness fails.

On t<a, both sides of the equation are zero. On t>a, y′=2(t−a) and 2√|y|=2√((t−a)²)=2(t−a), because t−a>0. At the join t=a, the left derivative is zero and the right derivative is limh→0+h²/h=0. Thus y is differentiable there with y′(a)=0=2√|y(a)|. We have checked the delicate point rather than assuming that two correct formulas can always be glued.

Locate the missing hypothesis

The right-hand side f(y)=2√|y| is continuous, including at zero. Continuity of a rate rule is associated with local existence in the standard Peano theorem, but does not alone guarantee uniqueness. Here existence is already shown directly by the constant and delayed formulas. To test Lipschitz behavior at zero, compare u>0 with v=0:

|f(u)−f(0)||u−0|=2u→∞ as u↓0

No finite local Lipschitz constant can bound these ratios. That explains why the familiar sufficient uniqueness theorem does not apply. Failure of a sufficient condition by itself does not prove nonuniqueness; the explicit pair of solutions proves it here.

A useful comparison is z′=2z with z(0)=0. This right-hand side is globally Lipschitz, since |2u−2v|=2|u−v|. Multiplying z′−2z=0 by e−2t gives (e−2tz)′=0, and the initial value makes z identically zero. Another comparison, z′=3z2/3 with z(0)=0, again permits delayed cubic departure. The exponent below one makes the slope as a function of state too sharp near zero for a simple Lipschitz bound.

For a continuous f(t,y) that is locally Lipschitz in y near an initial point, the Picard–Lindelöf theorem gives a unique solution on some time interval around that point. It does not promise a solution for all future times. The full existence proof constructs iterates and uses completeness of a space of continuous functions; it is outside this article. Even a smooth rule such as y′=y² has the solution y=1/(1−t) from y(0)=1, which blows up at t=1. Smoothness gives local uniqueness, not global duration.

A sketch of slope marks at y=0 would show horizontal segments; it would not reveal whether the curve must stay there. Every delayed curve initially follows the same horizontal trace. Exact verification is the decisive test.

Check the join and the theorem

1. A delayed cubic

Exercise 1. For y′=3|y|2/3, y(0)=0, test y(t)=0 for t≤4 and y(t)=(t−4)³ for t≥4.

Hint for 1

For t≥4, |(t−4)³|2/3=(t−4)². Examine both one-sided derivatives at t=4.

Worked solution for 1

Before 4, derivative and right-hand side are zero. After 4, y′=3(t−4)²=3|y|2/3. At 4 both one-sided derivatives are zero, so y′(4)=0 equals the right-hand side. Since 0≤4, y(0)=0. The constant zero solution also works, so the initial value has at least two solutions.

2. Test an inference

Exercise 2. A rate rule fails a Lipschitz test at an initial state. Must it have two solutions there?

Hint for 2

Distinguish a sufficient theorem from its converse. Try y′=1+√|y| with y(0)=0: its speed never vanishes.

Worked solution for 2

No. Failing a sufficient condition at one place does not logically assert nonuniqueness. For f(y)=1+√|y|, the quotient |f(u)−f(0)|/|u|=1/√u diverges as u↓0, so f is not locally Lipschitz at the initial state zero. Yet define F(y)=∫0y ds/[1+√|s|]. Its integrand is continuous and positive, so F is differentiable and strictly increasing near zero. Every differentiable solution obeys dF(y(t))/dt=F′(y)y′=1 and F(y(0))=0; hence F(y(t))=t. A strictly increasing F has only one local inverse, giving a unique local solution. Failure of a sufficient condition alone proves nothing either way.

What this counterexample proves

A continuous rate rule can allow several differentiable trajectories through one initial point. The delayed curves establish the failure constructively, and the divergent Lipschitz ratio identifies the hypothesis missing from a standard local theorem. Do not infer global existence from local existence or a plotted direction field. The linear direction field supplies a contrasting direct uniqueness proof.
