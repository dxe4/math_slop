Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Can a family tree end even when it grows on average?

Each fantasy creature independently produces 0, 1 or 2 descendants under one shared rule, then leaves. This Galton–Watson process has generation size Zₙ, starting at Z₀=1. Expected generation size and eventual extinction probability ask different questions.

Background capsule. If a creature has offspring count K with probabilities p₀,p₁,p₂, its mean is m=0p₀+1p₁+2p₂. Expectation is an average across imagined repeats; it need not describe a typical realized tree. A generating polynomial f(s)=p₀+p₁s+p₂s² packages the offspring probabilities. Extinction means Zₙ=0 at some finite generation; zero then remains zero. Independent, identically distributed reproduction is essential to the formulas below.

We will use a toy rule with p₀=1/4, p₁=1/4 and p₂=1/2. Its mean is 5/4, yet extinction still has positive probability.

One generation, then the whole future

Worked example. From one creature, the first generation has 0, 1 or 2 creatures with the stated probabilities. Its expected size is 0·1/4+1·1/4+2·1/2=5/4. The expected second generation is (5/4)²=25/16, because each of the expected first-generation creatures independently has mean 5/4 children. This does not imply the family grows on every run: it ends immediately with probability 1/4.

Let q be the eventual extinction chance from one creature. If the first creature has no children, extinction is certain. If it has one, the chance is q; if two, both independent child lines must die, chance q². Thus q=1/4+(1/4)q+(1/2)q². Rearranging, 2q²−3q+1=0, with roots 1/2 and 1. Which root is the extinction probability? The smaller one: q=1/2. The root 1 appears algebraically for every offspring distribution because f(1)=1, but in a supercritical process it need not be the actual extinction chance.

Expectation recurrence and the correct root

E[Zn]=Z0mn,q=f(q)

Condition on Zₙ=z. There are z parents, each with independent offspring count of mean m, so E[Zₙ₊₁|Zₙ=z]=mz. Averaging over Zₙ yields E[Zₙ₊₁]=mE[Zₙ]; induction gives Z₀mⁿ for a fixed finite initial number Z₀. Independence is not needed for this expectation step alone if conditional means remain m, but it is needed for multiplying child-line extinction probabilities.

For extinction, let qₙ be the chance the line is extinct by generation n. Start q₀=0 for one living ancestor. Independence of child lines gives qₙ₊₁=f(qₙ). Because extinction is absorbing, qₙ increases and is bounded by one, so it tends to a limit q. Polynomial continuity makes q=f(q). Iterating from zero selects the smallest fixed point in [0,1]: if r is any fixed point there, induction gives qₙ≤r and hence q≤r. This proves the root selection for the stated finite offspring model.

For the toy polynomial f(s)=1/4+s/4+s²/2, iteration starts 0, 1/4, 11/32, and rises toward 1/2. The other root 1 is not selected. In general, m≤1 implies extinction with probability one under the usual nondegenerate finite-mean Galton–Watson assumptions, except the deterministic exactly-one-child rule, where the line never ends. When m>1, extinction can still be positive if p₀>0; the exact value depends on the whole offspring distribution, not just m.

Failure case. If siblings' descendants are forced to share a single environmental coin, child lines are dependent. Then the chance both die need not be q², and f(q) need not be the extinction equation. Identical mean offspring alone does not preserve the fixed-point calculation.

Selecting eventual extinctions changes the offspring law

Let E mean eventual extinction, with probability q>0, and let K be the ancestor's offspring count. Given K=k, all k independent child lines must die out, with probability qk. For no children this probability is 1: there are no child lines to keep alive. Thus P(K=k,E)=pkqk. Dividing by P(E)=q gives the selected law:

Pselected(K=k)=pkqkq=pkqk−1

The probabilities sum to f(q)/q=1. For our original law they are (1/2,1/4,1/4), with mean offspring r=0×1/2+1×1/4+2×1/4=3/4. Compare that with the original mean 5/4. This describes a selected set of trees; it is not a causal intervention on the original reproduction mechanism. If q=0, conditioning on extinction by this division is undefined.

Why the selected child lines are still independent

A root calculation alone does not justify applying the new law to the whole tree. Suppose the ancestor has two children. Write E1,E2 for extinction of their lines, and B1,B2 for any events about their respective subtrees. Original independence makes the probability of both Bi∩Ei events the product of their probabilities. Conditional on both lines becoming extinct, divide by q². The result is the product of P(B1|E1) and P(B2|E2).

So, conditional on the number of children and eventual extinction, their subtrees are independent copies of the original tree conditioned on extinction. With one child there is one such copy; with zero there are none. Apply the same argument recursively at every child. The entire selected tree is therefore a Galton–Watson process with the selected offspring law above, rather than merely having a different ancestor distribution.

Total size needs the selected mean

Let S=Z0+Z1+⋯ be the total number of individuals ever born, including the ancestor once. Under the eventual-extinction selected law, generation n has mean rn. If r<1, nonnegative summation gives

E[S|E]=∑n=0∞rn=11−r

More explicitly, finite partial totals have means 1+r+⋯+rn; monotone convergence permits their nonnegative limit. This proves finiteness when r<1 rather than assuming it while solving an expectation equation. Here the conditional mean total is 4. Unconditionally, however, survival has probability 1/2 and then infinitely many individuals are born, so the original total-size mean is infinite. Do not insert the original mean 5/4 into the subcritical formula.

The hypothesis r<1 matters. In the critical zero-or-two example in question 1, extinction is certain and the selected law is unchanged, with r=1. Every selected tree is finite almost surely, yet its mean total size is infinite; question 5 develops the calculation.

A finite deadline is a different condition

Let Eh mean extinction by generation h, with probability qh. The ancestor is alive at generation 0, so q0=0. For h≥1, qh=f(qh−1). A child line now has only h−1 generations in which to die out. Therefore the deadline-selected ancestor probabilities are pkqh−1k/qh. At h=1 only zero children are possible; use an empty product of 1 for that case. There is no conditional law given E0, an event of probability zero.

For h=2, q2=11/32. The ancestor probabilities are (8/11,2/11,1/11), rather than the eventual law (1/2,1/4,1/4). Any children in this selected tree must themselves have zero children: only one generation remains. Reusing the ancestor's deadline law at each generation would incorrectly allow living grandchildren at the extinction deadline. Child subtrees remain conditionally independent, but their laws use the remaining deadline, which shrinks down the tree.

The explorer's three top-level masses concern all original trees: extinct by h, qh; extinct later, q−qh; and never extinct, 1−q. They sum to 1. Among eventually extinct trees, the fraction already extinct is instead qh/q, which at h=2 is 11/16. The finite slider illustrates this partition. The fixed-point proof establishes the eventual value; a finite display does not prove it.

Optional: exact full-size means within a deadline-selected set

At deadline 2, the only possible complete trees have sizes 1, 2 and 3. Their selected probabilities are 8/11,2/11,1/11, so their mean is (8+4+3)/11=15/11. This is a full tree-size mean conditional on extinction by the deadline. It is not the size of a still-living tree with later generations omitted.

For a general deadline, define a score equal to S on Eh and zero outside it; call its unconditional mean Ah. This definition avoids any product of infinity with zero. Start A0=0. The root contributes one individual on the event Eh, hence contributes qh to the score's mean. With one child, the child-size contribution is p1Ah−1. With two independent children, each child's size counts only if the other child line also dies by the remaining deadline. Their combined contribution is 2p2qh−1Ah−1.

Thus Ah=qh+(p1+2p2qh−1)Ah−1, and the selected mean full size is Ah/qh. These finite recurrences produce the exact explorer values. For the original law at h=3 they give q3=809/2048 and mean full size 1379/809. Allow about 15 additional minutes for this optional recurrence and its check.

Mean and extinction are different tasks

1. Calculation. Each creature has zero or two children, each with probability 1/2. Starting from one, find m, E[Z₃] and eventual extinction probability.

Hint for question 1

Write f(s)=1/2+s²/2, solve q=f(q), and select the smallest root in [0,1].

Full solution to question 1

m=0·1/2+2·1/2=1, so E[Z₃]=1³=1. The fixed-point equation is q=1/2+q²/2, or (q−1)²=0. Its only root is q=1, so eventual extinction has probability one, despite every generation having expected size one. Rare large surviving trees keep the mean at one.

2. Counterexample. Every creature deterministically has exactly one child. Compare its mean and extinction chance with question 1. Why does mean alone fail to specify q?

Hint for question 2

Write the actual generation sizes and its generating function.

Full solution to question 2

Here m=1 again, but Zₙ=1 for every generation, so q=0. The generating function f(s)=s makes every s a fixed point; iteration from q₀=0 stays at zero, selecting q=0. In question 1, f(s)=(1+s²)/2 and extinction is certain. Equal means conceal different offspring distributions, so the mean-growth formula cannot determine extinction by itself.

Change the reproduction law

3. Transfer to a fresh law. Give zero, one and two children probabilities 3/8,1/8,1/2. Find its original mean and eventual extinction probability. Derive the offspring law among eventual extinctions, then the mean full tree size in that selected set. Why is the unconditional mean full size different?

Hint for question 3

Solve 4q²−7q+3=0 and select the smaller fixed point. Apply the selected-law formula before using a geometric total-size sum.

Full solution to question 3

The original mean is 1/8+1=9/8. The fixed points are 3/4 and 1, so q=3/4. The selected probabilities are ((3/8)/(3/4),1/8,(1/2)(3/4))=(1/2,1/8,3/8). Their mean is r=1/8+2(3/8)=7/8<1, giving conditional mean total size 1/(1−7/8)=8, including the ancestor. The original tree survives with probability 1/4 and then has infinite total size, so its unconditional mean total is infinite.

4. Change the selection as well. For question 3's law, select trees extinct by generation 2. Find that selection's probability, the ancestor's offspring law and the mean full size. Partition all original trees into extinct by 2, extinct later and never extinct. What fraction of the eventually extinct trees are already extinct by 2?

Hint for question 4

Start q₁=3/8 and substitute into f. Conditional on extinction by 2, all of the ancestor's children have zero children, so the full size is 1+K.

Full solution to question 4

q₂=3/8+(1/8)(3/8)+(1/2)(3/8)²=63/128. The ancestor's three joint masses with this event are 48/128,6/128,9/128. Normalizing gives (16/21,2/21,1/7). The mean ancestor offspring is 2/21+2/7=8/21, so the mean full size is 1+8/21=29/21, not 8. The three unconditional masses are 63/128,33/128 and 1/4. Among eventual extinctions, the fraction extinct by 2 is (63/128)/(3/4)=21/32. The denominators describe different selected sets.

5. Check the mean hypothesis. Return to the zero-or-two critical law in question 1. Is the mean full tree size finite? How can your answer coexist with almost-sure extinction and a finite realized tree?

Hint for question 5

Compute the expected total through generation n before taking its nonnegative limit. Every generation has mean size 1.

Full solution to question 5

The partial total through generation n has mean n+1. Its monotone limit is the full total, so the mean is infinite. Since extinction occurs at a finite generation almost surely and each individual has at most two children, every realized extinct tree is finite. Rare large finite trees make its mean infinite. Here q=1 leaves the selected law unchanged, with r=1, so the finite formula 1/(1−r) does not apply.

Keep two summaries apart

Expected generation size follows a one-line conditional-expectation recurrence. Extinction follows a generating-function recurrence and the smallest fixed point, with independent child lines. A supercritical mean can coexist with a substantial chance of extinction; a critical mean can coexist with either certain extinction or deterministic survival, depending on the offspring law. These are conclusions about the specified toy process, not predictions about real populations.

State the selection event before describing a collected tree. Eventual-extinction selection has one new reproduction law throughout the tree; deadline selection has a shrinking horizon down its branches. A finite cutoff does not turn “not extinct yet” into “survives forever.” For total-size means, check the selected mean offspring and count the ancestor.
