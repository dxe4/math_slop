Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

A tangent makes a bold guess

Imagine standing at x on a curve and drawing its tangent line. The tangent’s intersection with the horizontal axis suggests a new root estimate. Repeating that construction is Newton’s method. Near a well-behaved simple root it often gains digits rapidly; away from such a root its steps may escape, cycle, or be undefined. The same formula supports both stories, so a reliable use needs hypotheses and a stopping check.

Background capsule. A derivative f′(x) is a tangent slope. The linear approximation near x is f(x)+f′(x)(t−x). A simple root r satisfies f(r)=0 and f′(r)≠0. “Residual” means |f(x)|; “root error” means |x−r|. They can differ greatly when the function is nearly flat.

Compare a success with a cycle

Worked example. For f(x)=x²−2, the Newton step from x=1.5 is 1.5−0.25/3 = 17/12 ≈1.416667. One more step gives about 1.414216, already close to √2. Because the derivative 2x is nonzero near positive √2, this is the favorable local setting. A bisection bracket [1,2] would need many halvings to achieve comparable decimal width, though every retained interval carries a location guarantee.

Now take g(x)=x³−2x+2, starting at x=0. Here g(0)=2 and g′(0)=−2, so Newton gives x=1. At x=1, g(1)=1 and g′(1)=1, so Newton returns x=0. The iteration cycles forever: 0, 1, 0, 1, … . The function does have a real root between −2 and −1, since g(−2)=−2 and g(−1)=3. Newton’s failure from this starting point says nothing about root nonexistence.

Derive the step and its local rate

xn+1=xn−f(xn)f′(xn)

Set the tangent approximation f(x_n)+f′(x_n)(t−x_n) equal to zero and solve for t. The division requires f′(x_n)≠0. This construction is not an assertion that the tangent’s intercept is a root.

For the local claim, suppose f is twice continuously differentiable near a simple root r. Taylor’s theorem about x_n gives 0=f(r)=f(x_n)+f′(x_n)(r−x_n)+(f″(ξ)/2)(r−x_n)² for some ξ between r and x_n. Rearranging and comparing with the Newton step yields x_{n+1}−r = [f″(ξ)/(2f′(x_n))](x_n−r)², with sign according to the expansion. Continuity and f′(r)≠0 ensure the denominator stays bounded away from zero near r, while f″ is bounded there. Hence |e_{n+1}| ≤ C|e_n|² for sufficiently close iterates. This is a local quadratic error bound, not a global promise or a claim that each step doubles digits under every rounding model.

At a multiple root, f′(r)=0 and the proof fails. For f(x)=x², Newton from nonzero x gives x/2, only linear convergence to zero. At a point with f′(x)=0 and f(x)≠0, the step is undefined. In floating point a derivative too small to divide safely is also a practical stop condition. A small residual needs a separate derivative or bracket bound before it certifies root location.

Inspect the steps

Exercise 1. Starting at x₀=2, take two Newton steps for f(x)=x²−3. Keep fractions for the first step and give the second as a fraction.

Hint for exercise 1

Use x_{n+1}=(x_n+3/x_n)/2.

Solution to exercise 1

x₁=(2+3/2)/2=7/4. Then x₂=(7/4+12/7)/2=(49/28+48/28)/2=97/56≈1.732143. The positive root is √3≈1.732051. These steps suggest convergence but do not replace a bracket or the local hypotheses in the proof.

Exercise 2. For f(x)=x³−2x+2, verify the 0↔1 cycle and explain why |f(1)|=1 cannot be treated as a positional error bound.

Hint for exercise 2

Compute f and f′ at both iterates, then recall the root lies to the left of −1.

Solution to exercise 2

At 0, f=2 and f′=−2, yielding 1. At 1, f=1 and f′=1, yielding 0. Repetition is exact. The real root is in (−2,−1), so the iterate 1 is more than 2 units from it despite residual 1. Even a much smaller residual would require a sensitivity or bracket argument to become a location certificate.

Speed needs a guard

Newton’s tangent step has a local quadratic error bound near a simple root under smoothness and a sufficiently close initial guess. Neither the formula nor a finite table proves global convergence. A cycle, zero derivative, or small misleading residual is a reason to return to a reliable bracket or to use a guarded hybrid.
