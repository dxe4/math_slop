Can the size of a group force a subgroup?

Lagrange's theorem says a subgroup's size divides the size of a finite group. That is a restriction, not a general existence rule. Sylow's first theorem supplies a striking existence claim when the proposed size is the largest power of a chosen prime dividing the group order. This lesson states and proves its existence claim using translations of finite subsets; the extra counting ideas are developed below.

Background capsule. A prime p has only 1 and p as positive divisors. Every positive integer N can be factored as pnm, where n≥0 and p does not divide m; pn is the largest p-power dividing N. A finite group subgroup H has order |H|, and Lagrange proves |H| divides |G|. A subgroup of order pn in a group of order pnm is called a Sylow p-subgroup. When p does not divide |G|, n=0 and the trivial subgroup of order 1 is the uninteresting case.

Read a group order as several questions

Worked example. Suppose |G|=12=2²·3. For p=2, the largest power is 4; for p=3, it is 3. Sylow's first theorem guarantees at least one subgroup of order 4 and at least one of order 3. It does not say those subgroups are unique, normal, cyclic, or visible from the number 12 alone. The square symmetry group D₄ has order 8=2³, so its entire group is a Sylow 2-subgroup; this case illustrates the statement but offers no insight into its difficult general proof.

The theorem and its boundary

|G|=pnm,p∤m⇒∃H≤G:|H|=pn

First Sylow theorem, proved below. Let G be a finite group, p a prime, and |G|=pnm with p∤m. Then G has a subgroup H of order pn. Neither the table nor the example proves it. The proof below supplies the group action and counting argument needed beyond Lagrange's equal-coset count. The larger Sylow package also discusses conjugacy and the number of such subgroups, but those claims are not established here.

A worked consequence, proved conditional on Sylow. Every group of order 30 has a subgroup of order 5. Factor 30=5·6 and note 5∤6; first Sylow provides a subgroup of order 5. Lagrange is consistent with this because 5 divides 30, but Lagrange alone would not produce it. The same theorem also gives subgroups of order 2 and 3 because 30=2·15=3·10. This application establishes only the listed prime-power sizes. It does not establish which other subgroup orders occur; divisibility alone is not a general existence theorem.

One must choose the maximal p-power. For a group of order 12, first Sylow specifically supplies order 4 for p=2, not merely order 2. Once an order-4 subgroup exists, a separate argument can produce an order-2 subgroup: every group of order 4 has either an element of order 2 or is generated by an element of order 4 whose square has order 2. That elementary deduction does not extend to a general converse to Lagrange.

A complete existence proof by translating subsets

Counting lemma. Put q=pn. The number of q-element subsets of a set of size qm is the binomial coefficient C(qm,q), and it is not divisible by p when p∤m. To prove this, calculate polynomial coefficients modulo p. For 0<j<p, p divides C(p,j): in p!/[j!(p−j)!] the numerator has a factor p and the denominator does not. Thus (1+t)p≡1+tp modulo p. Repeating this identity n times gives (1+t)q≡1+tq. Raise both sides to m and compare coefficients of tq: C(qm,q)≡m modulo p. This is nonzero by assumption.

Let Ω be the set of q-element subsets of G. Each g∈G sends S∈Ω to gS={gs:s∈S}. This is an action: the identity leaves S fixed, and g(hS)=(gh)S. The orbit of S consists of all its translates. Orbits partition Ω, since translating can be undone. Because |Ω| is not divisible by p, at least one orbit has size not divisible by p; otherwise adding the orbit sizes would make |Ω| divisible by p. Choose S in such an orbit.

Let H={g∈G:gS=S}, its stabilizer. It is a subgroup: the identity fixes S, products of fixing elements fix S, and gS=S implies g−1S=S. Two translates gS and hS agree exactly when h−1g∈H, or equivalently gH=hH. Hence the orbit is in bijection with the left cosets of H. Lagrange's coset count gives |G|=|H|·|orbit|. Since the orbit size is not divisible by p, the full factor pn=q dividing |G| must divide |H|.

On the other hand H acts on S by left multiplication. For each s∈S the map h↦hs is injective, because hs=h′s implies h=h′ after cancellation. Its image is the H-orbit of s, so every such orbit has exactly |H| elements. Those orbits partition S, which has q elements. Thus |H| divides q. Together q divides |H| and |H| divides q, forcing |H|=q. This is the required Sylow p-subgroup. The same argument includes n=0, when q=1 and the resulting subgroup is trivial.

This proof establishes existence only. It neither counts all Sylow subgroups nor proves their conjugacy. The extra ingredient beyond Lagrange was the action on subsets and the coefficient count; a divisibility statement about subgroup sizes by itself could not supply H.

Separate theorem use from proof

1. Calculation. A finite group has order 72=2³·3². What subgroup sizes does first Sylow force for p=2 and p=3? What does it not say about uniqueness?

Hint for question 1

Use the largest full prime power dividing 72 for each selected prime.

Full solution for question 1

For p=2, 72=2³·9 and 2∤9, so a subgroup of order 8 exists. For p=3, 72=3²·8 and 3∤8, so a subgroup of order 9 exists. First Sylow alone does not say either subgroup is unique. The counts of Sylow subgroups require additional Sylow theorems and hypotheses.

2. Explanation. A learner writes, “Since 4 divides 12, Lagrange proves every group of order 12 has a subgroup of order 4.” Repair the justification.

Hint for question 2

Name which theorem restricts existing subgroups and which theorem produces maximal prime-power subgroups.

Full solution for question 2

Lagrange proves that any existing subgroup order divides 12; it does not prove existence. Since 12=2²·3 and 2∤3, first Sylow guarantees a subgroup of order 2²=4. This is a correct theorem application, but it is not a proof of first Sylow from Lagrange or from the factorization alone.

Know what was established

You can state the first Sylow theorem with its finite-group and maximal-prime-power hypotheses, and use it to infer a forced subgroup size. The subset-action argument proves existence, including the trivial n=0 case. The failure case for overreach is the false converse to Lagrange: divisibility alone does not provide an arbitrary subgroup. Nor does first Sylow assert uniqueness or normality.

A satisfying stopping point is to identify, for a given group order, which sizes are forbidden by Lagrange and which maximal prime-power sizes are guaranteed by first Sylow. Keep the two logical directions separate.
