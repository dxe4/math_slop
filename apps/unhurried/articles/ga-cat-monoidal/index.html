Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Can two functions run side by side?

Functions compose in sequence: f followed by g. They can also act in parallel on pairs of inputs. In Set, the parallel operation is Cartesian product: (f×f′)(a,a′)=(f(a),f′(a′)). The central law says doing two steps in each lane agrees with doing the first steps in parallel, then the second steps in parallel.

Background capsule. For f:A→B and g:B→C, g∘f:A→C acts right to left. For f′:A′→B′ and g′:B′→C′, Cartesian products A×A′, B×B′ and C×C′ are sets of ordered pairs. The map f×f′ has type A×A′→B×B′. This typing is necessary for composing it with g×g′. A monoidal category adds such a parallel operation, a unit object and coherent associativity and unit isomorphisms; we study the concrete Cartesian instance.

Two lanes, one input pair

Worked example. Let A={0,1}, B={u,v}, C={red,blue}; set f(0)=u, f(1)=v, g(u)=red, g(v)=blue. In the other lane let A′={p,q}, B′={2,3}, C′={even,odd}; set f′(p)=2, f′(q)=3, g′(2)=even, g′(3)=odd. Starting at (1,q), first f×f′ gives (v,3), then g×g′ gives (blue,odd). Doing each lane's composite first gives ((g∘f)(1),(g′∘f′)(q))=(blue,odd).

Both routes through the paired process
Inputf×f′(g×g′)∘(f×f′)(g∘f)×(g′∘f′)
(0,p)(u,2)(red,even)(red,even)
(1,q)(v,3)(blue,odd)(blue,odd)

Those rows illustrate the law but do not prove it for all four input pairs, let alone arbitrary sets. The next calculation proves it pointwise for an arbitrary pair.

Check that regrouping respects processes

For a numerical example, take three copies of Z and functions f(n)=n+1, g(n)=2n and h(n)=−n. Begin at ((2,3),4) in (Z×Z)×Z. Applying (f×g)×h produces ((3,6),−4); the associator then gives (3,(6,−4)). Alternatively, first regroup to (2,(3,4)) and then apply f×(g×h). The answer is again (3,(6,−4)).

The same calculation with arbitrary a,b,c gives (f(a),(g(b),h(c))) along either route. Thus the regrouping bijection is compatible with the processes, rather than just a convenient encoding of elements. Its inverse restores the original parentheses. The order of the three entries stays fixed: an associator changes grouping, while swapping two lanes would use another specified map.

The singleton unit has an equally concrete role. Inserting its sole element beside a gives (a,∗), and the right unitor removes it uniquely. The empty set cannot replace the singleton: for a nonempty A, A×∅ has no elements and cannot be bijective with A. The unit requirement concerns a reversible way to add a trivial lane, not merely a small-looking set.

Interchange and the associator

Exact statement and full interchange proof in Set. For typed functions as above,

(g×g′)∘(f×f′)=(g∘f)×(g′∘f′)

At an arbitrary (a,a′)∈A×A′, the left side first produces (f(a),f′(a′)) and then (g(f(a)),g′(f′(a′))). The right side gives exactly that pair by definition. The domain and codomain of both sides are A×A′ and C×C′, so pointwise equality proves the equation as a function equality. The product of identity functions is the identity on A×A′, since (idA×idA′)(a,a′)=(a,a′). These are the functorial laws of ×:Set×Set→Set.

Parallel composition is associative up to a specified bijection, not literal equality of nested pair expressions. The canonical associator αA,B,C:(A×B)×C→A×(B×C) sends ((a,b),c) to (a,(b,c)); the reverse regrouping is its inverse. The unit is a chosen singleton set 1={∗}. The left unitor λA:1×A→A sends (∗,a) to a, and the right unitor ρA:A×1→A sends (a,∗) to a. These are bijections, not equalities of sets in a raw ordered-pair implementation. Together with × they give the Cartesian monoidal structure. We have defined the isomorphisms and proved interchange; the general coherence theorem for all regrouping diagrams is deferred.

A common failure is to place g′:B′→C′ in the first lane after f:A→B. Unless B=B′ with suitable types, g′∘f is undefined. Parallel notation cannot repair a broken sequential type. Another failure is declaring ((a,b),c) literally the same ordered pair as (a,(b,c)); the associator relates them, but their nesting differs.

Check two kinds of structure

1. Calculation. Using the functions above, calculate both sides of interchange at (0,q).

Hint for question 1

First lane: 0→u→red. Second lane: q→3→odd.

Full solution for question 1

(f×f′)(0,q)=(u,3), and (g×g′)(u,3)=(red,odd). Separately, (g∘f)(0)=red and (g′∘f′)(q)=odd, so their product also gives (red,odd).

2. Explanation. Are (A×B)×C and A×(B×C) literally the same pair construction? Give the appropriate comparison and its inverse.

Hint for question 2

Track the parentheses of one element.

Full solution for question 2

With ordinary nested ordered pairs, ((a,b),c) and (a,(b,c)) have different nesting, so the constructions are not being identified literally. The associator sends ((a,b),c)↦(a,(b,c)); its inverse sends (a,(b,c))↦((a,b),c). The two maps undo one another on every triple.

Parallel composition has laws

Cartesian product makes Set a concrete setting for sequential and parallel processes. Interchange was proved for arbitrary typed functions; associativity and unit were displayed through explicit bijections. General monoidal coherence remains outside this lesson. When applying a “side by side” picture elsewhere, first state the tensor's action on objects and arrows and check that the necessary associator and unit maps exist.
