Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

What if the tangent rate is zero?

Near an equilibrium x=0, a common first move is to replace x′=f(x) by x′≈f′(0)x. For f(x)=−x³ and g(x)=+x³, both derivatives at zero are zero. Their linear approximations are the identical equation x′=0, but their actual motions run in opposite directions. This is where first-order linearization stops deciding stability.

Local background. An equilibrium x* obeys f(x*)=0. Lyapunov stability means: for every allowed radius ε>0, some δ>0 ensures that any solution starting within δ of x* remains within ε for all later times for which it exists. Attraction additionally means nearby solutions tend to x*. “Asymptotically stable” combines the two. These are statements about all nearby starting values, not one trajectory.

Two opposite phase lines

Worked example. For x′=−x³, a positive x has negative velocity and a negative x has positive velocity. Both arrows point toward zero. For x′=+x³, both arrows point away. For x0≠0, separation gives the exact formulas x(t)=x0/√(1+2x0²t) in the negative-cubic case, and x(t)=x0/√(1−2x0²t) in the positive-cubic case while the denominator is positive. The signs are retained by choosing the branch with x(0)=x0.

Compare departures on a finite timescale. With x₀=0.1, the negative-cubic solution at t=50 is 0.1/√2, while the positive-cubic solution's denominator reaches zero at t=50. At much shorter times both may look nearly stationary because their initial velocities are only ±0.001. A short, coarse plot can therefore conceal their opposite eventual behavior. The exact denominator gives the timescale and the domain of each formula; visual agreement near the initial point cannot replace that information.

Prove the two stability claims

For the negative cubic, differentiating x−2 where x≠0 gives d(x−2)/dt=−2x−3(−x³)=2. That produces the first formula. It exists for all t≥0, satisfies |x(t)|≤|x0| and tends to zero. Given ε, choose δ=ε. Every start |x0|<δ then remains inside ε, and attraction follows from the formula. The zero start stays at zero. Thus zero is asymptotically stable.

For the positive cubic, d(x−2)/dt=−2. The denominator in the second formula vanishes at t=1/(2x0²). To prove instability without relying on the word “blow-up,” fix any ε>0. Whatever δ>0 is proposed, choose 0<x0<min(δ,ε). The solution rises continuously to ε at time (1/x0²−1/ε²)/2, which is positive and precedes blow-up. It therefore leaves the ε-neighborhood. That contradicts the stability condition.

f−′(0)=f+′(0)=0,f±(x)=±x3

For a continuously differentiable scalar rate f with f(x*)=0 and f′(x*)<0, the derivative's continuity makes the local arrows point toward x* and gives local asymptotic stability. If f′(x*)>0, the arrows point away and the equilibrium is unstable. The broader multidimensional hyperbolic linearization principle likewise requires no eigenvalue with zero real part and enough regularity; this lesson does not prove that general theorem. Here f′(0)=0, so neither sign test applies. The cubic term supplies the decision directly.

The negative-cubic approach is slower than exponential: for large t, |x(t)| is proportional to t−1/2. A zero eigenvalue does not mean “nothing happens.” It means the first-order term vanishes and higher-order terms may control the timescale and direction.

Inspect the first nonzero term

1. An even power

Exercise 1. For x′=−x², classify zero on the whole real line. Is an inward arrow on the positive side enough?

Hint for 1

On both sides, −x²<0. For x0<0 solve by separation.

Worked solution for 1

Positive starts move toward zero, but negative starts move farther left. The solution x(t)=x0/(1+x0t) blows down at t=−1/x0>0 for x0<0. Arbitrarily small negative starts eventually leave a fixed neighborhood, so zero is unstable on ℝ. Restricting the state space to x≥0 would change the claim.

2. A parameter perturbation

Exercise 2. For x′=−a x³ with a>0, give the solution from x(0)=b and a δ choice proving stability of zero.

Hint for 2

Repeat the derivative of x−2; compare |x(t)| with |b|.

Worked solution for 2

For b≠0, x(t)=b/√(1+2ab²t); for b=0, x=0. Because the denominator is at least one for t≥0, |x(t)|≤|b|. Given ε>0, δ=ε ensures starts |b|<δ stay below ε. The formula also tends to zero, proving asymptotic stability.

What zero linearization means

Matching zero derivatives can conceal attraction and instability. Exact sign and solution arguments decide these scalar examples. Linearization is powerful when its hypotheses apply; when a zero eigenvalue appears, inspect the nonlinear term and the state space. The fold model has a related nonhyperbolic boundary at its collision point.
