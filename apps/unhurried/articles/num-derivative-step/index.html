Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

When shrinking h makes the answer worse

The derivative is a limit as h approaches zero, so it is tempting to choose the smallest positive h a machine can hold. A forward difference, however, subtracts two nearby computed function values and divides by h. Its mathematical approximation error generally shrinks with h; its sensitivity to evaluation and rounding errors grows like 1/h. A finite step can therefore be better than a tinier one.

Background capsule. The forward difference is D_h f(x)=[f(x+h)−f(x)]/h for h≠0. Taylor’s theorem writes f(x+h)=f(x)+hf′(x)+(h²/2)f″(ξ) when f is twice continuously differentiable between x and x+h. A unit roundoff u describes the scale of one relative arithmetic rounding error away from underflow and overflow. We distinguish exact function evaluations from computed ones.

An exact truncation error you can see

Worked example. Take f(x)=x² at x=1. Exact arithmetic gives D_h f(1)=[(1+h)²−1]/h=2+h. The true derivative is 2, so the truncation error is exactly |h|. At h=0.1 the answer is 2.1; at h=0.001 it is 2.001. This side of the story favors smaller h.

Now use a toy device that rounds function values to four significant decimal digits before subtraction. At h=0.001, f(1+h)=1.002001 rounds to 1.002, while f(1)=1.000; the difference quotient is 2.000. At h=0.0001, f(1+h)=1.00020001 rounds to 1.000, so the quotient is zero. The input 1+h must also be represented accurately enough in an actual implementation; this toy example isolates output rounding to show the mechanism.

At h=0.01, 1.0201 rounds to 1.020, so the displayed toy quotient 2.00 is correct. The table is a demonstration under declared rules, not an empirical universal error curve for binary floating point.

Balance two different error mechanisms

|Dhf(x)−f′(x)|≤M2|h|

If |f″(t)|≤M throughout the segment from x to x+h, Taylor’s theorem gives the displayed exact-arithmetic truncation bound. It does not include rounding. Suppose separately that computed values have absolute errors at most ε_f each, and that h itself and the subtraction/division are exact for this simplified analysis. The difference of two evaluation errors has magnitude at most 2ε_f, so the computed quotient error relative to the exact quotient is at most 2ε_f/|h|. Together we obtain M|h|/2 + 2ε_f/|h|. This bound is minimized at |h|=2√(ε_f/M) when M, ε_f>0. The exact constants depend on the arithmetic and function evaluator; the result is a model choice, not a magic step size.

In an idealized floating-point model, ε_f is often on the order of u times the scale of f(x), giving the familiar schematic C₁h+C₂u/h. If f(x)≈0, if input x+h rounds to x, if function data are noisy, or if f″ changes rapidly, that schematic needs revision. Centered differences have a different truncation order under stronger smoothness assumptions and a different balance. Automatic differentiation or analytic derivatives may avoid this finite-difference tradeoff where available.

Choose a step with a reason

Exercise 1. For f(x)=x³ at x=1, derive the exact forward difference and its exact error for positive h.

Hint for exercise 1

Expand (1+h)³ before dividing by h.

Solution to exercise 1

D_h=[(1+3h+3h²+h³)−1]/h=3+3h+h². Since f′(1)=3 and h>0, the exact error is 3h+h². Its leading behavior is proportional to h, but the h² term remains visible for larger steps.

Exercise 2. A model bound is E(h)=h+10⁻⁶/h for h>0. Find its minimizing h and the bound there. Explain why setting h=10⁻¹² is worse according to this model.

Hint for exercise 2

Differentiate E or use the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality.

Solution to exercise 2

E′(h)=1−10⁻⁶/h², so the minimum occurs at h=10⁻³ and equals 0.001+0.001=0.002. At h=10⁻¹², the second term alone is 10⁶, dwarfing the truncation term. This is a bound within the stated error model; actual error can be smaller through cancellation or different evaluation behavior.

A finite difference is a computation

The derivative’s limiting definition does not prescribe the smallest machine step. Smoothness bounds control mathematical truncation; an explicit arithmetic or data-error model controls the amplification of subtraction errors. State both before selecting h. The related cancellation lesson explains the lost digits, while conditioning helps distinguish noisy input data from an unstable formula.
