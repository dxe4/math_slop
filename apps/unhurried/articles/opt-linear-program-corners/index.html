Why does the best mixture touch a corner?

Suppose two products consume limited resources and yield different value. Each inequality trims the plane to a half-plane; their intersection may be a polygon. A linear objective has straight, parallel level lines. Sliding such a line toward better values suggests a vertex solution, but the claim needs boundedness and a nonempty polygon, and an entire edge can tie.

Background capsule. A linear inequality such as x+y≤4 specifies all points on one side of a line. A feasible region is the intersection of all such conditions. A vertex is a corner that cannot be expressed as a nontrivial convex combination of two other feasible points. An objective cᵀx=c₁x+c₂y assigns a number to each feasible point. Maximizing reward is equivalent to minimizing its negative. We use exact arithmetic to check intersections and objective values.

A two-resource allocation

Worked example. Choose nonnegative x,y with x+y≤4 and 2x+y≤6. Maximize R=3x+2y. The feasible polygon has vertices (0,0), (3,0), (2,2), and (0,4). To find (2,2), solve x+y=4 and 2x+y=6 together: subtract to get x=2, then y=2. The rewards at the four vertices are 0, 9, 10, and 8. So (2,2) has the largest vertex reward, 10.

To see why this vertex check covers the interior, decompose any feasible polygon point as a convex combination of its vertices. Its linear reward becomes the same weighted average of the vertex rewards and cannot exceed their maximum. This geometric decomposition is the finite-polygon fact used here. In this example the winner is unique: any mixture involving a vertex with reward below 10 yields less than 10.

Check a rejected intersection. The lines x=0 and 2x+y=6 meet at (0,6), but this point violates x+y≤4. It is not a feasible vertex and its reward 12 cannot enter the comparison. Listing intersections of boundary lines is only a candidate step: each point must pass every inequality. The direct bound R≤10 is a useful independent protection against accepting such a spurious answer. A candidate reward exceeding that proven bound signals either infeasibility or an arithmetic mistake, rather than a better allocation.

The finite vertex certificate

In minimization notation the model is mincTxsubject toAx≤b

Exact claim. If the feasible region in ℝ² is a nonempty bounded polygon, then a linear objective attains its minimum at some vertex. A bounded polygon is the convex hull of its finitely many vertices. If x=Σᵢtᵢvᵢ with tᵢ≥0 and Σᵢtᵢ=1, linearity gives cᵀx=Σᵢtᵢcᵀvᵢ≥minᵢcᵀvᵢ. This proves the claim once the polygon decomposition is established. It does not say every optimum is a vertex.

For a tie, maximize x+y over the triangle x≥0, y≥0, x+y≤4. The vertices (4,0) and (0,4) both give 4, as does every point on the edge between them. A statement that "the best mix always sits at a unique corner" would be false. Nor may we ignore unboundedness: maximize x over x≥0; feasible values have no finite maximum. Some unbounded polyhedra have an optimum at a vertex, but the simple bounded-polygon theorem does not decide all such cases. Feasibility itself must be checked first.

There is a stronger direct certificate in the worked allocation: 3x+2y=(x+y)+(2x+y)≤4+6=10. The point (2,2) is feasible and reaches both bounds, so no feasible point can do better. This inequality proof is useful even without listing all vertices; it foreshadows dual bounds.

Vertices and edges

1. Calculation. Maximize P=2x+y over x,y≥0, x+y≤5, x≤3. List the vertices and identify every maximizer.

Hint for question 1

The rectangle cut by x+y≤5 has a top slanted edge. Check where x=3 meets it.

Full solution for question 1

The vertices are (0,0), (3,0), (3,2), and (0,5). Their P-values are 0, 6, 8, and 5. Any feasible point is a convex combination of vertices, so P≤8, attained at (3,2). It is the unique maximizer since the other vertices have strictly smaller values.

2. Tie check. Maximize x+y on x,y≥0 with x+y≤5. Is the answer one vertex, two vertices, or more?

Hint for question 2

Equality in the constraint gives the best possible objective.

Full solution for question 2

The inequality directly gives x+y≤5. Every point (t,5−t), 0≤t≤5, is feasible and attains 5. There are infinitely many maximizers, including vertices (5,0) and (0,5). The vertex theorem promises at least one vertex optimum and is fully consistent with an optimal edge.

What a corner proves

A bounded feasible polygon makes vertex enumeration a complete finite method for a linear objective. An explicit weighted-constraint bound can certify the answer even more directly. Check emptiness, boundedness and ties before making a claim. In higher dimensions the same finite-vertex principle applies to polytopes, but enumerating vertices can become expensive.
