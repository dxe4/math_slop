Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Can one equation draw a shape?

A polynomial equation in two coordinates selects the points where its value is zero. For example, x² + y² − 1 = 0 draws the unit circle in the real plane. The equation is an algebraic rule; the circle is its real zero locus. We will make that distinction precise, verify points exactly, and learn why a plot is only a guide.

Background capsule. A polynomial in x and y is a finite sum of terms such as 3x²y and −5. To evaluate it at (a,b), replace x by a and y by b. The ordered pair belongs to its zero locus when the result equals zero. Coordinates are real throughout this lesson unless a different number system is named. A square of a real number cannot be negative; that simple fact will let us prove one zero set is empty without trusting a screen.

Test before tracing

Worked example. Set f(x,y)=y−x². At x=−2, substituting gives y=4, so (−2,4) is on the locus. At x=1, the point (1,2) fails because f(1,2)=2−1=1. Repeating this calculation for each chosen x makes the parabola. A numerical plot approximates a continuous curve with samples; exact substitution certifies only the selected points, while the equation y=x² describes every real point on it.

Three polynomial rules with exact checks
RuleVerified point or argumentReal locus
y−x²(−2,4): 4−4=0Parabola
x²+y²−1(0,1): 0+1−1=0Unit circle
x²+y²+1Every value is at least 1Empty

Notice that the last expression has a perfectly valid equation, despite having no real solutions. Over complex coordinates it does have solutions: (i,0) works because i²=−1. Changing the field changes the set under discussion. Even for real coordinates, a pixel-level drawing cannot prove emptiness or identify every component; the inequality above does that for this particular polynomial.

The definition does the work

Exact statement. For a real polynomial f in two variables, its real zero locus is

Vℝ(f)={(a,b)∈ℝ2:f(a,b)=0}

This is a membership test, not a formula for how many points there are. Two different polynomials can have the same locus. Over real numbers, x²+y²=0 forces x=0 and y=0 because two nonnegative squares add to zero only if each is zero. The polynomial x²+y² and its square (x²+y²)² therefore have the same real locus, although their formulas differ and their algebraic behavior near the origin differs. Likewise, multiplying y−x² by 7 changes values away from the parabola but leaves its zero locus unchanged.

Verified derivation for this example. For f=y−x², the condition f(a,b)=0 rearranges to b=a². Thus every chosen real a gives one point (a,a²), and conversely every point on the locus has that form. This proves the whole description of this locus, not just the table entries. For g=x²+y²+1, the inequalities x²≥0 and y²≥0 imply g≥1, so its real locus is empty. Neither argument extends merely by looking at a finite grid of sampled values. A polynomial can vanish between samples or at an isolated point they miss.

A useful operation is to impose two equations at once: Vℝ(f,g) means points satisfying both. Geometrically it is the intersection of the separate zero loci. For instance, y−x²=0 and x=1 select only (1,1). That conclusion uses substitution, which becomes the main tool in curve intersections. We have proved descriptions only for the displayed examples; classifying zero loci of arbitrary polynomials is a much larger subject.

Check a point and challenge a claim

1. Calculation. Describe all real points on Vℝ(y−2x+3), and decide whether (−1,−5) and (2,1) belong.

Hint for question 1

Solve the equation for y, then substitute each ordered pair into the original polynomial.

Full solution for question 1

Rearranging y−2x+3=0 gives y=2x−3, so the locus is {(t,2t−3):t∈ℝ}, a line. At (−1,−5), −5−2(−1)+3=0, so it belongs. At (2,1), 1−4+3=0, so it also belongs. Both checks agree with the parameter description: t=−1 gives −5 and t=2 gives 1.

2. Explanation. Someone says that every polynomial equation in x and y must draw a visible real curve. Test the claim with x²+y²+4=0, then say what happens over complex coordinates.

Hint for question 2

Bound the left side below for real numbers. For complex numbers try x=2i and y=0.

Full solution for question 2

For real x and y, x²+y²+4≥4, so equality to zero is impossible. Its real locus is empty, disproving the claim. Over complex numbers, (2i,0) is a solution because (2i)²+0+4=−4+4=0. A change of allowed coordinates changes the locus.

What the picture can and cannot certify

A zero locus is defined by exact evaluation, and a graph suggests where to test. The same real set can come from distinct equations, while a valid equation may have no real points. Stop here once you can describe a simple locus in both directions: prove each proposed point satisfies the equation, and prove every solution has the proposed form. The next step is to impose two equations and solve their common points.
