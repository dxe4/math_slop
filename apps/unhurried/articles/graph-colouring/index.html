Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

How many meeting slots avoid conflicts?

Several meetings share participants. If two meetings share someone, they cannot run at the same time. Make each meeting a vertex and connect conflicting meetings by an edge. Assigning time slots becomes a vertex colouring: adjacent vertices must receive different colours, with each colour representing one slot. The number of colours is a model result, subject to the assumption that every relevant conflict was entered.

Background capsule. We use a finite simple undirected graph. The neighbors of vertex v are vertices adjacent to it, and its degree is the number of those neighbors. The maximum degree Δ(G) is the largest degree in the graph. A proper colouring gives every vertex one colour and gives adjacent vertices different colours. The chromatic number χ(G) is the smallest number of colours in any proper colouring. You can check one proposed colouring edge by edge; proving it uses the minimum possible number requires a lower bound too.

For meetings A,B,C,D, suppose AB, AC, BC, and CD are conflicts. The first three form a triangle. At least three slots are needed because each pair among A,B,C conflicts. Can three suffice when D conflicts only with C?

Construct a schedule and a lower bound

Worked example. Assign A to slot 1, B to slot 2, C to slot 3, and D to slot 1. Every listed conflict has different endpoint slots: AB is 1/2, AC is 1/3, BC is 2/3, and CD is 3/1. Therefore χ(G)≤3. The triangle forces at least three colours, so χ(G)≥3. Together these bounds prove χ(G)=3 for this particular graph.

Notice that D can share A's slot despite being two steps from A through C. Graph colouring forbids equal colours at edge endpoints, not at all vertices connected by any path. Requiring distinct slots for an entire connected component would solve a much stricter, usually unnecessary problem.

Why degree gives a safe upper bound

Theorem. Every nonempty finite simple graph admits a proper colouring with at most Δ(G)+1 colours:

χ(G)≤Δ(G)+1.

Complete greedy proof. Fix any order of the vertices. Work through that order, giving the current vertex the lowest numbered colour among 1 through Δ+1 not used by its already coloured neighbors. It has at most deg(v)≤Δ neighbors in total, so at most Δ colours can be forbidden. At least one of Δ+1 remains. Previously coloured adjacent pairs already had different colours, and the new vertex differs from every previously coloured neighbor. Induction over the order proves the final colouring is proper. The argument establishes an upper bound, not that this chosen order minimizes colours.

For our four-vertex example Δ=3 at C, so the theorem guarantees at most four colours. The explicit schedule improves that to three, and the triangle proves optimality. A greedy order can use more colours than necessary. In the path with edges AB, BC, CD, a poor order A,D,B,C gives colours 1,1,2,3 under lowest-available greedy colouring: C sees B with colour 2 and D with colour 1. Yet A,C can use colour 1 and B,D colour 2, so χ=2. This concrete failure prevents interpreting the algorithm's output as an optimality certificate.

There is another modelling boundary. If a meeting needs two slots, or if slots have different capacities, ordinary vertex colouring does not encode that alone. The theorem assumes one colour per vertex and conflicts represented exactly by edges. It remains useful as a quick guarantee, even when a better tailored schedule uses fewer than Δ+1 slots.

Check the bound and the model

Exercise 1. Four meetings form a cycle of conflicts AB,BC,CD,DA. Find Δ, give a colouring with as few slots as possible, and justify optimality.

Hint for exercise 1

Try giving opposite vertices the same colour. One edge already shows that one colour cannot work.

Full solution to exercise 1

Every vertex has degree two, so Δ=2 and the theorem promises at most three colours. Give A,C slot 1 and B,D slot 2. Each edge joins opposite slots, so χ≤2. Since AB is an edge, A and B need distinct colours, hence χ≥2. Therefore χ=2, better than the general upper bound of three.

Exercise 2. A planner colours a graph with no edge between X and Y but puts them in different slots, then says the colouring must be invalid because the slots differ. Explain the error. Can two nonadjacent vertices always share a colour in some optimal colouring?

Hint for exercise 2

A proper colouring imposes a one-way constraint only on adjacent pairs. For the last question, consider a graph where X and Y each have other neighbors.

Full solution to exercise 2

Different slots never violate the proper-colouring condition; only equal slots on an edge violate it. Nonadjacency permits equal colours locally but does not promise that X and Y share one in every optimal schedule. In the path X–A–B–Y, X and Y lie on opposite sides of its unique two-colouring, so any optimal two-colouring gives them different colours despite their nonadjacency. A three-colouring could put them together, but it would not be optimal. This distinguishes a local permission from a global guarantee.

A schedule needs both construction and proof

Conflicts become edges, slots become colours, and a proper colouring can be checked directly. The greedy argument always supplies at most Δ+1 slots. To claim the minimum, pair an explicit schedule with a lower bound such as a triangle or an edge. Greedy order can waste colours, and missing conflict edges make a schedule claim unreliable.
