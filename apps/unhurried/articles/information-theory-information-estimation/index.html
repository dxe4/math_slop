Why can a tiny sample look too predictable?

The entropy formula assumes a probability distribution. In practice we may have only a few draws and replace unknown probabilities with observed frequencies. That plug-in estimate can be useful, but its exact value is random and tends to understate true entropy in a finite sample. A sample that misses a rare symbol assigns it zero frequency, even when the source gives it positive probability. We can see the bias without relying on a simulation by enumerating every sample in a tiny fair-bit experiment.

Background capsule. For n draws, the empirical frequency p̂(x) is count(x)/n. Entropy in bits is H(P)=−Σp(x)log₂p(x), with 0 log₂0 treated as zero. The plug-in estimate uses the same function with p̂. Bias means E[estimate]−target, where expectation averages over all possible samples under a fixed source law. It is distinct from the error in a particular realized sample, which may be above or below the target.

Enumerate two fair-bit draws

Worked example. Let the true source emit independent fair bits. Its true entropy is 1 bit. Draw twice. The ordered samples 00, 01, 10, and 11 each have probability 1/4. For 00 or 11, empirical frequencies are (1,0) or (0,1), yielding plug-in entropy zero. For 01 or 10, frequencies are (1/2,1/2), yielding 1 bit. The average estimate over the four equally likely samples is (0+1+1+0)/4=1/2 bit. Thus bias is 1/2−1=−1/2 bit in this deliberately small case.

Nothing about a particular 00 sample proves the source deterministic. It records only two observations. If we instead drew one bit, the empirical distribution would always be a point mass and estimated entropy always zero. The sample size, alphabet, and independence assumption matter to any uncertainty report.

The estimator and its bias boundary

H^=−∑xp^(x)log2p^(x)

Exact claim. If n independent draws come from a fixed finite distribution P, the ordinary empirical plug-in entropy satisfies E[Ĥ]≤H(P). One way to see the direction is that entropy is concave in its probability vector and E[p̂]=P, so Jensen's inequality gives E[H(p̂)]≤H(E[p̂])=H(P). The enumeration above proves the numerical bias for n=2 without requiring Jensen's inequality. Strictness depends on the source and sample size; a deterministic source has entropy and estimated entropy zero, so no negative bias occurs there.

Concavity is the named proof boundary here: the binary maximum lesson shows the shape in two categories, but a general finite-alphabet Jensen proof needs the full concavity result. Bias alone does not supply a confidence interval or tell how far a particular estimate is from truth. For a large alphabet relative to n, unseen symbols can be common and the plug-in value especially fragile. A correction formula or bootstrap would require its own assumptions; we do not silently add one to this two-draw calculation.

A difference of biased estimates: exact two-pair investigation (35–45 additional minutes)

The entropy result concerns one concave functional. Does it tell the direction of bias when three estimated entropies are combined? The mutual-information lesson gives I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)−H(X,Y). Subtracting the third entropy means three downward-bias statements do not determine the sign of the resulting bias. We can settle a small case exactly.

Each observation is a pair (X,Y), with both coordinates binary. Its four possible values are 00, 01, 10, 11, with fixed source probabilities a,b,c,d, respectively. These sum to 1 and may include zeros. Take two independent observations from this same joint source. Independence between observations does not require independence of X and Y within an observation.

Form one empirical joint table from those two pairs, then obtain both empirical marginals by summing that table's rows and columns. Define Î=Ĥ(X)+Ĥ(Y)−Ĥ(X,Y), always using that same data. These are bits per paired observation; Î is not divided by the number of observations. Using separately collected or mismatched marginal samples would no longer evaluate mutual information of this empirical joint law.

Predict the bias. For independent fair X and Y, must Î always be zero? For a fair X copied perfectly to Y, must Î always be one? Compare the two sources, then inspect a repeated pair and two pairs with both coordinates changed. The known source and the inspected record play different roles.

Prove the complete two-observation law

There are sixteen ordered records, each weighted by the product of its two source probabilities. If the two observed pairs are identical, all three empirical entropies are zero. If exactly one coordinate changes, that coordinate has empirical entropy one, the unchanged coordinate has entropy zero, and the empirical joint entropy is one; hence Î=0. If both coordinates change, each empirical marginal is fair and the joint table has two equally weighted cells. Then Î=1+1−1=1 bit.

Thus Î equals one precisely for records (00,11), (11,00), (01,10), and (10,01). Their source probabilities are ad, da, bc and cb. Every other record gives zero. Consequently

P(I^=1)=E[I^]=2(ad+bc)

The other mass is P(Î=0)=1−2(ad+bc). Numerical equality between a probability and the expected bit value occurs because this estimator takes only the values zero and one. They remain different quantities with different units. This finite n=2 identity is not a general formula for arbitrary sample sizes.

Bias can point in either direction

  • Independent fair bits. Here (a,b,c,d)=(1,1,1,1)/4 and true I=0 exactly. The mean estimate is 2(1/16+1/16)=1/4 bit, so bias is +1/4. A record such as (00,11) has empirical information one despite zero population information.
  • A fair bit copied perfectly. Here (a,b,c,d)=(1,0,0,1)/2 and true I=1 exactly. The mean estimate is 2(1/4)=1/2 bit, so bias is −1/2. Repeated records (00,00) and (11,11) each give zero and each have source probability 1/4. The source's dependence is perfect even though those empirical marginals are constant.
  • The quarter-error lamp. The existing joint law (3,1,1,3)/8 gives mean estimate 2(9/64+1/64)=5/16 bit. True information is 1−h₂(1/4)≈0.188722 bit, so bias is approximately +0.123778 bit. The source's logarithmic value and this difference are rounded; the estimator law and its expectation are exact fractions.

Here h₂(p)=−p log₂p−(1−p)log₂(1−p). The source information is a fixed target, the estimate is a function of a record, and bias averages its errors across the fixed source's records. Even a possible record with zero empirical cells cannot establish zero source probabilities. In the copying model, off-diagonal source cells really are zero by assumption: records containing them are impossible and have no weight in the estimator law, although the empirical statistic can still be evaluated on that hypothetical record.

Paninski's Estimation of Entropy and Mutual Information, Section 3.3 and Section 4, distinguishes entropy's downward bias from mutual-information bias of either sign. That paper's default logarithms are natural; the finite calculations here use log₂ and are derived locally in bits. We infer no correction method or confidence interval from these two-observation laws.

Audit a small sample

Exercise 1. A fair binary source is sampled three times. Calculate the plug-in entropy for 000 and for 001. Which sample proves the source is fair?

Hint for exercise 1

Turn each sample into frequency counts, then use the entropy formula. Keep the true law separate.

Full solution to exercise 1

For 000, p̂=(1,0), so Ĥ=0. For 001, p̂=(2/3,1/3), so Ĥ=−(2/3)log₂(2/3)−(1/3)log₂(1/3)≈0.918 bit. Neither sample proves fairness: both have positive probability under the stipulated fair source, and the same finite samples could occur under many other positive-probability laws.

Exercise 2. A claimed theorem says “Every plug-in entropy estimate is below the true entropy.” Use the two-draw fair-bit enumeration to test the wording, then state the correct average claim.

Hint for exercise 2

Look at the 01 and 10 rows and compare each value with the true one-bit entropy.

Full solution to exercise 2

The 01 and 10 estimates equal the true entropy, so “below” is already false if interpreted strictly. With other nonuniform sources, a balanced observed sample can even exceed the true entropy. The supported statement is about expectation under repeated independent samples: E[Ĥ]≤H(P). In this fair two-draw case, the expectation is 1/2 bit, below the target 1 bit by 1/2 bit.

Transfer tasks for the optional mutual-information investigation

Exercise 3. A fresh independent source. X is Bernoulli(1/4), Y is Bernoulli(2/3), and X and Y are independent. Take two iid paired observations. Derive the four source probabilities in the order 00,01,10,11, then find the full two-point law of Î, its expectation and its bias. Why must the sixteen records not be averaged with equal weights?

Hint for exercise 3

Form products of the X and Y probabilities. Weight each ordered record by the product of its two joint probabilities, then use the proved two-observation characterization.

Full solution to exercise 3

The joint probabilities are (3/4×1/3, 3/4×2/3, 1/4×1/3, 1/4×2/3)=(3,6,1,2)/12. Independence gives true I=0 exactly. The probability of Î=1 is 2[(3/12)(2/12)+(6/12)(1/12)]=24/144=1/6. Hence P(Î=0)=5/6, E[Î]=1/6 bit and bias=+1/6 bit. As a second check, the chance that the two X values differ is 2(1/4)(3/4)=3/8; the chance that the Y values differ is 2(2/3)(1/3)=4/9. The X sequence and Y sequence are independent under this source, so both differences occur with probability (3/8)(4/9)=1/6. The sixteen ordered records are not equiprobable because their source-cell product weights vary; for example, (01,01) has probability 1/4 while (10,10) has probability 1/144.

Exercise 4. The pairing still matters when the marginals agree. Now change the sample size to three. Compare the paired records A=(00,00,11) and B=(00,01,10). Show that both empirical X and empirical Y have one-frequency 1/3 in each record. Compute their empirical joint entropies and mutual informations, and give their exact information difference. Does the n=2 estimator formula apply?

Hint for exercise 4

For A, two joint cells have frequencies 2/3 and 1/3. For B, three joint cells each have frequency 1/3. Compute all three entropies from each record's own joint table.

Full solution to exercise 4

A has joint counts (2,0,0,1), while B has (1,1,1,0). Their row and column counts are both (2,1), so all empirical marginal entropies equal h₂(1/3)=log₂3−2/3. For A, the joint entropy is also h₂(1/3), giving Î_A=h₂(1/3)=log₂3−2/3≈0.918296 bit. For B, the joint entropy is log₂3, giving Î_B=2h₂(1/3)−log₂3=log₂3−4/3≈0.251629 bit. Their difference is exactly 2/3 bit. Same marginals leave different joint pairings possible. These are individual three-observation statistic values, not their sampling expectations. The n=2 two-point law and expectation formula do not apply to n=3.

Report what the data actually support

The plug-in formula evaluates entropy at sample frequencies. It is easy to compute, but a tiny sample may miss possible outcomes, and its downward bias is an average statement rather than a guarantee for each data set. Here all four two-draw outcomes were enumerated, making both the target and bias transparent. For real measurement, report sample size, how observations were obtained, the assumed alphabet, and uncertainty about the estimated probabilities. Do not present a histogram from a few draws as the true source distribution.

A useful stopping question is whether a zero empirical count means an impossible symbol. It does not without a model assumption or enough evidence; count zero and probability zero are different statements. The MIT information and entropy text supports the entropy definition. Archer, Park and Pillow's JMLR paper, Section 2.1, identifies the empirical plug-in estimator and its negative bias in undersampled data. The finite-sample enumeration here is original; the general bias direction is tied explicitly to concavity rather than inferred from that single example. The optional paired-data investigation shows why subtracting entropy estimates can produce mutual-information bias in either direction, and why the empirical marginals must come from the same joint sample.
