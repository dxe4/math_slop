Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Can a line segment become a circle?

A segment can be bent almost into a ring. Closing the last gap, however, changes what happens when a point is removed. Topology makes this difference precise with a homeomorphism: a reversible continuous correspondence. The test is stronger than whether two sketches look alike, and it gives a short proof that a closed interval is not a circle.

Background capsule. A function is bijective if every point of its target is reached once and only once; then it has an inverse function. A map of metric spaces is continuous when the preimage of each open set is open. A space is connected when it cannot be split into two disjoint, nonempty sets that are open relative to it. An interval of ℝ is connected; we use that standard interval theorem here. A cut point of a connected space X is a point p for which X without p is disconnected.

Compare two removals

Worked example. In [0,1], remove 1/2. The remaining points split into [0,1/2) and (1/2,1]. Each part is nonempty and open relative to the remaining space: for example, [0,1/2) is the remaining space intersected with (−1,1/2). The two parts cover it without overlap. Thus 1/2 is a cut point. By contrast, removing endpoint 0 leaves (0,1], an interval and therefore connected.

On the unit circle S¹={(x,y):x²+y²=1}, remove (1,0). Starting just above that point and walking counterclockwise around the rest reaches any other point without crossing the gap. More precisely, t↦(cos t,sin t) maps the open interval (0,2π) continuously onto S¹ without (1,0). A continuous image of an interval is connected. Every other removed point is the same case after rotation. Hence a circle has no cut point. This argument uses the connected-image theorem, which can be proved directly from open preimages and is developed in the connectedness article.

Why a reversible map preserves the test

Exact definition. For topological spaces X and Y, a homeomorphism is a bijection f:X→Y such that both f and its inverse are continuous:

f:X→Y homeomorphism⇔f bijective, f and f−1 continuous

The inverse condition is essential. A continuous bijection may fail to have continuous inverse; for example, the map from [0,2π) onto the circle given by t↦(cos t,sin t) is bijective and continuous, but its inverse jumps near (1,0): points approaching (1,0) clockwise have parameter approaching 2π, while their image approaches parameter 0. This does not contradict the circle argument above, because [0,2π) includes one endpoint and has different local structure there.

Invariant proof. If f is a homeomorphism and p∈X, the restriction f:X∖{p}→Y∖{f(p)} is still bijective and continuous; its inverse is the restriction of f−1. Therefore the punctured spaces are homeomorphic. A homeomorphism carries an open separation in either direction to an open separation, by continuity of the inverse and f respectively. Consequently X∖{p} is disconnected exactly when Y∖{f(p)} is disconnected. Cut points are preserved. Since [0,1] has an interior cut point and S¹ has none, no homeomorphism [0,1]→S¹ exists.

This proof is complete once the interval-connectedness and continuous-image facts stated in the capsule are accepted. Those facts are standard and are proved for the latter in connectedness; a proof of interval connectedness uses the least-upper-bound property of ℝ. We have not classified all one-dimensional spaces or shown every physical bending can be represented by a homeomorphism.

Test maps and invariants

1. Is f:[0,1]→[2,5], f(t)=2+3t, a homeomorphism? Give its inverse and check both continuity claims.

Hint for question 1

Solve y=2+3t for t. Both resulting formulas are Lipschitz on their domains.

Full solution for question 1

f is strictly increasing and maps endpoints 0 and 1 to 2 and 5, so it is bijective onto [2,5]. Its inverse is f−1(y)=(y−2)/3. For inputs s,t, |f(s)−f(t)|=3|s−t|, so δ=ε/3 proves continuity. For outputs u,v, |f−1(u)−f−1(v)|=|u−v|/3, so δ=3ε proves inverse continuity. The relative endpoint topologies are included.

2. Could a letter “Y,” modelled as three closed segments meeting only at one central point, be homeomorphic to [0,1]? Use components after deleting one point.

Hint for question 2

Delete the branch point. How many connected pieces remain? Compare deletion of any point of an interval.

Full solution for question 2

Removing the center of Y leaves three disjoint open arms, each connected, so there are three connected components. Removing an interior point of [0,1] leaves exactly two interval components; removing an endpoint leaves one. A homeomorphism would restrict to a homeomorphism of the punctured spaces and preserve their component count. No point of [0,1] has a three-component complement, so no such homeomorphism exists.

Use properties a map cannot erase

A homeomorphism preserves topology in both directions. Cut points distinguish an interval from a circle even when a drawing tempts us to join the interval’s ends. The cut-point test cannot distinguish every pair of spaces; the absence of a cut point alone does not establish a homeomorphism. For another global property preserved by continuous maps, study compactness.
