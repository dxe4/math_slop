Two clear directions, one hidden cliff

Suppose a function has an eastward and a northward derivative at a point. Have we learned its behavior on every approach? No: two axes are only two of infinitely many paths. A sharp counterexample lets us separate partial derivatives, directional derivatives, continuity, and a total derivative.

Background capsule. For f(x,y), the partial at the origin in x is the limit [f(h,0)−f(0,0)]/h; the y partial uses (0,h). Continuity at the origin requires f(x,y) to approach f(0,0) along every path, not only axes. Total differentiability requires f(h,k)=f(0,0)+L(h,k)+o(√(h²+k²)) for one linear map L. That condition implies continuity because both L(h,k) and its smaller error tend to zero.

Walk the axes, then the diagonal

Worked example. Define f(x,y)=xy/(x²+y²) away from the origin and f(0,0)=0. On either coordinate axis the numerator is zero, so f(h,0)=f(0,h)=0. Both partial derivatives at the origin exist and equal zero. A test limited to those directions would suggest a flat surface.

Along the diagonal x=y=t≠0, however, f(t,t)=t²/(2t²)=1/2. The value stays one-half no matter how close t gets to zero, while f(0,0)=0. Along x=−y=t, the value is −1/2. Thus there is no single two-variable limit at the origin and no continuity there. For an explicit exact-value table, t=1, 1/10, or 1/1000 always gives zero on the axes and one-half on the main diagonal. More decimal sampling cannot repair that disagreement.

Try changing the declared central value. Assigning f(0,0)=1/2 would match the diagonal limit, but the axes would still have values zero. Assigning any other constant also leaves at least two disagreeing path limits. The discontinuity cannot be repaired by redefining only the origin. This is different from a removable one-variable hole, where all approaching values already share a common limit and only the value at the missing point needs correction.

What exactly fails?

Exact conclusion. Both coordinate partial derivatives exist at the origin, but f is neither continuous nor totally differentiable there. In fact, for a nonaxis unit direction u=(a,b) with ab≠0, f(tu)=ab/(a²+b²)=ab for every t≠0. The quotient [f(tu)−0]/t=ab/t has no finite two-sided limit. On the axes it is zero. Even directional differentiability fails in most directions.

The proof of noncontinuity needs only the diagonal sequence t=1/n. If continuity held, values on that sequence would approach zero; instead every value is one-half. Since differentiability implies continuity, noncontinuity rules out differentiability. This is an implication argument, not merely a claim based on a plot.

A common sufficient condition goes the other way: if both partials exist in a neighborhood and are continuous at the point, then f is differentiable there. That is a separate theorem; this example violates its continuity premise. Do not misread the counterexample as saying partial derivatives are useless. They correctly describe coordinate-axis rates, but they cannot alone license the gradient-dot-direction formula or a tangent plane.

The example is bounded between −1/2 and 1/2 away from the origin, because 2|xy|≤x²+y². Boundedness therefore does not rescue continuity. The obstruction is persistent directional disagreement: as the distance to the origin tends to zero, diagonal values remain fixed away from the declared value. This also shows why a numerical grid that omits the diagonal could misleadingly suggest a smooth center.

Test the hidden path

1. Calculation. For the same f, compute f(t,2t) and decide whether continuity at the origin is possible.

Hint for question 1

Cancel t² only after declaring t≠0.

Worked solution for question 1

f(t,2t)=2t²/(t²+4t²)=2/5 for every t≠0. As t→0 the path limit is 2/5, different from f(0,0)=0. Thus continuity is impossible even without considering the main diagonal.

2. Explanation. A student finds fx(0,0)=fy(0,0)=0 and writes f(h,k)=o(√(h²+k²)). Locate the unjustified step.

Hint for question 2

Ask whether axis estimates control points where both h and k are nonzero.

Worked solution for question 2

The partial limits only give f(h,0)=o(|h|) and f(0,k)=o(|k|). They give no estimate for f(h,k) when both coordinates vary. Along h=k=t, f=1/2, and [f(t,t)]/√(2t²)=1/(2√2|t|), which diverges. The proposed remainder estimate is false.

Use the correct level of evidence

Coordinate partials answer two restricted limit questions. Continuity requires every approach, and differentiability requires one linear approximation with a uniform small remainder. This example proves the separation exactly. To continue, ask which additional local conditions guarantee differentiability; continuous partial derivatives near the point are a standard sufficient route, while an isolated point's partials are not.
