Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Can a perfect curve still be a poor story?

A tiny gallery records visitor counts over three opening days: 10, 12 and 14. Two simple formulas can fit those points exactly yet disagree about a fourth day. That makes the data useful for exploring interpolation, held-out prediction and mechanism. No invented visitor table proves how real galleries behave; it only lets us see what a fitted formula does and does not establish.

Background capsule. A function f(x) predicts an output at input x. A residual is observed minus predicted, y−f(x), in the output's units. Interpolation predicts inside the observed input range; extrapolation predicts beyond it. A held-out observation is set aside before fitting and used afterward to assess predictions. A good training fit means small residuals on used data; it cannot by itself establish a causal explanation.

Let x be day number and y visitors per day. Our training observations are (1,10), (2,12), (3,14). Hold day 4 aside; suppose its recorded count is 16. We will compare two models fitted only to days 1–3.

Two formulas, one training table

Worked example. The straight-line rule f(x)=8+2x hits all three training points and predicts f(4)=16. A second rule g(x)=8+2x+5(x−1)(x−2)(x−3) also hits them, because the cubic product is zero at x=1,2,3. At x=4 the product is 3×2×1=6, so g(4)=16+30=46. Both have zero training residual at every observed training day. Against the held-out count 16, f has residual 0 and g has residual −30 visitors.

The line predicts this held-out day better, but a single held-out day cannot prove the line's mechanism. Perhaps opening hours, advertising or weather change. If we inspect many candidate curves and repeatedly choose the one that does best on day 4, day 4 has become part of model selection; it is no longer an untouched test. A fresh future day would then be needed for a new honest check.

What a residual proves

For observations (xᵢ,yᵢ) and a named prediction rule f, define

ri=yi−f(xi)

This subtraction is exact and has the units of y. Zero residual means the formula equals that observed number at that input; it says nothing by itself about unobserved inputs. The cubic construction is a direct proof: any multiple c(x−1)(x−2)(x−3) added to the line fits all three training points, and different c values can make arbitrarily different day-4 predictions. Data at finitely many x values do not uniquely determine a general function without restricting the model class.

A held-out error checks predictive performance under the split and conditions used. If future days differ in an important way, even a good holdout result may fail to transfer. Causal explanation is a stronger claim: to say opening-hours changes caused counts to rise, we would need a design addressing competing causes, not a regression line through daily totals. The formula g is mathematically valid; its term has no justified gallery mechanism in this example.

One can prefer a simpler rule as a tentative prediction when data are scarce, but “simpler” is a modelling choice, not a theorem that the line is true. State the use: estimate a nearby day under stable conditions, with an error check and a limit note.

Audit the fit

1. Calculate. On a separately held-out day 5, the observed count is 18. Find the predictions of f and g above, their residuals, and each model's absolute error.

Hint for question 1

At x=5 the cubic product is 4×3×2=24.

Full solution to question 1

f(5)=8+10=18, residual 18−18=0 and absolute error 0. g(5)=18+5×24=138, residual 18−138=−120 and absolute error 120 visitors. These numbers compare the two rules on this example's fifth day; they do not guarantee future performance.

2. Explain a boundary. A colleague refits the cubic coefficient after seeing day 4 so that g(4)=16, then calls day 4 an independent validation. Why is that claim incorrect?

Hint for question 2

Ask whether the held-out value influenced the chosen model.

Full solution to question 2

Setting the coefficient using day 4 makes that observation part of fitting. It is no longer held out, even if it was hidden during an earlier step. A fair validation would use a new observation not used to select the coefficient or model class. The revised rule might still predict well, but day 4 cannot be counted as independent evidence for it.

Fit, prediction and explanation are different claims

A residual describes a particular observation and formula exactly. A holdout checks a prediction under a chosen split. Neither establishes why visitor counts changed. The cubic's perfect training fit and poor held-out result provide a concrete counterexample to equating fit with reliability. Model validation combines this check with units, boundaries and sensitivity.
