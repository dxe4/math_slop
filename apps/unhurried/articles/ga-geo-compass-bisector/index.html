Can an unmarked compass halve an angle?

Suppose two rays OA and OC form an angle. You can bisect it without reading a number from a protractor. A straightedge draws the joining ray; a compass transfers equal lengths. The exactness comes from congruent triangles, not from pixels appearing symmetric.

Background capsule. A circle with centre O and radius r contains precisely points at distance r from O. Two triangles are congruent by side-side-side (SSS) when their three corresponding side lengths agree; then corresponding angles agree. A bisector of a nonzero angle smaller than a straight angle is a ray in its interior that makes the two adjacent angles equal. We assume an ideal straightedge and compass: exact circles, exact intersections and exact lines, with no measurement error.

Make two copies of the same triangle

Worked example. Choose radius r>0. Draw a circle centred at O, marking its intersections X and Y with the two rays. Thus OX=OY=r. Choose a second radius s large enough that equal circles centred at X and Y meet at a point B inside the angle. Draw those circles and join O to B. The claim is that OB is the bisector.

For a numerical instance, put O=(0,0), X=(2,0), Y=(0,2), and choose s=2. The inside intersection is B=(2,2): both XB and YB have length two. The drawn ray from (0,0) through (2,2) has direction (1,1), visibly halfway between the coordinate axes. The equal-side table, rather than the visual symmetry of this special instance, is what works for every allowed angle.

The congruence argument

Exact statement and proof. Let X and Y lie on the two distinct rays of an angle at O with OX=OY>0. Let B be an intersection of equal-radius circles centred at X and Y lying inside the angle. Then ray OB bisects that angle:

∠XOB=∠BOY

OX=OY follows from the first circle. XB=YB follows from the equal radii of the second pair. OB is identical to itself. By SSS, triangles OXB and OYB are congruent, and their angles at O are corresponding angles. Since B was chosen inside the original angle, these two equal angles partition it. Therefore each is exactly half of ∠XOY.

There is a small existence condition behind the instructions. X and Y are distinct for a nonzero angle, so the circles of radius s meet in two points when s>XY/2. The perpendicular bisector of XY passes through O because OX=OY. One intersection lies inside the smaller angle when that angle is strictly between 0 and π; select that one. Choosing s=XY/2 makes the circles tangent at the midpoint of XY, which is still inside, but drawing a transverse intersection is easier to specify. At the zero-angle case X=Y and the intended pair of triangles is degenerate.

The construction establishes an exact relation in ideal Euclidean geometry. A physical pencil line has width; two displayed circles may have visibly imperfect intersections. Those practical errors do not alter the mathematical claim and cannot serve as its proof. The proof uses SSS and the ability to construct and intersect Euclidean circles. It does not prove that every visually symmetric sketch has equal angles.

Use the construction deliberately

1. Calculation. An angle measures 74°. After the construction, what are ∠XOB and ∠BOY? Which equalities justify the answer?

Hint for question 1

Find the three matching sides before halving the measure.

Full solution for question 1

The first arc gives OX=OY, the equal later arcs give XB=YB, and OB is common. SSS gives ∠XOB=∠BOY. They add to 74° because B lies inside the angle, so each is 37°.

2. Failure test. Suppose the second circles have different radii. Is OB guaranteed to bisect? Construct a concrete numerical counterexample.

Hint for question 2

Use X=(2,0), Y=(0,2), and B=(1,2). Compare the two distances from B.

Full solution for question 2

XB=√5 while YB=1, so B can be an intersection of circles with those unequal radii. OB points in direction (1,2), not (1,1), hence does not split the right angle equally. The SSS argument loses its XB=YB step.

Where the certainty lives

The compass transfers two pairs of equal lengths, and SSS converts those equal lengths into equal angles. This is a complete Euclidean proof for a nondegenerate angle and the specified inside intersection. A zero angle has no distinct sides to bisect by this recipe; choosing the outside circle intersection would split the reflex region instead. The same method can be repeated to quarter an angle, although it cannot produce every desired numerical angle by straightedge and compass. The important habit is to annotate the equal radii before claiming the pictured ray is a bisector.
