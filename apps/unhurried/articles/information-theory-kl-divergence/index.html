Powers and logarithms

For a positive integer n, aⁿ means a multiplied by itself n times. For example, 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. For a nonzero base, a⁰=1 and a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. A negative exponent describes a reciprocal.

Worked example. With base 3, the logarithm asks which exponent produces a positive input. Since 3⁴=81, log₃(81)=4. More generally, for a positive base b different from 1, log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with x positive.

34=81log3(81)=4

Multiplying powers with the same base adds their exponents: 2³×2²=2⁵. For positive inputs, this becomes the logarithm rule log₂(uv)=log₂(u)+log₂(v). It is a rule about a product.

Try one. Find log₂(1/8) and explain the negative sign.

Worked answer

Because 2⁻³=1/2³=1/8, the logarithm is −3. For a base larger than 1, inputs between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms.

Watch the operation. Logarithms do not generally turn a sum into a sum: log₂(2+2)=2, while log₂(2)+log₂(2)=2 happens to agree in this case. Using 2+4 instead gives log₂(6), which differs from 1+2=3 because 2³=8.

How costly is a mistaken probability model?

A stipulated source has law P, but a predictor or code uses Q. KL divergence measures its excess expected log loss over using P. Averaging Q's predictions under P differs from averaging P's predictions under Q: direction matters.

Background capsule. A distribution on a finite alphabet assigns nonnegative probabilities summing to one. Log₂ converts ratios to bits; log₂(a/b) is positive when a>b and negative when a<b. Individual terms in a KL sum can therefore be negative, even though the complete sum is nonnegative. Support means the symbols with positive probability. We define 0 log₂(0/q)=0 when q is positive; when p>0 and q=0, the divergence is infinite.

Compare two guesses for a coin-like source

Worked example. Let P=(1/2,1/2) and Q=(3/4,1/4) for symbols A and B. Under P, using Q gives average log loss −(1/2)log₂(3/4)−(1/2)log₂(1/4)=about 1.208 bits per draw. Using P gives H(P)=1 bit. The extra is about 0.208 bit. Algebraically, D(P||Q)=(1/2)log₂(2/3)+(1/2)log₂2=(1/2)log₂(4/3), the same result.

D(P||Q)½ log₂(4/3)0.208
D(Q||P)¾ log₂(3/2)+¼ log₂(1/2)0.189

The second row uses Q as the averaging law and P as the model. Its value differs, so KL divergence is asymmetric. It is not a metric distance. A second obstruction is support: if P assigns B positive chance but Q assigns B zero, a draw of B has infinite log loss under Q, making D(P||Q)=∞. A finite observed run without B cannot erase that model-level possibility.

The formula and Gibbs inequality

DKL(P∥Q)=∑xp(x)log2p(x)q(x)

Exact claim. For finite distributions, D(P||Q)≥0, with equality exactly when P=Q, provided divergence is interpreted as infinite if Q vanishes on positive P support. To see the finite case, use ln u≤u−1 for u>0, with equality only at u=1. For each x with p(x)>0, set u=q(x)/p(x). Then −p(x)ln(q(x)/p(x))≥p(x)−q(x). Sum over positive-P symbols. Their p masses total one, while their q masses total at most one, so the right side is nonnegative. Divide by ln2 to change nats to bits. Equality forces q=p on positive support and leaves no q mass elsewhere. This is Gibbs inequality in a finite setting; an infinite value already satisfies nonnegativity.

When support makes it finite, cross-entropy H(P,Q)=−Σp log₂q=H(P)+D(P||Q). This is expected log loss under P, not a guaranteed sample penalty: chance can favor Q on one run. Asymmetry also prevents using KL as a metric in triangle-inequality arguments.

Optional investigation: when does merging symbols lose no KL?

Change the observation, keep both laws

Grouping reveals a symbol's block but hides its identity. Does that strictly reduce KL between two stipulated laws? Apply the same deterministic grouping to both; this is neither Bayesian updating nor estimation from data.

Extra background. A map g gives each symbol a block label. For a block B, add its original probabilities separately under the two laws: pB=Σi∈Bpᵢ and qB=Σi∈Bqᵢ. These are the coarse laws Pg, Qg. When the corresponding block mass is positive, divide by it to obtain the conditional distribution of the hidden symbol inside that block. Each law has its own normalizer.

Use new four-symbol laws P=(3,3,1,1)/8 and Q=(2,2,2,2)/8. Their symbol likelihood ratios P/Q are (3/2,3/2,1/2,1/2). The full divergence is c=(3/4)log₂(3/2)−1/4≈0.188722 bits. A grouping 12 | 34 retains only whether the symbol is in {1,2} or {3,4}; grouping 13 | 24 instead retains membership in {1,3} or {2,4}. Both discard the original symbol.

Predict before inspecting. Which grouping keeps equal likelihood ratios together? Check both blocks' conditional laws under P and Q before deciding whether KL is preserved.

The exact decomposition

First assume finite D(P||Q). On a finite alphabet, this means qᵢ>0 wherever pᵢ>0. Every pB>0 then has qB>0. Define PB(i)=pᵢ/pB and QB(i)=qᵢ/qB for every symbol in that block. For each positive-pᵢ cell, split its log ratio as log₂(pᵢ/qᵢ)=log₂(pB/qB)+log₂(PB(i)/QB(i)). Multiply by pᵢ and sum inside each block. The first part becomes pBlog₂(pB/qB); the second becomes pBD(PB||QB).

Let F=D(P||Q) be the full divergence, C=D(Pg||Qg) the coarse divergence, and L=ΣB:pB>0pBD(PB||QB) the weighted within-block divergence. Summing the split gives the exact identity

F=C+L

Each complete conditional divergence is nonnegative by the Gibbs inequality proved above, so L≥0 and C≤F. This is deterministic data processing for KL. Individual terms need not be nonnegative: in the preserving example the coarse sum has terms (3/4)log₂(3/2) and −1/4. The conclusion concerns their total and the complete conditional divergences.

Equality requires every active block. With finite divergence, C=F exactly when PB=QB as complete vectors in every block with pB>0. Every weight there is positive, so their nonnegative sum vanishes exactly when every conditional divergence vanishes. Equivalently, pᵢ=(pB/qB)qᵢ for every i in each active block. This includes zero-pᵢ coordinates. Merely checking constant ratios on positive-P cells can miss Q mass elsewhere in that block. One matching inspected block cannot establish this all-block statement.

Zero masses and infinite divergences: which quantities exist?

A zero-P cell contributes zero, including pᵢ=qᵢ=0; do not evaluate log(0/0). A block with pB=0 is omitted from L. Its P-conditional is not needed, whether qB is positive or zero. Common-null blocks require neither conditional. Inside a positive-P block, however, Q's mass on zero-P cells still matters when comparing the complete vectors.

If every positive-P block is Q-positive, the coarse divergence C is finite even if F is infinite. All required conditionals exist. A positive-P/zero-Q cell inside one such block gives an infinite conditional divergence, and the identity means ∞=finite+∞. The sum uses only positive pB, never 0·∞. With finite C, equality is still equivalent to all active conditional laws matching; infinite F cannot equal finite C.

If some pB>0 has qB=0, both F and C are infinite, and Q's conditional in that block is undefined. State the support failure directly. Do not form that conditional, subtract ∞−∞, or call equal infinity labels a no-loss certificate. The finite-coarse equality criterion is not asserted there. The extended inequality still holds because both sides are infinite.

Check direction and support

Exercise 1. Let P=(1/2,1/2) and Q=(3/4,1/4). Compute D(P||Q) and D(Q||P) to three decimals. Which distribution supplies weights in each sum?

Hint for exercise 1

Write each log ratio before rounding; reverse both the weights and the ratio for the second quantity.

Full solution to exercise 1

D(P||Q)=½log₂((1/2)/(3/4))+½log₂((1/2)/(1/4))=½log₂(4/3)≈0.208 bit, weighted by P. D(Q||P)=¾log₂((3/4)/(1/2))+¼log₂((1/4)/(1/2))=¾log₂(3/2)−¼≈0.189 bit, weighted by Q. Their inequality demonstrates asymmetry without relying on a picture.

Exercise 2. P=(0,1) and Q=(1/2,1/2). Find both directional divergences. Explain why one can be finite while the other is infinite.

Hint for exercise 2

In the reverse direction, Q assigns positive chance to a symbol that P forbids.

Full solution to exercise 2

D(P||Q)=1·log₂(1/(1/2))=1 bit; the P-zero first term contributes zero. But D(Q||P)=∞ because Q gives the first symbol probability 1/2 while P gives it zero, so the required log ratio has zero denominator. The two directions average over different supports, not merely different numerical weights.

A changed task: nonuniform reference weights inside each block

Exercise 3. Keep grouping 12 | 34 fixed throughout. Let Q=(1/6,1/3,1/4,1/4) and P=(1/4,1/2,1/8,1/8). (a) Find both coarse laws and all block conditionals. Does grouping preserve KL? (b) Replace P by P′=(1/8,5/8,1/8,1/8). Show that the coarse laws remain fixed, identify the changed conditional and derive its exact additional divergence and an approximate value. (c) Why can inspecting only block 34 fail to establish a global no-loss conclusion? (d) For this Q and grouping, find every generating law R that loses no KL, including zero-mass blocks. If R's coarse masses must be (3/4,1/4), how many no-loss laws remain? These changed laws are outside the controls.

Hint for exercise 3

Normalize separately by each law's own block mass. For equality, compare every cell in every active block. Weight conditional KL by the generating law's block mass. For (d), let a be R's first block mass and allocate it in Q's conditional proportions; handle a=0 or 1 without conditioning on a zero-R block.

Full solution to exercise 3

(a) A nonuniform reference can still lose nothing. Q's coarse masses are (1/2,1/2), while P's are (3/4,1/4). In block 12, P's conditional is ((1/4)/(3/4),(1/2)/(3/4))=(1/3,2/3), and Q's is ((1/6)/(1/2),(1/3)/(1/2))=(1/3,2/3). In block 34 both are (1/2,1/2). Symbol ratios are (3/2,3/2,1/2,1/2). Every active conditional KL is zero; full and coarse divergence both equal c=(3/4)log₂(3/2)−1/4≈0.188722 bits.

(b) Same coarse laws, different hidden detail. For the fixed grouping 12 | 34, P′ still gives (3/4,1/4), so coarse KL remains c. Its first conditional becomes (1/6,5/6), whereas Q's remains (1/3,2/3). The second conditional still matches. The first conditional divergence is d=(1/6)log₂(1/2)+(5/6)log₂(5/4)=(5/6)log₂(5/4)−1/6≈0.101607 bits. Multiply by P′(12)=3/4 to obtain the discarded total r=(3/4)d=(5/8)log₂(5/4)−1/8≈0.076205 bits. Thus full D(P′||Q)=c+r≈0.264927 bits. Direct fine-alphabet calculation confirms (1/8)log₂(3/4)+(5/8)log₂(15/8)−1/4=c+r.

The unchanged-coarse statement uses that fixed grouping. Under the crossed grouping, Q's coarse masses would be (5/12,7/12), P's (3/8,5/8), and P′'s (1/4,3/4), so the generating coarse law would change.

(c) One inspected term cannot certify the sum. Block 34 contributes zero for both P and P′, but block 12 contributes positive r for P′. Equality requires matching complete vectors in every positive-generating-mass block.

(d) Characterize every no-loss law. Q's conditional proportions force R=(a/3,2a/3,(1−a)/2,(1−a)/2) for 0≤a≤1. For 0<a<1 both conditional rows match. At a=0 the first R block has zero mass and is skipped; at a=1 the second is skipped. In either endpoint the remaining active block matches, and fine=coarse=1 bit. Conversely the equality criterion forces those proportions in every active block, while a zero block has all cell masses zero. Requiring coarse masses (3/4,1/4) fixes a=3/4 and gives the original P as the unique no-loss law. P′ has the same block totals but fails the conditional requirement.

Use divergence in its proper direction

KL divergence is nonnegative extra expected log loss when Q predicts outcomes actually generated under P. Its support condition can turn it infinite, and reversing arguments changes the question. In information theory, mutual information is KL divergence between a joint law and the product of its marginals; this supplies a proof of nonnegativity for that measure. In data analysis, both P and Q may be estimated, which adds uncertainty and can create misleading infinities from zero empirical counts. Those are model-estimation questions, not defects in the finite identity.

At a stopping point, state which law generates observations before writing D(P||Q). The MIT information and entropy text supports the information-divergence framework. The two-symbol distributions, directional calculations, and exercises on this page are original.
