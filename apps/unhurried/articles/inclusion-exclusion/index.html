Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

Try a laboratory

  • Overlap ledger

A festival attendee may appear on two mailing lists.

At a festival, 18 attendees joined a music list, 13 joined a food list, and 7 joined both. How many joined at least one list? Adding 18+13=31 double counts the seven people in the overlap. Subtracting seven gives 24. This correction looks simple, but with three lists we must track whether a person in all three was subtracted too many times. A membership ledger makes the reason visible.

Prerequisite capsule. For finite sets A and B, the union A∪B contains items in at least one set; the intersection A∩B contains items in both. A cardinality |A| counts distinct items, not entries in a list that might repeat a name. If a question asks for “A or B,” the usual inclusive meaning counts someone who belongs to both once.

Count a concrete case

Worked example. Split the music set into two disjoint pieces: seven people in both lists and 11 only in music. The food list has 13 people, including those same seven. Counting 11 music-only people plus all 13 food people gives 24 distinct attendees. Equivalently, 18+13−7=24. For three sets, imagine a person in exactly one list: their contribution to |A|+|B|+|C| is one. Someone in exactly two contributes two, then one pairwise-intersection subtraction reduces it to one. Someone in all three contributes three, is removed three times by the pairwise terms, and must be added back once by the triple intersection. This is why the signs alternate.

The exact rule and its proof

|A∪B|=|A|+|B|−|A∩B|

Inclusion–exclusion for two and three sets. For any finite A,B,C, the two-set formula is displayed. Its proof partitions A∪B into three disjoint regions: A only, B only, and A∩B. Summing |A|+|B| counts the third region twice, so one subtraction leaves each region once. For three sets, |A∪B∪C| equals |A|+|B|+|C|−|A∩B|−|A∩C|−|B∩C|+|A∩B∩C|. This is proved item by item using the membership counts in the preceding paragraph, which cover every possible item of the union. Empty intersections contribute zero; the formulas do not assume the sets are mutually exclusive. The same alternating idea extends further, but that general formula is beyond today's proof.

Where the rule fails. Subtracting only one overlap from three lists can fail. Let A=B=C={x}. Then |A|+|B|+|C|=3, and removing just |A∩B| gives 2, while the union has one item. Even subtracting all three pairwise overlaps yields zero; the triple intersection must restore one. A second failure occurs when list entries themselves contain duplicate names: set cardinalities must first collapse repeated entries.

Worked example: recovering the regions of three lists

Suppose three finite lists A,B,C have sizes 10, 9, and 8. Their pairwise intersection sizes are |A∩B| = 4, |A∩C| = 3, and |B∩C| = 2, while the triple intersection has size 1. Every pairwise count includes the person in all three lists. The union formula gives 10 + 9 + 8 − 4 − 3 − 2 + 1 = 19 distinct people.

Audit that answer by disjoint regions. Exactly two lists contain respectively 4 − 1 = 3 people in A and B only, 3 − 1 = 2 in A and C only, and 2 − 1 = 1 in B and C only. A alone contains 10 − 3 − 2 − 1 = 4; B alone contains 9 − 3 − 1 − 1 = 4; C alone contains 8 − 2 − 1 − 1 = 4. Adding all seven regions gives 4 + 4 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 19.

This calculation also checks consistency: no region has negative size. In an actual application, an impossible negative region would signal incompatible supplied counts, rather than a negative number of people. Intersection data must describe the same underlying individuals throughout.

Try two counts

Exercise 1. Two clubs have 21 and 16 members, with 9 belonging to both. How many people belong to at least one, and how many belong only to the first?

Hint for exercise 1

Use the two-set formula for the union. For first-only members, remove the overlap from the first club.

Full solution to exercise 1

The union contains 21+16−9=28 distinct people. The first-only region has 21−9=12 people. A region check gives 12 first-only, 7 second-only, and 9 both, totaling 28.

Exercise 2. Three tags A,B,C are attached to a single record, and every pairwise intersection and the triple intersection therefore each has size one. Apply the three-set formula.

Hint for exercise 2

Track this one record through every term, including the final triple term.

Full solution to exercise 2

Each single-set count is one, so singles total 3. Each pairwise intersection is one, so the three subtractions total −3. The triple intersection adds 1. Thus 3−3+1=1, matching the union's single record. Omitting the final addition would incorrectly give zero.

What to carry forward

Inclusion–exclusion counts a union by correcting repeated membership. The two-set proof is a disjoint-region argument; the three-set formula follows by checking each possible membership pattern. When applying it, define distinct objects first, obtain intersection sizes from actual overlaps, and audit a person in every set. That extreme membership pattern catches missing final terms.
