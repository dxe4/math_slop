Can openness detect a jump?

A graph can suggest that a function has no jump, but a graph cannot check every point. A precise alternative is to pull each open set in the output space back to the inputs that land in it. Continuous maps always produce an open input set this way. We will prove the equivalence in metric spaces, where distances make both sides explicit.

Background capsule. A metric space X has a distance dX(x,y), with positive-radius balls BX(x,r)={z:dX(z,x)<r}. A subset is open if each member lies in some contained ball. For f:X→Y and V⊆Y, the preimage f−1(V)={x∈X:f(x)∈V}; no inverse function is needed. Continuity at x says: for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that dX(x,z)<δ implies dY(f(x),f(z))<ε.

Pull back one interval

Worked example. Let f:ℝ→ℝ be f(x)=x² and choose V=(1,4). Its preimage is (−2,−1)∪(1,2), since 1<x²<4 means 1<|x|<2. That union is open. For an input near 1.5, a small change stays in the right component; near −1.5 it stays in the left. The preimage is allowed to have disconnected pieces. A function does not need to map an open set to an open set to be continuous; the direction here is specifically preimages of open sets.

Compare g(x)=0 for x≤0 and g(x)=1 for x>0. The open output interval (−1/2,1/2) pulls back to (−∞,0], which is not open in ℝ. The point 0 witnesses failure: arbitrarily close positive inputs have output 1. This single open target set proves that g is discontinuous.

Both directions of the metric-space theorem

Exact statement. For a map f between metric spaces X and Y, continuity at every point is equivalent to open preimages:

f continuous⇔∀V⊆Y,V open⇒f−1(V) open in X

Forward proof. Suppose f is continuous and V is open in Y. Take x∈f−1(V), so f(x)∈V. Openness gives an ε-ball around f(x) contained in V. Continuity at x gives δ>0 sending every z in BX(x,δ) into that ε-ball. Thus BX(x,δ) lies in the preimage. As x was arbitrary, the preimage is open. The empty preimage is open vacuously.

Reverse proof. Suppose every open preimage is open. Fix x∈X and ε>0. The Y-ball V=BY(f(x),ε) is open in any metric space: a point y inside has remaining radius ε−dY(f(x),y)>0, and the triangle inequality keeps its smaller ball inside V. By assumption, f−1(V) is open and contains x, so some δ-ball about x lies in it. For all z with dX(x,z)<δ, f(z) belongs to V, exactly the ε–δ condition. Since x and ε were arbitrary, f is continuous everywhere.

This proof depends on the metric-space definitions and the triangle inequality, but does not depend on a picture or a formula for f. In a general topological space, open preimages are used as the definition of continuity; metric ε–δ language may not exist. Be careful with subspaces: a preimage must be open in its domain. For f:[0,1]→ℝ given by f(x)=x, the preimage of (−1,1/2) is [0,1/2), open relative to [0,1], although not open in the whole real line.

Use preimages deliberately

1. For h:ℝ→ℝ, h(x)=|x|, calculate h−1((1,3)) and explain why this one calculation alone does not prove continuity everywhere.

Hint for question 1

Translate 1<|x|<3 into two signed intervals. Watch the word “every” in the theorem.

Full solution for question 1

The preimage is (−3,−1)∪(1,3), an open union. The theorem asks for every open V, so one example is insufficient. Continuity can instead be proved by ||x|−|z||≤|x−z|: given ε>0, δ=ε works at every x. The theorem then guarantees all open preimages are open.

2. Let k(x)=0 when x<0 and k(x)=1 when x≥0. Find an open V whose preimage disproves continuity, and identify the bad input.

Hint for question 2

Choose a small interval around the output 1 that excludes 0.

Full solution for question 2

Take V=(1/2,3/2). Its preimage is [0,∞), because precisely inputs x≥0 have output 1. This set is not open in ℝ: every neighborhood of 0 contains a negative point. Thus k fails continuity at 0. It is continuous at each nonzero input, since a sufficiently small neighborhood stays on one side of zero.

Read the arrow backwards

Open-preimage continuity is a global condition built from local neighborhoods. It says something about how output tests pull back to the domain, including its relative topology. It does not say that images of open sets are open. For a stronger notion that preserves shape in both directions, continue to homeomorphisms.
