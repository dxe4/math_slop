Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Can a rule make a repeatable dot picture?

Consider a square canvas on which a program places ten dots. It uses a named rule and a saved seed. Two runs with the same seed and implementation should give the same picture; different seeds usually differ. That repeatability is useful when discussing an artwork, but the picture alone does not prove that its dots are uniformly random or visually pleasing. Those are separate questions.

Background capsule. A coordinate pair (x,y) locates a point: x is horizontal and y vertical. The unit square has 0≤x≤1 and 0≤y≤1. A uniform point on that square assigns probability equal to area; for example, the left half has probability 1/2. “Independent and identically distributed” (iid) means each dot follows the same distribution and one dot does not change another's stipulated chances. A computer seed initializes a deterministic pseudorandom algorithm; repeatability does not make its output physically random.

Our model is a design recipe: draw n coordinate pairs independently from the uniform distribution on the square, then render them as dots. The distribution is a declared ideal rule; an actual implementation needs a specified generator and seed if another person is to reproduce exact coordinates.

Count a feature before judging the picture

Worked example. Choose n=10. Let K be the number of dots in the left half, x<0.5. Each dot independently lands there with probability 0.5, so K has a binomial distribution under the ideal model. Its expected value is 10×0.5=5, but an actual picture can have 3 or 8. The chance that all ten land in the left half is (1/2)¹⁰=1/1024. Rare is not impossible; a clustering event is not automatically a broken generator.

Three possible dot features under the stipulated rule
FeatureExact probabilityWhy
First dot left1/2Half the area
First two left1/4Independent product
All ten left1/1024Ten independent factors

Suppose the artist chooses a seed after viewing fifty previews and selects the one with the most balanced left and right halves. The exhibited image no longer represents an unselected draw from the original distribution. The seed still reproduces it, but the selection rule changes what can be inferred from the final image. A model note should say how many candidates were viewed and what criterion selected the display.

State the probability model exactly

For each i from 1 to n, let (Xᵢ,Yᵢ) be a pair sampled uniformly on [0,1]², independently across i. For any rectangle R inside the square, the model assigns P((Xᵢ,Yᵢ)∈R)=area(R). In particular, with L the left half,

P(K=k)=(10!k!(10−k)!)(12)10

Here 10! means 10×9×…×1 and k ranges from 0 to 10. The coefficient counts which k of the ten labelled draws land left; independence gives the factor (1/2)¹⁰ for each such pattern. This is a finite probability derivation under the iid hypothesis. It does not certify that a particular software generator satisfies ideal independence, or that a human eye finds the arrangement balanced.

A fixed seed yields a fixed sequence for a fixed algorithm and version. For transparent reproduction, record both. A seed alone is insufficient if the generator changes. If the design enforces a minimum distance between dots, draws become dependent; the simple binomial law for arbitrary regions may fail. Such a rule can be artistically useful, but it is a different model and deserves its own probability calculation.

Separate rule, draw and judgement

1. Calculate. Four iid uniform dots are placed in the square. What is the chance that none falls in the top quarter, y>0.75? What is the expected number there?

Hint for question 1

For one dot, the region has area 1/4, so outside has chance 3/4.

Full solution to question 1

Each dot avoids the top quarter with probability 3/4. Independence makes the chance that all four avoid it (3/4)⁴=81/256≈0.3164. Each dot contributes expected count 1/4, so the expected number is 4×1/4=1. Expectation one does not mean every picture has exactly one top-quarter dot.

2. Explain a failure. A designer redraws any dot that lies within 0.1 units of an earlier dot. Are the final dots iid uniform? Is saving a seed still useful?

Hint for question 2

Ask whether the allowed position for a later dot depends on earlier positions.

Full solution to question 2

The later dot's accepted positions exclude discs around earlier dots, so its final distribution depends on their coordinates. Independence fails and uniformity over the full square fails near excluded regions. A saved seed and specified redraw algorithm can still reproduce the image, but the iid uniform formula no longer describes the accepted dots.

What repeatability establishes

A seeded generative rule makes a result reproducible under a specified algorithm; probability statements depend on the sampling rule, including any selection or rejection steps. Visual quality is an aesthetic judgement, not a theorem following from uniformity. This small exercise illustrates the broader modelling cycle: record the question, assumptions, mechanism, output and an honest check.
