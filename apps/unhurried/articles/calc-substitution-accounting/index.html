When you change the input, what happens to the width?

Substitution is often presented as replacing symbols in an integral. Its real content is accounting for how a new input changes with the old one. If u=g(t), then a small change in t produces approximately g′(t) times that change in u. The endpoints move too, and their order carries orientation.

Background capsule. The chain rule differentiates F(g(t)) as F′(g(t))g′(t). A definite integral from a to b is signed: reversing endpoints changes its sign. For continuous f, the fundamental theorem supplies an antiderivative F(u)=∫ from a chosen base point to u of f(v)dv with F′=f. A continuously differentiable g has a continuous derivative on the interval, enough for the calculations here. No one-to-one condition is required for the formula we will prove.

A doubling and a reversal

Worked example. Compute ∫ from 0 to 1 of 2t(1+t²)³dt. Choose u=1+t². Then du/dt=2t, exactly the factor outside the cube. At t=0, u=1; at t=1, u=2. The answer is ∫ from 1 to 2 of u³du=[u⁴/4] from 1 to 2=(16−1)/4=15/4. Keeping t endpoints 0 and 1 after changing the integrand to u³ would silently calculate a different integral.

Now take g(t)=1−t on [0,1], and f(u)=u². The transformed integrand is (1−t)²(−1). Its integral is −1/3. The right side has endpoints g(0)=1 and g(1)=0, so ∫ from 1 to 0 of u²du=−1/3. The negative derivative and reversed endpoints agree. If the task instead asks for ∫₀¹(1−t)²dt, move the minus sign explicitly: its value is +1/3.

The orientation check is useful before any algebra: a positive integrand over increasing t cannot acquire a negative answer merely because a substitution reverses its coordinates.

Check the ruler in a small example. Set u=2t on 0≤t≤1 and integrate f(u)=u. The original integral is ∫₀¹(2t)·2 dt=2; the transformed integral is ∫₀²u du=2. Omitting the derivative factor would give 1, exactly half the answer. The interval in u is twice as long as the interval in t, and g′=2 accounts for that stretch. The endpoint calculation and the width calculation are separate parts of the same substitution.

The definite substitution formula

Statement. Let f be continuous on an interval containing the values g(t) for a≤t≤b, and let g be continuously differentiable on [a,b]. Then the following equality holds, even if g reverses direction or revisits a value:

∫abf(g(t))g′(t)dt=∫g(a)g(b)f(u)du

Derivation. Set F(u)=∫ from a fixed u₀ to u of f(v)dv. Continuity of f makes F′(u)=f(u). By the chain rule, d[F(g(t))]/dt=f(g(t))g′(t). The integral of this derivative from a to b is F(g(b))−F(g(a)); by the second fundamental theorem, this difference is ∫ from g(a) to g(b) of f(u)du. This proof uses the fundamental theorem and chain rule under the stated regularity. It does not rely on counting stretched rectangles one by one.

If g goes forward then backward, some contributions cancel. For example, g(t)=t² on [−1,1] has g(−1)=g(1)=1, so ∫ from −1 to 1 of 2t f(t²)dt=0 for continuous f. The formula captures the signed cancellation. It is not the same as an unsigned area change-of-variables formula, where multiple coverings and absolute values require different bookkeeping.

Boundary. Omitting g′ generally fails: ∫₀¹(1+t²)³dt cannot be replaced by ∫₁²u³du, because du is not dt. Likewise, a discontinuous f can fall outside this proof even if a more general substitution result remains true. State the version you use.

Keep the limits attached

1. Calculate. Evaluate ∫ from 0 to 2 of 3(3t+1)²dt by substitution.

Hint for 1

Set u=3t+1; compute both transformed endpoints.

Worked solution for 1

du=3dt, while t=0 gives u=1 and t=2 gives u=7. The integral becomes ∫₁⁷u²du=[u³/3]₁⁷=(343−1)/3=114. The result is positive, as the original integrand is nonnegative.

2. Explain. Let g(t)=t² on [−1,1] and f(u)=1. A learner writes ∫ from −1 to 1 of 2t dt=∫₀¹1du=1. Locate the error and calculate the correct value.

Hint for 2

Both t endpoints map to the same u endpoint.

Worked solution for 2

g(−1)=1 and g(1)=1, so the transformed integral runs from 1 to 1 and equals zero. Directly, ∫₋₁¹2t dt=[t²]₋₁¹=1−1=0. The learner kept only the forward trip from t=0 to 1 and lost the backward trip from t=−1 to 0.

Change the width and the endpoints together

The derivative g′ accounts for signed local stretching, and g(a),g(b) account for the new endpoint labels. The proof is an application of chain rule plus fundamental theorem; an invertible substitution is a convenient special case, not a necessary assumption. When solving a fresh problem, write the endpoint map before evaluating the antiderivative.
