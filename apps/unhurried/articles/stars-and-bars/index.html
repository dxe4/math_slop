How many ways to split identical pastries among friends?

Three friends split four identical biscuits. One may receive none. How many allocations exist? A list such as (2,0,2) gives the three shares, but listing every nonnegative triple is tedious. Encode the biscuits as four stars and separate the three friends' portions with two bars. For example, **||** means shares (2,0,2): two stars before the first bar, none between bars, and two after the second. Each allocation becomes a short string.

Prerequisite capsule. A weak composition of n into k parts is an ordered list (x₁,…,xₖ) of nonnegative integers with sum n. Here the parts are named recipients, so (2,0,1) differs from (1,0,2). The n items being distributed are identical, meaning we care only how many each person gets. C(a,b) counts b-element subsets of an a-element set; for this lesson n≥0 and k≥1.

Count a concrete case

Worked example. Four stars and two identical bars occupy six positions. Choosing the two bar positions determines the entire pattern, so there are C(6,2)=15 allocations. Adjacent bars encode a zero share; a bar at the beginning or end also permits zero. The all-to-first allocation (4,0,0) is ****||, while the all-to-second allocation (0,4,0) is |****|. For a smaller audit, two identical biscuits and three recipients give C(4,2)=6 solutions: (2,0,0),(0,2,0),(0,0,2),(1,1,0),(1,0,1),(0,1,1). Six bar-and-star strings encode those six triples exactly once. The order of recipients is fixed by their names; only the biscuits are indistinguishable.

The exact rule and its proof

#{(x1,…,xk):∑ixi=n,xi∈ℤ≥0}=(n+k−1k−1)

Stars-and-bars theorem. For nonnegative n and positive k, the displayed count is exact. Map each allocation to a string of n stars and k−1 bars: write x₁ stars, a bar, x₂ stars, and continue, ending with xₖ stars. The reverse map reads the number of stars in each segment. These maps undo each other, so this is a bijection, not just a suggestive picture. There are n+k−1 total positions and choosing which k−1 positions hold bars uniquely determines a string. For n=0, all positions hold bars and there is exactly one all-zero allocation; the formula gives C(k−1,k−1)=1. For k=1, no bars are needed and the sole allocation gives all n items to the one recipient. The proof assumes unbounded shares; upper limits require extra work.

Where the rule fails. If the four biscuits are individually decorated and their identities matter, 15 is wrong. Each biscuit then has three possible recipients, yielding 3⁴=81 assignments. Stars and bars forgets which biscuit is which. Likewise, if every friend must receive at least one biscuit, strings with adjacent or boundary bars must be excluded. Subtract one biscuit per friend first: for four biscuits and three friends, the remaining one biscuit has three possible destinations, so there are only three positive-share allocations.

Worked example: reserving an unequal minimum

Split eight identical biscuits among named friends A,B,C, requiring A to receive at least two and B at least one, with no minimum for C. Set A's share to 2 + u, B's to 1 + v, and C's to w. The conditions become u,v,w ≥ 0 and u + v + w = 5. Thus five stars and two bars give C(7,2) = 21 allocations.

The substitution is reversible: add the reserved biscuits back to recover one admissible original allocation. For example, residual shares (0,2,3) produce (2,3,3). No two residual triples produce the same original triple, and every original triple can have the prescribed reserves removed. This verifies that the new count solves the original constrained question, rather than merely a similar-looking equation. An upper limit would need additional restrictions; subtracting minima handles lower bounds only.

Try two counts

Exercise 1. Find the number of nonnegative solutions to x+y+z=5. Show the string length and number of bars.

Hint for exercise 1

There are five stars and two bars. Count the choices of bar positions among all positions.

Full solution to exercise 1

The encoding uses five stars and two bars, hence seven positions. Selecting two bar positions gives C(7,2)=21 solutions. For instance, **|***| represents (2,3,0). The bijection ensures no solution is omitted or duplicated.

Exercise 2. Count positive integer solutions to a+b+c=7, explaining why you may apply a nonnegative formula after a change of variables.

Hint for exercise 2

Give one unit to each variable, then count the distribution of the remaining units.

Full solution to exercise 2

Set a=1+a′, b=1+b′, c=1+c′ with a′,b′,c′≥0. The new sum is a′+b′+c′=4. Stars and bars counts C(4+3−1,3−1)=C(6,2)=15 solutions. The substitution is reversible, so all positive solutions are counted once.

What to carry forward

Stars and bars is a bijection between nonnegative named shares of identical items and strings of stars separated by bars. Its boundary cases explain why zero shares are allowed. Before using C(n+k−1,k−1), check that recipients are distinct, items are indistinguishable, and no share has a hidden upper bound. Those assumptions determine whether the encoding preserves the outcomes you actually want to count.
