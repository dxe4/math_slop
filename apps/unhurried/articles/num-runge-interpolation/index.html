Why extra sample points can hurt

Polynomial interpolation passes exactly through chosen data. One might expect that adding points always improves the curve between them. Runge’s function, f(x)=1/(1+25x²) on [−1,1], is a famous warning: high-degree interpolation at equally spaced nodes can swing badly near the ends. Node placement can matter more than the sheer number of nodes. This lesson gives a concrete small calculation and the error formula that explains where the danger enters.

Background capsule. Through n+1 distinct nodes x₀,…,x_n there is exactly one polynomial p_n of degree at most n matching the sampled values. Its maximum error is max over the interval of |f−p_n|. Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes are cos(jπ/n), j=0,…,n; they cluster near ±1. The exact interpolating polynomial is a mathematical object. A computed one also incurs finite-precision errors.

Compare degrees and node choices

Worked example. For n=2, both equally spaced nodes and Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes happen to be −1,0,1, so they give the same interpolant. We can calculate it exactly: f(0)=1 and f(±1)=1/26. Symmetry makes p₂(x)=1−(25/26)x². At x=1/2, the true value is 1/(1+25/4)=4/29≈0.13793, while p₂(1/2)=1−25/104=79/104≈0.75962. The discrepancy is about 0.62168 despite exact agreement at all three nodes.

For n=4, the equally spaced nodes are −1, −1/2, 0, 1/2, 1; Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes are −1, −√2/2, 0, √2/2, 1. Symmetry makes both quartics of the form 1+ax²+bx⁴. Matching f(1)=1/26 and f(1/2)=4/29 gives the uniform-node polynomial 1−(3225/754)x²+(1250/377)x⁴. Matching f(1)=1/26 and f(√2/2)=2/27 instead gives the clustered-node polynomial 1−(1925/702)x²+(625/351)x⁴. This supplies a reproducible comparison without relying on a plot.

At this one interior point, adding equally spaced nodes makes the error almost twice as large, while the clustered nodes reduce it. That is a finite counterexample to “more nodes always improve every point,” not a proof of any whole-interval or asymptotic comparison. Clustering controls a key node-product factor near the endpoints, but it does not assert that every finite Chebyshev interpolant beats every equally spaced one at every x.

The node product and what it does not prove

f(x)−pn(x)=f(n+1)(ξ)(n+1)!∏j=0n(x−xj)

For f with n+1 continuous derivatives, this pointwise remainder holds for x in the interval and some ξ depending on x and the nodes. One proof subtracts from f−p_n a multiple of the node product chosen to make the difference zero also at x; repeated Rolle’s theorem then produces the derivative value. At a node, both sides vanish without needing the quotient construction. The node product is small near a node but can be large between sparse edge nodes. Chebyshev-type clustering controls its maximum far better than equally spaced placement for high degree.

The formula alone does not prove convergence as n grows: the derivative f^(n+1), its factorial ratio, and the product all change with n. For Runge’s function, complex singularities at ±i/5 limit polynomial approximation behavior, and equally spaced interpolation is known to diverge near interval endpoints as degree grows. A complete asymptotic proof needs additional estimates beyond this lesson. Trefethen’s cited author-hosted approximation text treats the Chebyshev-centered theory. Our exact degree-two calculation demonstrates that matching sample values does not control between-node error; it does not itself prove divergence.

Interpolating noisy data raises another issue. The interpolant fits noise exactly at the nodes and may amplify it elsewhere through its interpolation weights. More nodes are not automatically helpful when the data themselves are uncertain. Piecewise low-degree interpolation is another practical response, with its own smoothness and error analysis.

A different error, with an explicit budget

Keep the original three nodes −1,0,1 and Runge data (1/26,1,1/26). Suppose each recorded value differs from its exact value by at most ε=1/100. This is an absolute error budget, not a percentage and not a probability model. The nodes themselves are exact. Let p be the exact polynomial through the original data and p̃ the exact polynomial through the recorded data. Can |p̃−p| exceed 1/100 between nodes, even though both polynomials interpolate their own data exactly?

Extra background. Three distinct nodes determine one polynomial of degree at most two: the difference of two candidates would have three distinct zeros. The cardinal polynomial ℓⱼ equals one at node j and zero at the other two. Thus p(x)=Σⱼyⱼℓⱼ(x). If data change by eⱼ, linearity gives δp(x)=p̃(x)−p(x)=Σⱼeⱼℓⱼ(x). No differentiability assumption on an unknown underlying function is needed for this data-to-interpolant identity.

Build the weights and prove the sharp bound

For three distinct nodes t₀,t₁,t₂, construct ℓⱼ(x)=∏k≠j(x−tₖ)/(tⱼ−tₖ). Substituting any node verifies its zero-or-one values. The same interpolation uniqueness gives Σⱼℓⱼ(x)=1, because both sides interpolate constant data. For nodes −1,0,1, the weights are ℓ₀=x(x−1)/2, ℓ₁=1−x² and ℓ₂=x(x+1)/2.

Define Λ(x)=Σⱼ|ℓⱼ(x)|, the Lebesgue function for these nodes. The triangle inequality gives an exact absolute data-error bound:

|δp(x)|≤ε∑|ℓj(x)|=εΛ(x)

Why sharp? At a specified target x, choose eⱼ=ε when ℓⱼ(x)>0 and eⱼ=−ε when ℓⱼ(x)<0. Zero weights can receive any admissible error. All nonzero products then contribute positively, attaining δp=εΛ. Negating the errors attains the negative bound. These optimizing signs can depend on the target; they need not describe one record that attains the envelope everywhere. “Attained” in the instrument means |δp|=εΛ, including a negative signed δp.

On [0,1], ℓ₀≤0 and ℓ₁,ℓ₂≥0, so Λ(x)=1+x−x²=5/4−(x−1/2)². Reflection gives Λ(x)=1+|x|−x² on [−1,1]. Therefore its exact continuous-interval maximum is 5/4, attained at ±1/2. The sharp full-interval bound is consequently ε·5/4=1/80. This proves a statement about every real x in the interval, not only the seventeen displayed probes. Continuity on the closed interval supplies a maximizing point; choosing errors for that point proves sharpness of the full-interval bound too.

The fixed right-target record gives δp=ε(1+x−x²). A different record (+ε,+ε,−ε) attains the positive envelope on the negative half. By contrast, a common offset (+ε,+ε,+ε) gives δp=εΣℓⱼ=ε everywhere. This is not a statement about all unequal same-sign errors. At every node Λ=1; checking only nodes would miss amplification between them.

Keep three error questions separate

The certificate bounds p̃−p, not the total error from an underlying function. Here at x=1/2, the original p value is 79/104. The right-target errors add 1/80, giving p̃=803/1040. The true Runge value remains 4/29. Hence f−p=−1875/3016 while f−p̃=−19127/30160: the new data error reinforces the pre-existing approximation error at this point. Other signs could partially cancel it.

For a continuous underlying f on the closed interval, write f−p̃=(f−p)−(p̃−p). Thus the maximum total error is at most the maximum noiseless approximation error plus ε times the maximum Λ. A computed evaluation would require a third, separately justified, arithmetic-error term. Smaller Λ alone does not prove smaller total error for every function or every record, and exact arithmetic cannot remove uncertain input data. This is an absolute sensitivity bound; it never divides by a possibly zero output as a relative condition number would.

The quadrature lesson already bounds data errors using nonnegative weights, and finite differences amplify evaluation errors by 2εf/|h|. Here the new issue is a spatially varying sum of absolute signed interpolation weights, with a constructive sharp bound over a continuous interval. Trefethen's authored Lebesgue-function note in the sources defines this data-to-interpolant viewpoint. Our three-node calculations are derived here.

Scope. Three distinct fixed nodes, exact arithmetic and bounded value errors are essential to the stated model. No random noise, confidence band, noisy-node analysis, high-degree asymptotic theorem or numerical algorithm certification follows. Check what the nodes guarantee

Exercise 1. For f(x)=1/(1+25x²) and nodes −1,0,1, derive p₂ and compute the signed error f(1/2)−p₂(1/2) exactly.

Hint for exercise 1

Use symmetry to write p₂(x)=1+cx², then fit x=1.

Solution to exercise 1

From 1+c=1/26, c=−25/26. Thus p₂=1−25x²/26. At 1/2, f=4/29 and p₂=79/104. The signed error is 4/29−79/104=(416−2291)/3016=−1875/3016≈−0.62168. Interpolation is exact only at the three nodes.

Exercise 2. A plot at degrees 8 and 12 shows smaller maximum error for Chebyshev nodes. Does it prove convergence for every degree? State what additional type of argument is required, and one situation where neither plot is enough.

Hint for exercise 2

Separate finite observations from a bound uniform in all sufficiently large n.

Solution to exercise 2

No. Two finite cases cannot establish a limit over all n. One needs an asymptotic error bound, for example by controlling approximation error and interpolation amplification or the remainder uniformly as n grows. If sample values contain unknown noise, even an exact-arithmetic plot of noiseless f does not bound the error for the measured data; noise amplification needs its own estimate.

Hint for exercise 3

Build each ℓⱼ to be one at its own node and zero at the other two. Its sign can change only at those zeros. Since Σℓⱼ=1, subtract twice the negative weight to obtain Λ on each interval. Fully worked answer to exercise 3

(a) The weights are ℓ₀=(x+1/2)(x−1), ℓ₁=4(1−x²)/3 and ℓ₂=(x+1)(x+1/2)/3. At x=1/4 they are (−9/16,5/4,5/16), summing to one with absolute sum 17/8. The sharp bound is (17/8)(1/50)=17/400. Errors (−1/50,+1/50,+1/50) attain its positive sign. The exact f data are (0,−1/4,2), so recorded data are (−1/50,−23/100,101/50). The noiseless interpolant is f itself, with f(1/4)=5/16; the perturbed value is 5/16+17/400=71/200. Thus p̃−f=17/400 and f−p̃=−17/400.

(b) On [−1,−1/2], ℓ₂≤0 while ℓ₀,ℓ₁≥0, so Λ=1−2ℓ₂=2/3−x−(2/3)x²=25/24−(2/3)(x+3/4)². Its maximum there is 25/24 at −3/4. On [−1/2,1], ℓ₀≤0 while ℓ₁,ℓ₂≥0, giving Λ=1−2ℓ₀=2+x−2x²=17/8−2(x−1/4)². Its maximum there is 17/8 at 1/4. Both pieces equal one at the common boundary. Because 17/8>25/24, the global maximum is 17/8 and its unique maximizing point is 1/4. All three nodes have Λ=1, so checking only nodes misses that maximum.

(c) At x=−3/4 the weights are (7/16,7/12,−1/48), and Λ=25/24. The fixed record (−ε,+ε,+ε) gives δp=ε(−7/16+7/12−1/48)=ε/8=1/400. Its magnitude is below the local bound (25/24)ε=1/48. A different fixed record (+ε,+ε,−ε) attains the positive bound there. A pointwise sharp envelope can require different errors at different targets.

(d) Since f is degree two, its noiseless interpolant equals f everywhere. Therefore maximum total error is at most (17/8)(1/50)=17/400, and the record in (a) attains it at 1/4. Runge's noiseless interpolation error is nonzero, so its total error requires that separate term. The larger constant describes a larger worst case, not every record: zero errors give zero data perturbation, and a common offset gives exactly ε under either node set. It does not rank total errors for every function.

Place points for the space between them

Interpolation matches nodes exactly, while error between nodes depends on smoothness and node geometry. Chebyshev clustering mitigates endpoint trouble for high-degree polynomial interpolation, but finite tables are evidence of behavior only for the tested degrees. The remainder formula has explicit differentiability assumptions; an asymptotic claim needs more work. Continue with quadrature error for a different use of interpolation that yields a full finite bound.
