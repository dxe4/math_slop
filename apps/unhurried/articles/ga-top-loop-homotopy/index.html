When can a loop be pulled tight?

A loop drawn in a disk can often shrink to its starting point. Around a missing point, the same motion may be impossible without crossing the hole. The formal object is a based homotopy: a continuous family of loops whose common basepoint stays fixed. We will give a complete contraction in a convex disk and a carefully bounded obstruction in the punctured plane.

Background capsule. A path in X is a continuous map γ:[0,1]→X. It is a loop based at x₀ if γ(0)=γ(1)=x₀. A homotopy H:[0,1]²→X changes one map continuously into another; the first coordinate s tracks the deformation and the second t tracks a position on the loop. Continuity here means continuity in both variables together. A set C in the plane is convex when every straight segment between its points stays in C.

Watch a contraction and an obstruction

Worked example. Let C be the closed unit disk centered at 0 and choose the basepoint x₀=(1,0) on its boundary. Any loop γ in C based at x₀ contracts by straight lines toward x₀: H(s,t)=(1−s)γ(t)+s x₀. At s=0 the loop is γ; at s=1 it is constantly x₀. For every s, H(s,0)=H(s,1)=x₀. Convexity keeps all intermediate points inside C. Thus the formula is a full constructive proof for this disk, including loops that cross themselves.

Now use X=ℝ²∖{(0,0)} and the loop γ(t)=(cos 2πt,sin 2πt), based at (1,0). Its straight-line contraction toward (1,0) fails: at t=1/2 the starting point is (−1,0), and the segment to (1,0) passes through the deleted origin. This failure of one proposed formula alone does not rule out every conceivable deformation. A winding-number invariant supplies the genuine obstruction, conditional on the angle-lifting fact developed in the next lesson.

Try the formula, then audit the whole family

First predict what happens halfway through the straight-line contraction. Select the punctured plane and use “See the exact crossing.” Moving beyond s=1/2 produces loops that avoid 0 again, but the whole family has already crossed the deleted point. Compare the winding before and after that crossing.

Worked setting: one counterclockwise turn, straight-line formula, closed unit disk, s = 1/4.

θ(t) = 2πt; γ(t) = (cos θ(t), sin θ(t)). H(s,t) = (1−s)γ(t) + s(1,0).

Dashed circle: unit circle. Solid curve: current path. Square: position t. Double ring: basepoint.

On paper, set s = 1/2 and t = 1/2: H = (0,0). That is permitted in the disk and forbidden in the punctured plane.

Now select “Contract a retraced loop.” This loop goes once counterclockwise and then follows the same circle backwards; its winding is 1−1=0. Set θ(t)=4π min(t,1−t). The angle formula H(s,t)=(cos((1−s)θ(t)), sin((1−s)θ(t))) stays on the unit circle. Because θ(0)=θ(1)=0, it fixes both endpoints at (1,0), and at s=1 it is constant. This is an explicit valid contraction in the punctured plane even though the straight-line formula for this very same loop hits 0 at s=1/2, t=1/4 and 3/4. A failed formula is not a proof of noncontractibility.

Finally select “Check the basepoint trap.” For the once-around loop θ(t)=2πt, reducing the angle keeps every point away from 0, but H(s,1)=(cos(2π(1−s)), sin(2π(1−s))) is not (1,0) when 0<s<1. These slices are open paths, so assigning them a fractional winding number would be a mistake. The formula finishes at a constant yet fails to be a homotopy of loops.

The four boundary conditions

Exact definition. Based loops γ₀ and γ₁ at x₀ are based-homotopic if there is continuous H:[0,1]²→X satisfying

H(0,t)=γ0(t)H(1,t)=γ1(t)H(s,0)=x0H(s,1)=x0

The first two conditions describe start and finish; the last two keep the basepoint fixed throughout. Reversing s reverses a homotopy. Joining two homotopies in time gives another, so based homotopy is an equivalence relation. The joined formula is piecewise continuous because its pieces agree at their common edge; this uses the standard gluing lemma for continuous maps. A free homotopy still requires H(s,0)=H(s,1), so every intermediate path remains a loop, but allows this common endpoint to move. It answers a different question; dropping closure altogether would give a family of paths instead.

Obstruction, with boundary named. A loop in the punctured plane has an integer winding number around the origin. For the unit-circle loop above it is 1; for a constant loop it is 0. Winding number remains unchanged under a homotopy that never touches the origin. Therefore those loops cannot be homotopic in X, based or free. The invariance step depends on a continuous lift of angle over the homotopy square, or an equivalent winding theorem. We state that lifting result here rather than pretending the failure of straight-line contraction proves impossibility. We do not compute the full fundamental group of the punctured plane in this article.

The basepoint may be anywhere in a convex C, not necessarily at its center. In the contraction formula, t=0 and t=1 both evaluate to (1−s)x₀+s x₀=x₀. This is the often-missed boundary check. If X is not convex, the formula may leave X even when a different homotopy exists; the punctured-plane example needs the winding invariant to settle nonexistence.

Check formulas and claims

1. In the convex square C=[−2,2]², based at x₀=(2,0), give a based homotopy from γ(t)=(2cos 2πt,2sin 2πt) to the constant loop. Verify all four boundary conditions.

Hint for question 1

Interpolate between γ(t) and x₀, then use convexity of the square.

Full solution for question 1

Set H(s,t)=(1−s)(2cos 2πt,2sin 2πt)+s(2,0). At s=0 this is γ and at s=1 it is (2,0). At t=0 or 1, γ(t)=(2,0), so H(s,0)=H(s,1)=(2,0). Both endpoints of every interpolating segment belong to C and C is convex, so H stays in C. Algebraic operations and trig functions make H continuous.

2. Someone argues, “The straight-line formula hits the origin, so the unit-circle loop cannot shrink in the punctured plane.” Identify the logical gap and the missing invariant.

Hint for question 2

One failed construction does not rule out every construction. Compare a number assigned to the loop and to a constant loop.

Full solution for question 2

The calculation rules out that particular straight-line H, not all continuous H. The unit-circle loop has winding number 1 and a constant loop has winding number 0. If winding number is homotopy-invariant for loops avoiding the origin, no homotopy can connect them. That invariance rests on the angle-lifting theorem, which must be supplied or explicitly assumed; a picture of a loop circling a hole is not a proof by itself.

3. Two loops have exactly the same trace, the unit circle. The first travels counterclockwise once; the second travels counterclockwise once and then retraces clockwise. A student concludes that both are noncontractible in the punctured plane because their pictures surround the same hole. Repair the argument and give an explicit contraction when one exists.

Hint for question 3

A loop is a parametrized walk. Follow its angle without resetting it to a principal value; compare the final angle to the initial one.

Full solution for question 3

The first loop has winding 1, so homotopy invariance prevents a contraction in the punctured plane. For the second choose θ(t)=4π min(t,1−t); its angle returns to 0, so its winding is 0. The formula H(s,t)=(cos((1−s)θ(t)),sin((1−s)θ(t))) contracts it within the unit circle. The triangular θ is continuous at t=1/2, both endpoints have θ=0, and s=1 gives (1,0) throughout. Here the formula proves existence; zero winding alone would not be a general proof in an arbitrary space.

Distinguish construction from obstruction

Convexity gives an explicit contraction and all basepoint checks. A deleted origin calls for a homotopy invariant; the winding argument uses a named lifting result. The winding-number lesson computes that invariant on polygonal examples and spells out its proof boundary.
