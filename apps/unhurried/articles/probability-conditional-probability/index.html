Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

The question and needed ideas

A mystery card is drawn uniformly from a standard 52-card deck. Before you see it, the chance that it is a queen is 4/52. Someone then tells you it is a heart. The card is still hidden, but the information changes the relevant group of cards. How likely is a queen now?

Background capsule. P(A|B), read “probability of A given B,” is the probability of A after restricting attention to outcomes where B happened. The vertical bar is not division. A∩B means both conditions hold. The conditional fraction uses the probability of B as its denominator; it is meaningful only if B has positive probability.

Work through the model

Worked example. This first model conditions on the event that the card is a heart, keeping every heart with its original weight; a rule for choosing which clue to announce is introduced later. Let A mean queen and B mean heart. There are 13 hearts. Exactly one is a queen, so among the 13 cards still possible after the clue, one is favorable: P(A|B)=1/13. The original probability 4/52 also equals 1/13, but that coincidence is due to the deck's symmetry. A different clue can change the number dramatically.

Suppose the clue is “face card” instead. Twelve cards remain possible and four are queens. Then P(queen|face)=4/12=1/3. The cards outside the clue were removed from the denominator. The queen event did not physically change; the information narrowed the possible cases.

Using the original full-deck probabilities gives the same result: P(queen and face)=4/52, P(face)=12/52, and their ratio is (4/52)/(12/52)=4/12. This calculation is useful when outcomes are not naturally counted as equally likely.

See the new denominator

Now roll a blue die and a yellow die fairly and independently. We condition on the event “at least one die shows six.” This means keeping all pairs that meet that condition, rather than adding a rule that selects or reports particular rolls. Explain the dice count

The six pairs in blue’s row 6 and the six in yellow’s column 6 share (6,6), so only 6 + 6 − 1 = 11 pairs remain. Two of them, (1,6) and (6,1), total seven. The conditional probability is 2/11. Dividing by 36 would keep outcomes the clue has ruled out; dividing by 12 would count (6,6) twice.

Try the clue “blue is even” with the same target. Eighteen pairs remain and three total seven, giving 3/18 = 1/6. A clue can change the denominator without changing this event’s probability. The dice were independent before the clue; after the “at least one six” clue, learning that blue is not six forces yellow to be six. Do not assume independence still holds after conditioning.

The statement and its limits

Exact statement. For events A and B with P(B)>0, conditional probability is defined by the displayed ratio. In a finite equally likely model, it equals |A∩B|/|B| because dividing (|A∩B|/|Ω|) by (|B|/|Ω|) cancels the original total. The new denominator is precisely the number of B outcomes. This derivation explains the card count and extends the idea to models with unequal masses.

Rearranging gives the multiplication rule P(A∩B)=P(A|B)P(B). For the queen and face clue, (1/3)(12/52)=4/52. Reversing the condition changes the question: P(face|queen)=1 because every queen is a face card, while P(queen|face)=1/3. The two values generally differ.

The boundary is essential. If a six-sided die is known to show seven, B has probability zero in the stated model. P(A|B) is undefined, and no simple “new denominator” exists. One must revise the model, perhaps by allowing a misreported die, before assigning a chance.

P(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)P(B)>0

Conditioning can be checked by reconstructing a full partition. For the face-card clue, the twelve face cards divide into four queens and eight non-queens. The conditional probabilities 4/12 and 8/12 add to one. The other forty cards have no place in this conditional denominator, although they still matter for the probability that the clue itself occurs. This separation is especially useful when comparing two clues: “face” has chance 12/52, while “heart” has chance 13/52; each clue creates its own group of survivors and its own conditional distribution.

How did this report reach you?

The first dice grid conditions on an event: keep every pair containing a six. Now receive the truthful sentence “at least one die is six” from a specified reporter. Its meaning excludes pairs without a six, but its chance of being sent may vary among the remaining pairs. To find the posterior distribution, we need the reporting rule as well as the sentence.

The dice are still fair and independent. In both protocols, also generate an independent fair selector, blue or yellow. That makes 36×2=72 equally likely prior states, each a triple (blue face, yellow face, selector). The receiver knows the protocol and hears only the sentence; neither faces nor selector identity are revealed.

  • Scan both. Inspect both dice and send the report whenever at least one is six. Otherwise send no report. The selector is ignored.
  • Select one. Inspect only the die named by the independent selector. Send the report if that die is six; otherwise send no report, even if the unselected die is six. Do not reveal which die was selected.

Both reports are truthful: a reported six guarantees at least one actual six. Truthfulness alone does not give all compatible pairs the same posterior weight. Predict whether changing the protocol will preserve 2/11. Then inspect (1,6), (6,6), and a pair without a six. The same 72-state prior gives different report events

In the scan protocol, each of the eleven pairs containing a six has two selector states that report. There are 22 reported triples. The two pairs totaling seven, (1,6) and (6,1), supply four of them. Thus P(total seven | report)=4/22=2/11, agreeing with the original event-only grid.

In the select-one protocol there are six reports when blue is selected and six when yellow is selected: twelve triples altogether. Only (6,1,blue) and (1,6,yellow) also total seven, giving 2/12=1/6. A particular one-six pair, such as (1,6), reports only for one selector, while (6,6) reports for both. After receiving this report, the ten one-six pairs each have probability 1/12 and the double-six pair has probability 2/12=1/6. The same eleven pair values remain possible, but they are not equally likely.

The selector was independent of the dice before reporting. Conditioning on the report couples them: if the pair is (1,6) in the selected protocol, the selector must have been yellow. We average over that hidden selector to obtain the posterior over pairs. Counting all 72 prior triples equally is valid; counting the eleven posterior pair values equally is not valid for this protocol.

Weight a world by its chance of producing the report

Let W be a finite-valued hidden world, with prior probability p(w). Let R be the event that this exact report is received. Write r(w)=P(R | W=w), its reporting likelihood, for worlds with positive prior probability. The multiplication rule gives P(W=w and R)=p(w)r(w). Summing these joint probabilities gives Z=P(R)=Σ_u p(u)r(u). If Z>0,

P(W=w|R)=p(w)r(w)∑up(u)r(u)

For our dice worlds w=(blue,yellow), every prior p(w) is 1/36. Scan likelihood is 1 on any pair with a six and 0 elsewhere. Select-one likelihood is 1/2 on exactly-one-six pairs, 1 on (6,6), and 0 elsewhere. The denominator normalizes these weights. A likelihood such as 1/2 is a chance of sending the report from a fixed world; it is not that world's posterior probability.

Exactly when can the report be replaced by its truthful content?

Let U be the event described by the sentence. Assume the report is truthful, so r(w)=0 outside U, and assume Z=P(R)>0. Then P(U)>0. The full posterior given R equals the prior conditioned just on U if and only if r(w) is the same positive constant for every world in U having positive prior probability.

To prove sufficiency, let that constant be c. Then Z=cP(U), so p(w)r(w)/Z=p(w)/P(U) for w in U and both posteriors are zero outside. Conversely, equality at each positive-prior w in U gives p(w)r(w)/Z=p(w)/P(U). Cancel p(w)>0 to obtain r(w)=Z/P(U), the same positive number for each such world. Zero-prior worlds impose no restriction. If Z=0, the received report cannot be conditioned on by this ratio.

This compares every world, hence every event in the full posterior. Agreement for one target event can occur accidentally even when the likelihood is not constant; exercise 4 provides an example. The general observation-mechanism criterion appears in Grünwald and Halpern, Updating Probabilities, Theorem 3.2 (UAI 2002, printed page 190). The finite algebra here proves the special statement we use.

The card and original dice counts remain valid for their stated event-conditioning models. If an actual person chooses which truthful clue to announce, that selection is an additional part of the experiment. With an unspecified rule, a truthful sentence alone need not determine a unique posterior. Our two rules make that difference exact without inventing any observed evidence.

Try the event and the reporting rule

Exercise 1. A fair die is rolled. Given an even result, what is the chance the result is at least four?

Hint for exercise 1

Write the outcomes left after the even clue.

Full solution to exercise 1

The clue leaves {2,4,6}. The favorable outcomes are {4,6}; hence P(at least four|even)=2/3. In the formula, the intersection has chance 2/6 and even has chance 3/6, yielding (2/6)/(3/6)=2/3.

Exercise 2. A bag holds three red and two blue tokens. Is P(red|blue)=P(blue|red) when one token is drawn? Explain.

Hint for exercise 2

Can one drawn token have both colors?

Full solution to exercise 2

For this single draw, red and blue are disjoint, so both conditional probabilities are zero; each denominator is positive. That equality is a special case, not a general symmetry. If the events were “red” and “striped” and some red tokens were striped, the two denominators could differ. The question must specify the event model before swapping the condition.

Exercise 3. A different truthful sentence. Change the report to “at least one die is even.” Keep fair independent dice and an independent fair selector. The scan rule reports iff either die is even. The selected rule reports iff the chosen die is even, concealing its identity. Under each rule, find the report probability and P(total seven | report). Find the posterior probabilities of the individual pairs (1,2) and (2,2). Work from the 72 states or likelihoods; the explorer only covers six-reports.

Hint for exercise 3

There are nine odd-odd pairs, eighteen pairs with exactly one even face, and nine even-even pairs. Every total-seven pair has exactly one even face. Each pair has two prior selector states.

Full solution to exercise 3

Scan keeps 36−9=27 pairs, each with two reporting selectors, so 54/72=3/4 of prior triples report. All six total-seven pairs contribute both selectors, giving 12/54=2/9. Every compatible pair, including (1,2) and (2,2), then has posterior 1/27.

Selected reports for three even blue faces times six yellow faces, plus three even yellow faces times six blue faces: 18+18=36 triples. Its report probability is 36/72=1/2. The six total-seven pairs each report for just their even selector, giving 6/36=1/6. The likelihood for (1,2) is 1/2, giving posterior 1/36; the likelihood for (2,2) is 1, giving 2/36=1/18. The posterior check is eighteen single-even pairs times 1/36 plus nine double-even pairs times 1/18, totaling 1.

Exercise 4. One matching answer does not identify a posterior. Keep exercise 3's even-report protocols. Let A be “the blue face is either 1 or 2.” Compute P(A | report) under each. Does agreement show that reporting can be replaced by conditioning only on “at least one even”? Explain using one individual pair.

Hint for exercise 4

When blue is 1, three yellow faces are compatible. When blue is 2, all six are compatible. In the selected protocol, count reporting selectors separately for the two blue values.

Full solution to exercise 4

Scan gives nine compatible pairs for A out of 27, so P(A | report)=1/3. Selected gives three reporting triples when blue is 1 (yellow must be even and selected), and nine when blue is 2 (six with blue selected, three with yellow selected). Thus P(A | report)=12/36=1/3 as well. This particular event agrees, but the full posteriors differ: (2,2) has probability 1/27 under scan and 1/18 under selected. Nonconstant likelihood fails the full-posterior criterion despite this accidental event equality.

What to carry forward

Conditional probability uses a positive-probability event and the correct prior weights. Conditioning on a truthful report eliminates incompatible worlds and weights compatible worlds by their chances of producing that report. Constant reporting likelihood makes event-only conditioning valid for the full posterior; truthfulness or one matching event probability does not establish that condition. Specify the prior, any hidden selection and the reporting rule before counting. Continue to Bayes’ update to reason backward from an observed clue to a hidden cause.
