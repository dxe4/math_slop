One quotient, every possible approach

A derivative of a real function checks steps from two sides of a line. For a complex function, the step h can approach zero along any path in a plane. A proposed answer must survive all such approaches. This extra demand explains why familiar real operations, such as reflection across the real axis, can fail complex differentiability.

Background capsule. For z=x+iy, conjugation is z̄=x−iy, and |z|=√(x²+y²). A limit L of a complex quantity Q(h) as h→0 means: for every ε>0 there is δ>0 such that 0<|h|<δ implies |Q(h)−L|<ε. This tests all complex h in the punctured δ-disk, not merely a selected list of directions. Function composition and algebra work as usual when quantities are defined.

A polynomial succeeds; a mirror fails

Worked example. For f(z)=z², expand before taking a limit: [(z+h)²−z²]/h=2z+h whenever h≠0. Its distance from 2z is |h|, so choosing δ=ε proves the limit for every approach. In particular f′(z)=2z even at z=0. The calculation is stronger than checking the real and imaginary axes separately.

For g(z)=z̄, the corresponding quotient is h̄/h. If h=t is nonzero real, it equals 1. If h=it is nonzero pure imaginary, it equals −1. Both can tend to zero, so no common limit exists at any z. Conjugation has real partial derivatives and is smooth as a map of real coordinates (x,y)↦(x,−y); nevertheless it has no complex derivative.

A small imaginary step h=0.01i instead has predicted change −0.02+0.02i and exact change −0.0201+0.02i. Both errors have modulus 0.0001=|h|². One multiplication predicts both directions, with the same relative error bound.

The exact definition and its boundaries

At an interior point z of a function's domain, complex differentiability means the following limit exists as a finite complex number:

f′(z)=limh→0f(z+h)−f(z)h

An interior point is required so the function is defined on a full small disk around it. If the derivative exists at every point of an open set, the function is called holomorphic there. A derivative at a single point alone does not make a function holomorphic on a neighborhood.

Complex differentiability also has an approximation form: f(z+h)=f(z)+Ah+r(h), with |r(h)|/|h|→0 and A=f′(z). For the square map, r(h)=h². This form explains why the derivative is multiplication by one complex number: a local rotation and scale when A≠0. It follows directly by multiplying the quotient's error by h, and conversely dividing the approximation by h.

Two different approach paths are enough to disprove a limit, because a true limit would agree on both. Agreement on any finite collection is not enough to prove it. For example, define q(h)=1 when h lies on either coordinate axis and q(h)=0 elsewhere, for h≠0. Its values along both axes agree, but along the diagonal h=t(1+i) they are zero. A full δ-bound, as in the square-map calculation, is the positive evidence needed.

Test a derivative and a tempting shortcut

1. Calculate. Determine whether f(z)=z̄² has a complex derivative at z=0. Then test z=1.

Hint for 1

At zero, bound |h̄²/h|. At one, expand (1+h̄)²−1 and compare real and imaginary h.

Worked solution for 1

At zero the quotient is h̄²/h, with modulus |h|, so it tends to zero in every direction. Thus f′(0)=0. At one the quotient is 2h̄/h+h̄²/h. The last term has modulus |h|→0, while the first tends to 2 along real steps and −2 along imaginary steps. No derivative exists at one. Differentiability at zero does not imply holomorphy nearby.

2. Explain. Someone checks h=t and h=it for f(z)=z² and announces a proof. Is the answer correct? Is the reasoning sufficient?

Hint for 2

Compare the two path checks with the exact quotient 2z+h and the ε–δ definition.

Worked solution for 2

The announced derivative 2z is correct, but two path checks do not prove it. The exact identity makes the error equal to h for every nonzero complex h. Hence its modulus is below ε whenever |h|<ε. That universal estimate is the missing proof.

A demanding but usable test

Expand and simplify when possible, then bound the error for arbitrary complex h. A mismatch along two paths certifies failure. Real smoothness and even a derivative at one exceptional point do not supply holomorphy on an open set. The Cauchy–Riemann equations next translate directional agreement into coordinate conditions, with a necessary regularity qualification for the converse.
