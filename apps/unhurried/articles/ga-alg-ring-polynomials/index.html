Are polynomials just functions in disguise?

Polynomials can be added and multiplied much like numbers, but it is useful to treat them as formal coefficient lists. Over a finite field, different polynomials can even produce the same value at every available input. This lesson proves a central ring law from finite sums and shows exactly where “same function” fails to mean “same polynomial.”

Background capsule. A commutative ring R has addition, multiplication, zero and one with the familiar associative and distributive laws; additive inverses exist, but multiplicative inverses need not. A polynomial f∈R[x] is a finite formal sum a₀+a₁x+⋯+adxd. Two polynomials are equal when every coefficient agrees, including trailing zeros. Evaluation at r∈R substitutes r for x and adds in R. The field F₂ has coefficients 0 and 1 with arithmetic modulo 2.

Multiply coefficients, then evaluate

Worked example. Let f=1+x and g=1+x+x² in Z[x]. Multiplication distributes every term of f over every term of g: fg=1+2x+2x²+x³. At x=2, f(2)=3 and g(2)=7, so f(2)g(2)=21. The product polynomial gives 1+4+8+8=21 as well. The agreement is a general algebraic law, not an accident of the chosen input.

Now work over F₂. The polynomial h=x²−x is not the zero polynomial: its x² coefficient is 1, and its x coefficient −1 equals 1 in F₂. Yet h(0)=0 and h(1)=1−1=0. It represents the zero function on F₂, whose only inputs are 0 and 1. An evaluation table cannot always recover formal coefficients.

Why R[x] inherits distributivity

(fg)k=∑i+j=ki,j≥0aibj

The displayed coefficient formula says that the coefficient of xk in fg is the sum of aibj over pairs i+j=k; only finitely many terms are nonzero. Exact statement and proof. If R is a commutative ring with 1, then R[x] is also a commutative ring with 1. Addition is coefficientwise, giving associativity, commutativity, zero and additive inverses from R. Multiplication uses the displayed finite sum. Associativity follows because the coefficient of xk in (fg)h and f(gh) is in both cases the finite sum of aibjcl over i+j+l=k; reassociating terms is valid in R. Its unit is the constant polynomial 1. Commutativity follows by exchanging i and j.

For distributivity, write g=∑bjxj and h=∑cjxj. The coefficient of xk in f(g+h) is ∑i+j=kai(bj+cj). Distributivity in R turns this into the sum of the k-coefficients of fg and fh. Equal coefficients mean equal formal polynomials. This proves f(g+h)=fg+fh for every polynomial, rather than relying on values at selected points.

Evaluation preserves both addition and multiplication because substitution distributes through finite sums and products. The converse is delicate. Over an infinite integral domain, a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish everywhere; over F₂ the explicit h above does. Thus the polynomial ring and the ring of polynomial functions on F₂ are different objects.

Track coefficients and functions

1. Calculation. Multiply (1+x)(1−x+x²) in Z[x], then evaluate both factors and the product at x=2.

Hint for question 1

Distribute once and combine like powers; then use ordinary integer arithmetic at x=2.

Full solution for question 1

The product is (1−x+x²)+(x−x²+x³)=1+x³. At x=2 it gives 1+8=9. The factors give 1+2=3 and 1−2+4=3, whose product is also 9.

2. Explanation. Over F₂, are x² and x the same polynomial? Are they the same function F₂→F₂?

Hint for question 2

Compare coefficients first, then test both possible inputs.

Full solution for question 2

They are different formal polynomials because their coefficients of x² and x differ. As functions they agree: at 0 both return 0, and at 1 both return 1. Their difference x²−x is nonzero as a polynomial and zero at every element of F₂.

A formal object with evaluation maps

Polynomial addition and multiplication form a ring because finite coefficient sums inherit the ring laws. Evaluation sends a formal polynomial to a function, but the map can merge distinct polynomials over finite fields. This is the failure case to remember when a table of outputs seems to determine an expression.

Ideals provide another way to identify expressions, by declaring selected polynomial or integer relations to count as zero. That is a different construction from merely testing finitely many inputs.
