Why does a chord make a half-angle?

Fix two points A and B on a circle. Move a point P along one of the arcs between them. The angle APB remains unchanged as long as P stays on that same arc. It equals half the central angle subtending the other arc AB. Stating the arc is essential: on the opposite arc, the angle is supplementary, not identical.

Background capsule. A chord joins two circle points. A central angle has its vertex at the circle centre O; an inscribed angle has its vertex P on the circle. Equal radii make triangles OAP and OBP isosceles, so each has equal base angles. A circle's full turn is 2π radians. We take distinct A, B and P, and for the simple half-angle form choose AB's minor arc with central angle θ strictly between 0 and π and P on the major arc.

One exact configuration

Worked example. Use the unit circle with A=(1,0), B=(0,1), O=(0,0), and P=(−1,0). The central minor angle AOB is π/2. At P, PA points right and PB points northeast, so ∠APB=π/4. Moving P along the major arc changes both rays but preserves that angle. If instead P lies on the minor arc, for example at (√2/2,√2/2), it sees the long arc AB and ∠APB=3π/4. These exact values make the arc restriction more than a technical footnote.

The two inscribed angles add to π. Thus opposite vertices of a cyclic quadrilateral make supplementary angles, provided the vertices occur in alternating arc order. A screen drawing might obscure which arc is intercepted; marking A, B and P in order resolves the ambiguity.

From equal radii to the inscribed-angle theorem

Exact theorem with case argument. Let A, B, P be three distinct points on one Euclidean circle, and let θ be the central measure of the arc AB that does not contain P. Then

∠APB=θ2

Draw diameter PQ through centre O, with Q opposite P. In isosceles triangle OAP, let x=∠APO=∠OAP. The angle AOQ is exterior to that triangle at O, so ∠AOQ=2x. Likewise, if y=∠BPO, then ∠BOQ=2y. When PA and PB lie on opposite sides of PQ, ∠APB=x+y and the intercepted central arc measure is 2x+2y. When they lie on the same side, ∠APB is the positive difference |x−y| and the intercepted central arc measure is |2x−2y|. If a ray coincides with PQ, one term is zero. These cases prove the same half relation for every placement of P that does not equal A or B. The “central arc measure” can exceed π; it is the arc not containing P, not automatically the smaller central angle.

For P on the major arc and θ<π, the intercepted arc is the minor arc, so the familiar form ∠APB=(1/2)∠AOB follows. For P on the minor arc, the intercepted arc measure is 2π−θ, giving π−θ/2. The proof uses Euclidean exterior-angle and isosceles-triangle facts. It does not establish the same equation for a circle drawn on a sphere using great-circle geodesics.

Choose the correct arc

1. Calculation. Minor central angle AOB is 110°. Find APB when P is on the major arc, then when P is on the minor arc.

Hint for question 1

First decide whether P intercepts 110° or 360°−110°.

Full solution for question 1

On the major arc, P intercepts the 110° minor arc, so APB=55°. On the minor arc, P intercepts the 250° major arc, so APB=125°. These angles are supplementary, as 55°+125°=180°.

2. Failure test. A classmate says that any P on the circle gives APB=55° in the previous setup. Give the error and a counterexample location.

Hint for question 2

Put P on the other arc.

Full solution for question 2

Take any P strictly on the minor arc AB. It intercepts the 250° major arc, giving APB=125°, not 55°. The formula needs the intercepted arc, so moving P across A or B changes which arc is seen. At P=A or P=B the angle itself is undefined because one ray has zero length.

The chord remembers an arc

Every inscribed angle is half the measure of the arc it intercepts. For a fixed chord, points on the same open arc see the same opposite arc and hence the same angle. Crossing to the other arc changes the intercepted arc and usually changes the angle to its supplement. The proof is complete for Euclidean circles with distinct points; it uses equal radii and exterior-angle arithmetic. A reliable diagram should label the intercepted arc as well as the central point, because “half the angle at the centre” without that label can give the wrong answer.
