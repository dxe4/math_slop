Can two pack sizes make exactly one unit?

Imagine movements of 26 units forward and 15 units backward. Can some signed combination land at 1? Negative coefficients are allowed: they stand for reversing a movement, not for ordering a negative number of physical packs. An expression 26x+15y with integer x and y is an integer linear combination. The smallest positive number attainable this way is connected to gcd(26,15).

Here is the local refresher. A common divisor of a and b divides both. The Euclidean algorithm replaces (a,b) by (b,r) whenever a = qb+r and 0 ≤ r < b; the pair has the same common divisors, and positive remainders decrease until zero. Bézout's identity states that the gcd can be written as one of these integer linear combinations. We will construct the coefficients and prove why this always succeeds, rather than treating the coefficients as a lucky guess.

Reverse a remainder ledger

Worked example. Divide: 26 = 1·15+11; 15 = 1·11+4; 11 = 2·4+3; 4 = 1·3+1; 3 = 3·1+0. The final nonzero remainder is 1, so gcd(26,15)=1. To express that 1 using only the original inputs, reverse the equalities. Start with 1 = 4−3 and replace 3 by 11−2·4, giving 1 = 3·4−11. Replace 4 by 15−11 to get 1 = 3·15−4·11. Finally replace 11 by 26−15: 1 = 7·15−4·26.

Checking the numerical certificate is easy: 7·15−4·26 = 105−104 = 1. The hard part is knowing such a certificate must exist for any pair not both zero.

Why back-substitution always works

Bézout's identity. For integers a and b, not both zero, there exist integers x and y such that ax+by=gcd(a,b), where gcd is positive. Apply Euclid to |a| and |b|. The initial two numbers are integer combinations of themselves: |a|=1·|a|+0·|b| and |b|=0·|a|+1·|b|. If two successive ledger values are such combinations, their remainder, the first minus an integer multiple of the second, is another. Induction through the finite ledger makes the last nonzero remainder a combination. Euclid proved that remainder is the gcd. Change coefficient signs as needed to return from |a|,|b| to a,b.

ax+by=gcd(a,b)

This is a complete existence proof assuming the division algorithm for integers, which supplied the quotient and remainder. It does not say that x and y are unique. For our example, x = −4, y = 7 works; so does x = 11, y = −19, because 26·11+15·(−19)=1. The coefficient pair changes while the gcd does not.

Failure case. If gcd(a,b)=d>1, no integer combination can equal 1. Every ax+by is divisible by d, while 1 is not. For example 14x+21y is always divisible by 7. Its best positive target is 7, obtained as 21−14. The conclusion “we can always make one unit” therefore needs the coprimality condition d=1. The identity itself remains true for noncoprime inputs.

Worked example: reaching a multiple of the gcd

Which integer targets can 18x + 30y reach? Euclid gives 30 = 18 + 12 and 18 = 12 + 6, so 6 = 18 − 12 = 2·18 − 30. To reach 24, multiply this certificate by 4: 24 = 8·18 − 4·30. The chosen coefficients are integers, and direct multiplication checks 144 − 120 = 24.

More generally, multiplying the certificate for 6 by any integer t reaches 6t. Conversely, both 18 and 30 are divisible by 6, so every integer combination is divisible by 6. Together these two arguments characterize all reachable targets as the multiples of 6. Finding one Bézout certificate therefore supplies more information than finding just one small successful target. The coefficients need not be nonnegative; imposing that additional constraint would change the problem.

Build and interpret two certificates

1. Find integers x,y with 35x+12y=1 using remainder equations, then check your result by multiplication.

Hint for question 1

Start with 35=2·12+11 and 12=1·11+1. Express the last remainder using the two earlier equations.

Full solution to question 1

Euclid gives 35=2·12+11 and 12=1·11+1. Thus 1=12−11=12−(35−2·12)=3·12−35. Hence x=−1 and y=3. Direct check: 35(−1)+12(3)=−35+36=1, so gcd(35,12)=1.

2. Someone claims 18x+30y=5 has an integer solution because 5 is small. Decide whether this is possible and provide a reachable positive target.

Hint for question 2

Compute the gcd before searching for coefficients.

Full solution to question 2

gcd(18,30)=6 because 30=1·18+12, 18=1·12+6, and 12=2·6. Every integer combination 18x+30y is divisible by 6, so it cannot equal 5. A reachable positive target is 6: 6=2·18−30, using x=2 and y=−1. The failure concerns target 5, not the validity of Bézout's identity.

Keep the coefficients, not just the gcd

The Euclidean algorithm computes a gcd; its reverse ledger produces a certificate ax+by=gcd(a,b). This certificate will tell us exactly when multiplication has an inverse on a modular clock. It does not by itself claim uniqueness of the coefficients or any efficient cryptographic security. For practical calculation, checking the final equality catches a surprising number of sign mistakes.
