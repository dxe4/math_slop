Can curvature fix a badly scaled valley?

Gradient descent uses slope and a chosen step length. Newton's method builds a local quadratic model and solves for that model's minimum. For an actual positive quadratic it can reach the answer in one step; elsewhere it is a local method whose full step may fail. A positive-definite Hessian guarantees a unique model-minimizing Newton step and, away from stationarity, a descent direction.

Background capsule. For twice differentiable f:ℝⁿ→ℝ, ∇f(x) is the gradient and Hf(x) is the symmetric matrix of second partial derivatives. A quadratic Taylor model around x for displacement p is mₓ(p)=f(x)+∇f(x)ᵀp+½pᵀHf(x)p. Positive-definite H makes its quadratic term strictly upward in every nonzero direction. Solving Hp=−∇f means finding a vector p whose Hessian-weighted image cancels the gradient; it is preferable to explicitly computing a matrix inverse.

A narrow quadratic, solved exactly

Worked example. Take f(x,y)=½(x−1)²+50(y+2)². Its minimizer is (1,−2). The gradient is (x−1,100(y+2)), and the Hessian is diag(1,100). From (3,0), gradient is (2,200). Newton solves p₁=−2 and 100p₂=−200, so p=(−2,−2). One full update lands at (1,−2), where the objective is zero. A single gradient step with α=1 would instead move to (1,−200), wildly overshooting the steep y direction.

The one-step result is not magic: this f equals its quadratic Taylor model exactly everywhere. Newton corrects each eigen-direction by its own curvature scale. That explains the appeal of the method on a narrow valley, but a general function's Hessian changes with location and its local model may mispredict a distant full step.

Derive the step, then inspect its assumptions

Setting the model gradient with respect to p to zero gives Hf(xk)pk=−∇f(xk),xk+1=xk+pk

Exact quadratic theorem. If f(x)=½(x−x*)ᵀQ(x−x*)+c with Q symmetric positive definite, Newton's full step from any x lands at x*. Indeed ∇f(x)=Q(x−x*) and Hf=Q. The linear system Qp=−Q(x−x*) has unique solution p=−(x−x*), hence x+p=x*. Positive definiteness ensures the target is the unique global minimum. This is a theorem for a quadratic objective; it says nothing about arbitrary nonlinear functions.

For a general twice differentiable function, if H(x) is positive definite and ∇f(x)≠0, then the Newton direction is locally descending: ∇f(x)ᵀp=−pᵀH(x)p<0. By differentiability, a sufficiently small positive step t along p decreases f. A full step t=1 may still increase f, so practical algorithms often use line search or a trust region. This paragraph proves local descent from positive definiteness; it does not prove a global convergence theorem. Quadratic local convergence near a suitable nondegenerate minimizer requires stronger smoothness and a sufficiently close start, which we leave as a named advanced result.

A simple failure is f(x)=−x². At x=1, gradient −2 and Hessian −2. Newton's equation gives p=−1 and lands at x=0, a maximum. The Hessian was negative, so the quadratic model had no minimum. Another failure is a singular Hessian: for f(x)=x⁴ at zero, both gradient and Hessian vanish, and a unique Newton direction is undefined even though zero is a minimum.

Separate model from objective

1. Calculation. For f(x,y)=2(x−3)²+½(y+1)², start at (0,4). Compute the Newton direction and next point.

Hint for question 1

The Hessian is diagonal. Divide each negative gradient component by its matching diagonal entry.

Full solution for question 1

At (0,4), the gradient is (4(0−3),4+1)=(−12,5), and H=diag(4,1). Solving Hp=−∇f gives p=(3,−5). The next point is (0,4)+(3,−5)=(3,−1), the unique minimizer, with value zero. The exact quadratic theorem explains the one-step finish.

2. Failure check. Apply a full Newton step to f(x)=−x² from x=2. Does the new point minimize f locally or globally?

Hint for question 2

Solve (−2)p=−(−4), then classify the new point by nearby values.

Full solution for question 2

At x=2, gradient is −4 and Hessian −2, so −2p=4 and p=−2. The full step lands at 0. There f(0)=0 but f(ε)=−ε²<0 for every nonzero ε, so 0 is a strict local maximum, not a local minimum. Globally f has no lower bound on ℝ. The negative Hessian invalidates the model-minimum interpretation.

Use curvature with a check

Newton's direction minimizes a positive-definite quadratic local model. It exactly solves positive-definite quadratics in one step, but full steps on changing or indefinite curvature require control and classification. A solver for the Hessian system is a tool, not a certificate of global optimality. Compare this with fixed-step descent: each method has a different narrow theorem and a different failure mode.
