Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

If a quantity accumulates, what is its current rate?

Let rain fall at a changing rate f(t). The total rain collected by time x is the area under the rate graph from a starting time a to x. A thin additional time interval should add roughly its width times the rate at x. The first fundamental theorem of calculus makes that intuition exact when the input is continuous.

Background capsule. A definite integral ∫ from a to b of f(t)dt is the limit of signed rectangle sums when that limit exists. For a nonnegative rate, it is an area; for a rate that changes sign, positive and negative contributions cancel. A derivative of A at x is the limit of [A(x+h)−A(x)]/h for nonzero h approaching zero from both sides. Continuity of f at x says nearby values f(t) are arbitrarily close to f(x). Here a is fixed and x may vary inside an interval where f is integrable.

A bowl that fills according to x²

Worked example. Take f(t)=t² on [0,2] and define A(x)=∫ from 0 to x of t²dt. The rectangle-sum lesson gives A(x)=x³/3 for x≥0. At x=1, the accumulated amount is 1/3. At x=1.1, it is 1.331/3≈0.443667, an increment of 0.110333. Divide by 0.1 to get an average rate of about 1.10333. The current input at x=1 is exactly f(1)=1. The difference comes from the rate rising across that tenth of an interval.

For a symmetric-looking but genuinely one-sided increment h=−0.1, A(0.9)=0.729/3=0.243, and [A(0.9)−A(1)]/(−0.1)=0.90333. The two average rates surround 1. As |h| shrinks, both tend to 1. The sign convention works: a negative increment means going backwards in accumulation, and division by a negative h returns a positive local rate.

Do not confuse A(x) with f(x). The first stores everything from the starting point to x; the second is the input right now. Changing the starting point shifts A by a constant but does not change its interior derivative.

A theorem from local bounds

Statement (first fundamental theorem). Let f be Riemann integrable on an interval containing a and x, and continuous at the interior point x. Define A(y)=∫ from a to y of f(t)dt. Then A is differentiable at x and A′(x)=f(x). A familiar sufficient setup is f continuous on the entire interval. At endpoints, only the appropriate one-sided derivative is claimed.

A(x)=∫axf(t)dt⇒A′(x)=f(x)

Proof. Additivity of the integral gives A(x+h)−A(x)=∫ from x to x+h of f(t)dt, including h<0 under signed orientation. Subtract f(x)h and divide by h. Given ε>0, continuity at x supplies δ>0 such that |f(t)−f(x)|<ε whenever |t−x|<δ. For 0<|h|<δ, every t between x and x+h satisfies this bound, so the integral of the deviation has absolute value at most ε|h|. The quotient's error is therefore at most ε, for either sign of h. This proves the derivative statement without assuming a graph looks smooth.

The second fundamental theorem says something different: if f is continuous on [a,b], F is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and F′=f there, then ∫ from a to b f=F(b)−F(a). It follows here because A is one antiderivative and two antiderivatives differ by a constant on an interval, using the mean value theorem. We do not need this consequence to prove the local statement above.

Boundary. Continuity at the point matters. Let f(t)=0 for t<0 and f(t)=1 for t≥0, with A(x)=∫ from −1 to x f(t)dt. At x=0, A is constant to the left and rises with slope 1 to the right; A′(0) does not exist. The integral still exists. Merely being integrable does not force a derivative at every point.

Rates from accumulated input

1. Calculate. Define B(x)=∫ from 2 to x (3+t²)dt. Find B(2), B′(2), and B(3).

Hint for 1

The interval from 2 to 2 has zero width. For B(3), integrate the constant and t² separately.

Worked solution for 1

B(2)=0. The input is continuous, so B′(2)=3+2²=7. For B(3), ∫₂³3dt=3 and ∫₂³t²dt=(27−8)/3=19/3. Thus B(3)=28/3. The average input over [2,3] is 28/3, larger than its initial value 7 because t² increases.

2. Explain. If f(t)=|t| and C(x)=∫ from −1 to x f(t)dt, does the corner in f prevent C′(0)?

Hint for 2

The theorem requires continuity of f, not differentiability of f.

Worked solution for 2

No. |t| is continuous at zero even though its derivative there fails. Therefore C′(0)=f(0)=0. Directly, for h>0 the added area is h²/2; for h<0 it is the negative of the area over [h,0], namely −h²/2. Dividing by h gives |h|/2 in both cases, which tends to zero.

Accumulation remembers; differentiation asks now

For an integrable input continuous at x, a short accumulated increment differs from f(x)h by less than any chosen fraction of |h| once h is small enough. That is the precise bridge between area and rate. A jump in the input can break differentiability of the accumulation at the jump. Explore the mean value theorem next if you want a complementary statement: an average change must be attained as a local rate somewhere.
