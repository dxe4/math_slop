Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Preparation return. You need fractions, finite sample spaces and conditional probability. If those feel uncertain, return to the original lesson’s product test and two original cases, or the conditional-probability explanation. Here a word such as 101 records three lights in the fixed order X,Y,Z; 1 means on, 0 means off. An event is a collection of words. P(A) is the sum of their probabilities, and A ∩ B means both events occur. When P(A) is positive, P(B | A)=P(A ∩ B)/P(A).

Read the complete ticket law before multiplying

Hint for the ticket calculation

For A ∩ B, add 110 and 111. For A ∩ C, add 101 and 111; for B ∩ C, add 011 and 111. The combined clue A ∩ B has positive probability even though 111 has none.

Full worked ticket calculation

The intersections are P(A ∩ B)=2/8=1/4=(1/2)(1/2), P(A ∩ C)=1/8=(1/2)(1/4), and P(B ∩ C)=1/8=(1/2)(1/4). All three pairs pass. But P(A ∩ B ∩ C)=0, whereas (1/2)(1/2)(1/4)=1/16. The three events are pairwise independent and are not mutually independent. One clue gives P(C | A)=(1/8)/(1/2)=1/4. Both clues give P(C | A ∩ B)=0/(1/4)=0. Neither division has a zero denominator. Dependence on the combined clues is compatible with independence from each clue separately.

State exactly which products a claim requires

Exact finite statement. Two events are independent when the probability of their intersection equals the product of their probabilities. Three events are pairwise independent when this holds for all three pairs. They are mutually independent when the three pair equalities and the triple equality all hold. The central test for three is therefore:

P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A∩C)=P(A)P(C)P(B∩C)=P(B)P(C)P(A∩B∩C)=P(A)P(B)P(C)

The ticket calculation is a complete counterexample to the claim that passing all pairs suffices. A triple equality alone would also leave the pair requirements unchecked. This is a calculation from an exact model, not a conclusion drawn from a few observed records.

Replacement describes the bag, not the whole mechanism. Putting a token back restores the contents. It does not specify how the next token is selected. Independent uniform draws from a restored bag do give the product law: for any first labelled token i and second labelled token j, the conditional second-selection probability is 1/N whatever i, hence the joint probability is (1/N)(1/N). Summing over the chosen colour events gives the colour product law. A rule that remembers i can give a different conditional chance, even with the same restored bag and the same unconditional colour chances.

Change the procedure, then return without the labels

The restored four-token bag

Exercise 1. Work through the following changed selection procedure.

A bag has four distinct tokens, two red and two blue. Choose the first token uniformly, record its colour and replace it. Then use a fresh fair six-sided die whose result is independent of the first selection. On 1 or 2, select that same labelled token again. On 3,4,5 or 6, select uniformly from all four tokens using fresh randomness independent of the first selection and the die. Record the second colour. The copying branch is deliberately not a fresh uniform draw conditional on the first token.

Compute the four colour-pair probabilities, both single-red probabilities and the chance of second red given first red. Does physical replacement justify multiplying the two red probabilities here? Explain which assumption from fresh repeated draws is missing.

Hint for exercise 1

Separate the die branches. With probability 1/3 the colours must agree. With probability 2/3 the four colour pairs each have conditional chance 1/4. Include the chance of the first colour in the copying branch too.

Full solution to exercise 1

Write A for first red and B for second red. The copying branch contributes (1/3)(1/2)=1/6 to red-red and 1/6 to blue-blue, and nothing to mixed pairs. The fresh branch contributes (2/3)(1/4)=1/6 to each of the four pairs. Thus red-red, red-blue, blue-red, blue-blue have probabilities 1/3,1/6,1/6,1/3. They sum to one. P(A)=1/3+1/6=1/2, and P(B)=1/3+1/6=1/2. But P(A ∩ B)=1/3 differs from their product 1/4, so these events are dependent. Also P(B | A)=(1/3)/(1/2)=2/3. Replacement restored the bag; the memory-dependent selection rule prevented the full procedure from being a fresh uniform draw conditional on the first selection. If the copying branch were removed and every second draw used the fresh uniform rule, each colour pair would have chance 1/4.

Full solution to exercise 2

Record I. The probability of a first gold is 2/5. The conditional second-gold chance is 2/5 after every possible first label, so gold-gold has chance 4/25. Gold-silver, silver-gold and silver-silver have chances 6/25,6/25,9/25. The two single-gold chances are both 2/5 and their product is 4/25: the events are independent under this specified rule.

Record II. A first gold forces a second gold, and a first silver forces a second silver. The colour table in the same order is 2/5,0,0,3/5. Each single-gold chance remains 2/5, but the two-gold chance is 2/5, not 4/25. The events are dependent. The single-position probabilities and physical replacement do not distinguish these two procedures; the joint law does.

Record III. The counts sum to sixteen. With A meaning X=1, B meaning Y=1 and C meaning Z=1, their counts are 8,4,4, so P(A)=1/2 and P(B)=P(C)=1/4. The two-light counts are 2,2,1: P(A ∩ B)=1/8=(1/2)(1/4), P(A ∩ C)=1/8=(1/2)(1/4), and P(B ∩ C)=1/16=(1/4)(1/4). All pairs pass. But the all-three chance is 1/16, twice the proposed product (1/2)(1/4)(1/4)=1/32. These events are pairwise independent and not mutually independent. The technician’s proposal fails already on word 111. Indeed, the combined clue A ∩ B gives P(C | A ∩ B)=(1/16)/(1/8)=1/2, while C alone has chance 1/4.

These conclusions follow from the complete exact probability models given in the prompts. Counts in a short observed sample would instead be uncertain evidence about an unknown law; matching a few empirical products would not prove the underlying independence claim.

Repair conditional fractions and evidence for two events

Repair a conditional fraction. Work within one specified probability law. P(B | A)=P(A ∩ B)/P(A) requires P(A) to be positive. If P(A)=0, this ratio does not define P(B | A); a zero numerator does not repair the zero denominator. The product test separately compares P(A ∩ B) with P(A)P(B).

Restored four-token bag. Let A mean first red and B mean second red. P(A)=1/2 and P(A ∩ B)=1/3, so P(B | A)=(1/3)/(1/2)=2/3. The denominator is positive. This differs from P(B)=1/2: after a first red, the copying branch increases the second-red chance.

Record I. Let A mean first gold and B mean second gold. P(A)=2/5 and P(A ∩ B)=4/25, so P(B | A)=(4/25)/(2/5)=2/5. The denominator is positive. This equals P(B)=2/5 under this record's fresh uniform second-selection rule.

Record II. Keep those gold events in this record's law. P(A)=2/5 and P(A ∩ B)=2/5, so P(B | A)=(2/5)/(2/5)=1. The denominator is positive. This differs from P(B)=2/5 because the second selection must have the first selection's colour.

Repair an observation claim. These calculations follow exactly from the stipulated selection laws. A short observed run instead gives uncertain evidence about a law: a near equality of empirical products does not prove exact independence. An empirical conditional frequency divides the count of A ∩ B by the count of A only when that sample denominator is positive. If no A was observed, this empirical conditional fraction is undefined, not zero. A zero sample count does not establish P(A)=0: a finite run of independent repetitions of a model whose P(A) is strictly between zero and one can contain no A. Keep the model probabilities and sample frequencies distinct before revising your explanation.

Keep the model attached to the multiplication

Independence concerns specified events under a specified probability law. All pairs can pass while a three-event claim fails. Restoring a bag does not determine the next selection mechanism, and equal single-position chances do not settle a joint probability. Return to the original preparation and examples when the distinction needs another concrete pass. Once you can reconstruct both tables, use the binomial lesson to ask which full-sequence products its derivation needs.
