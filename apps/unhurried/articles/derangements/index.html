A secret gift exchange forbids self-matches.

At a gift exchange, nobody should receive the gift they brought. For three participants A, B, C, the six possible assignments can be written as strings of received gift labels in A,B,C order: ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA. Only BCA and CAB avoid matching a position with its owner. Thus D₃=2. For larger groups, listing all n! assignments grows quickly; we need a way to remove every forbidden self-match while restoring assignments removed more than once.

Prerequisite capsule. A permutation assigns each of n named people one of n named gifts, using every gift exactly once. A fixed point is a person who receives their own gift. A derangement has no fixed points. We use n! for all assignments and C(n,k) for ways to choose k named people whose self-assignments we require. Empty products give 0!=1.

Count a concrete case

Worked example. Let Eᵢ be the set of assignments in which person i receives their own gift. There are (n−1)! assignments in Eᵢ because the remaining gifts can be permuted freely. For any chosen set of k people, the intersection of their Eᵢ sets has (n−k)! assignments: fix those k people and permute the rest. There are C(n,k) different k-person sets of required self-matches. Starting with n! assignments, inclusion–exclusion subtracts the one-event intersections, adds the two-event intersections, and alternates through all k. For n=3 the ledger is 3!−C(3,1)2!+C(3,2)1!−C(3,3)0! = 6−6+3−1=2. For n=4 it is 24−24+12−4+1=9.

The exact rule and its proof

Dn=∑k=0n(−1)k(nk)(n−k)!=n!∑k=0n(−1)kk!

Derangement formula. For every n≥0, the displayed finite sum counts permutations with no fixed point. To justify inclusion–exclusion directly, take a permutation with exactly t fixed points. It appears once in the initial n! count. For each k it appears in C(t,k) intersections of k forbidden events, so its signed total contribution is Σ from k=0 to t of (−1)^k C(t,k) = (1−1)^t. For t>0, choose one fixed point and pair every subset of the t fixed points that omits it with the subset obtained by adding it. Each pair has opposite signs and cancels. When t=0 only the empty subset remains, with contribution one; hence exactly the desired permutations survive. The last equality in the formula uses C(n,k)(n−k)!=n!/k!, a factorial identity valid through k=n. This proves the exact finite result. The approximation Dₙ≈n!/e is an optional later result and is not needed to count a small exchange.

Where the rule fails. Subtracting n copies of (n−1)! from n! alone fails because an assignment with two owners receiving their own gifts was removed twice. For n=3 that first subtraction gives zero, even though the two cyclic assignments exist. Also, D₁=0: one person cannot avoid their own gift. The formula gives 1!−1=0. A proposed shortcut of dividing n! by two has no stable justification; for n=4 it predicts 12 instead of the exact nine.

Worked example: five owners, five gifts

For five participants, the signed ledger is 120 − 120 + 60 − 20 + 5 − 1 = 44. The term 60 is C(5,2)·3!: choose two owners required to receive their own gifts, then freely assign the three remaining gifts. Those remaining assignments may have further fixed points. That is intentional, because this is an intersection term rather than a final answer.

Consider the assignment that gives everyone their own gift. It appears in every required-fixed-point intersection. Its total signed contribution is 1 − 5 + 10 − 10 + 5 − 1 = 0, so it is excluded exactly. A genuine derangement belongs to none of the forbidden events and contributes only its initial 1. The numerical answer 44 is supported by these per-assignment weights, rather than by assuming the successive subtractions remove disjoint groups.

Try two counts

Exercise 1. Compute D₄ using the intersection ledger, naming the count of assignments that fix each chosen k-person set.

Hint for exercise 1

The intersection for a specified k-set has (4−k)! assignments; there are C(4,k) such sets.

Full solution to exercise 1

The terms are k=0: 4!=24; k=1: −C(4,1)3!=−24; k=2: +C(4,2)2!=+12; k=3: −C(4,3)1!=−4; k=4: +C(4,4)0!=+1. Their sum is 9.

Exercise 2. Explain why fixing two named people in a five-person exchange leaves 3! assignments, and why this does not yet count derangements.

Hint for exercise 2

Once two matches are required, how many gifts and recipients remain? Can any of those remaining people still receive their own gift?

Full solution to exercise 2

After the two specified people take their own gifts, three people and three gifts remain, giving 3!=6 unconstrained assignments. Some of those six contain additional fixed points. Thus six is an intersection size used within inclusion–exclusion, not a derangement count. The later signed terms correct these additional overlaps.

What to carry forward

Derangements count permutations avoiding all named self-matches. Inclusion–exclusion works because an assignment with t violations has signed weight (1−1)^t, zero unless it has no violation. The resulting finite sum is exact and includes small boundary cases. Before adapting it to another exchange rule, verify that each forbidden event and every intersection have the stated sizes.
