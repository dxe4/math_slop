Counting choices

Begin by naming one complete outcome. If you choose one of three drinks and then one of two snacks, an outcome is a drink–snack pair. Each drink has two snack choices, so there are 3×2=6 pairs. This counts combinations of choices; it does not require a probability model.

Worked example. Arrange three distinct postcards in a row. There are three choices for the first place, two remaining choices for the second, and one for the last. The result is 3×2×1=6 arrangements, written 3!.

3!=3×2×1=6

For a positive integer n, n! multiplies every integer from 1 through n. The convention 0!=1 counts the single empty arrangement. Factorials count arrangements of distinct objects when each is used exactly once.

Try one. How many two-letter codes can you make from A, B and C when repetition is forbidden?

Worked answer

Choose the first letter in three ways and the second in two ways, giving six codes: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA and CB. If repetition were allowed, the second position would have three choices too, giving nine.

Watch the rule. If different first choices allow different numbers of second choices, count those branches separately and add. A single fixed product needs the stated number of options at every stage.

The question and needed ideas

A board game asks you to roll a blue die and a yellow die. You win if their total is seven. What exactly can happen before we call anything a “chance”? The first job is to choose an outcome description precise enough to settle the win condition. Recording the two colors in order works: (blue result, yellow result).

Background capsule. A sample space, written Ω, is the set of all elementary outcomes in a specified experiment. An event is a subset of Ω. Here (2,5) is one outcome, whereas “total seven” is an event containing several outcomes. The bars |A| mean the number of members of A. Choosing a coarser description such as “the sum” is possible, but its members are generally not equally likely.

Work through the model

Worked example. For two fair, independent six-sided dice, Ω contains all 36 ordered pairs (i,j) with i and j each from 1 through 6. Every pair has chance 1/36: the blue die has six equally likely faces, and each yellow result has chance 1/6 regardless of blue. The winning event A contains (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), and (6,1). It has six members, giving P(A)=6/36=1/6.

Here is the full grid: rows are blue results and columns yellow results. Each cell shows the total; a tick marks a winning pair. For seven, the winning cells form a descending diagonal. “Blue 1, yellow 6” is only one of six winning paths. A sum of seven has six pairs producing it, while a sum of two has just (1,1).

Before changing the target to eight, predict whether more or fewer cells will match. Then count them. You can also switch the question to doubles or to “blue is greater than yellow.” The 36 possible pairs stay the same; the event being counted changes.

The statement and its limits

Read the counting rule in words

Ω is the Greek letter omega, used here as a name for all possible outcomes. A names the event we want. The bars in |A| count its members; here they do not mean an absolute value. P(A) means “the probability of event A.” So the fraction says “number of matching outcomes divided by number of possible outcomes.” The equal-likelihood assumption is what makes counting enough.

Exact statement. If Ω is finite and nonempty, and every elementary outcome has the same probability, then an event A has probability |A|/|Ω|. The disjoint elementary outcomes in A each contribute 1/|Ω|, so adding |A| identical contributions proves the formula. Independence of the dice is one route to equal pair probabilities, but the counting rule itself assumes equal pair probabilities directly.

The total probabilities add to one because Ω contains |Ω| such outcomes. A useful check is P(Ω)=|Ω|/|Ω|=1 and P(∅)=0, where ∅ is the empty event with no outcomes. Partitioning Ω into A and its complement (everything outside A) also gives P(not A)=1−P(A), since their counts add to |Ω|.

Now consider a bag with two red tokens and one blue token. If you treat “red” and “blue” as two equally likely elementary outcomes, you get the wrong answer 1/2. Label the physical tokens R1, R2 and B; these three have equal draw chances, and the red event contains two of them. Alternatively use the two color outcomes with unequal probabilities 2/3 and 1/3. The mathematical description must match the random procedure.

P(A)=|A||Ω|

Try two cases

Exercise 1. Roll two fair, independent colored dice. What is the chance that the first result is greater than the second?

Hint for exercise 1

Count by the first die: how many smaller second results follow each first value?

Full solution to exercise 1

For first values 1 through 6, the counts are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Their sum is 15. Each of 36 ordered pairs has chance 1/36, so the event probability is 15/36=5/12. The six ties and 15 reverse-order pairs account for the remaining 21.

Exercise 2. An ideal spinner lands uniformly by angle and has three sectors with angles 180°, 90°, and 90°. Is choosing one of three sector names uniformly a valid model? Find the chance of the largest sector.

Hint for exercise 2

Compare angular sizes with the full 360° turn.

Full solution to exercise 2

No. If the spinner lands uniformly by angle, sector names have probabilities 180/360, 90/360, and 90/360. The largest sector has probability 1/2. A three-name equiprobable sample space would incorrectly give 1/3 because the physical sectors have unequal size.

What to carry forward

The usable stopping point is to name Ω, name A as a subset, and justify equal likelihood before dividing counts. This lesson proves a finite counting rule, not a rule that every imaginable list of outcomes is uniform. The biased spinner is the failure case. Next, combine overlapping events without counting their shared outcomes twice.
