How many times does a line meet a circle?

A line may appear to cross a circle twice, touch once, or miss it. Exact algebra distinguishes these cases and reveals something a real picture can hide: the missed intersections can still have complex coordinates. We will solve one family completely, without invoking a general curve-counting theorem.

Background capsule. A point lies on both curves only if it satisfies both equations. Substitution replaces one variable using the line equation. A real quadratic at²+bt+c=0 with a≠0 has discriminant Δ=b²−4ac: positive means two distinct real roots, zero one repeated real root, and negative no real roots. In the complex numbers, a square root of a negative real exists because i²=−1, so the same quadratic still has roots counted with repetition.

Move a horizontal line

Worked example. Intersect the unit circle x²+y²=1 with y=t, where t is real. Substitution gives x²=1−t². If t=0, the two points are (−1,0) and (1,0). If t=1, the equation becomes x²=0, so there is one geometric point (0,1), reached by two coinciding roots. If t=2, then x²=−3; there is no real meeting, but over complex coordinates x=±i√3 gives two solutions (±i√3,2).

Substitution and the discriminant

Exact result for this family. For a real t, the points common to y=t and x²+y²=1 obey

x2−(1−t2)=0,Δ=4(1−t2)

Derivation: replace y with t in the circle equation, then move 1−t² to the left. Here a=1, b=0, c=t²−1, hence Δ=0²−4(1)(t²−1)=4(1−t²). If |t|<1, the roots ±√(1−t²) are distinct real numbers. If |t|=1, both collapse to zero. If |t|>1, 1−t² is negative, so neither root is real. This accounts for every real t and every intersection in the affine coordinate plane; substitution is reversible because each candidate also has y=t.

For a nonhorizontal line y=mx+b, the same method yields (1+m²)x²+2mbx+(b²−1)=0. Its leading coefficient is positive for real m. The discriminant is 4(1+m²−b²), after cancellation of 4m²b². Thus there are two, one or zero real intersection points according as b² is less than, equal to or greater than 1+m². The line may be slanted, but its closest distance to the origin is |b|/√(1+m²), which tells the same story geometrically. The algebra proves the root count; the distance interpretation is a cross-check.

Counting a double root as two is a special-case multiplicity convention justified here by the substituted quadratic. It suggests, but does not prove, the general intersection multiplicity used in Bézout's theorem. That theorem also requires projective coordinates and an algebraically closed field. We will state its full hypotheses in the counting lesson; this elementary calculation cannot establish it.

Find and classify meetings

1. Calculation. Intersect x²+y²=1 with y=x. Give every real point and explain why there are no others.

Hint for question 1

Replace y by x; solve 2x²=1 and restore y.

Full solution for question 1

Substitution gives 2x²=1, hence x=±1/√2. Since y=x, the points are (1/√2,1/√2) and (−1/√2,−1/√2). Conversely both satisfy y=x and their squared coordinates sum to 1. The quadratic has only these two roots, so there are no further affine intersections.

2. Explanation. For the line y=−1, how many distinct real points and how many roots counted with quadratic multiplicity? Why are the answers different?

Hint for question 2

Substitute y=−1 and factor the resulting polynomial in x.

Full solution for question 2

The circle equation becomes x²+1=1, or x²=0. Its only coordinate is x=0, giving one point (0,−1). The polynomial is x·x, so x=0 is a root of multiplicity two. The line is tangent there; two roots have merged into one geometric point.

What we have counted

Substitution gives an exact answer for line-circle intersections, and the discriminant sorts the real cases. A repeated root records tangency in this family, and complex roots can persist when no real point is visible. Stop once you can solve and check both equations. Do not use this example alone to claim that every pair of curves has a degree-product count; shared components and points at infinity require a broader framework.
