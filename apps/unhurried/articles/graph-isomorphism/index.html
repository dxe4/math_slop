Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Are these two diagrams the same network?

A social network can be redrawn with people in new positions and with different labels. If every relationship is preserved, it is the same abstract graph. Deciding this requires more than checking that the pictures look alike; one must exhibit a vertex correspondence that preserves edges and nonedges.

Background capsule. A finite simple graph has a vertex set and unordered edge pairs. A function f from V(G) to V(H) assigns each vertex of G one vertex of H. It is a bijection when each vertex of H is used exactly once. The degree of a vertex counts its neighbors. An isomorphism is a bijection that carries adjacency in both directions: u and v are adjacent in G exactly when f(u) and f(v) are adjacent in H. Degrees are therefore preserved, but matching degrees alone may not settle the question.

Let G have vertices A,B,C,D and edges AB,BC,CD,DA. Let H have vertices 1,2,3,4 and edges 12,24,43,31. The drawings could place their vertices very differently. Are they the same four-cycle under relabelling?

Exhibit the map and check every pair

Worked example. Try f(A)=1, f(B)=2, f(C)=4, f(D)=3. Then AB maps to 12, BC to 24, CD to 43, and DA to 31, precisely H's four edges. The map uses all four H vertices once, so it is bijective. Because both graphs have four edges, sending the four distinct edges of G onto H's entire edge set also assures that no nonedge of G can map to an edge of H. We can check the two nonedge pairs directly: AC maps to 14, absent in H; BD maps to 23, also absent. Thus the correspondence is an isomorphism.

Moving vertices in a drawing or bending an edge does not change this pair audit. Adding a vertex at a crossing, however, changes the vertex and edge sets unless it was already a named junction. Isomorphism concerns the abstract pair structure, not the style of the sketch.

What a certificate must preserve

Definition and test. Graphs G and H are isomorphic when there is a bijection f:V(G)→V(H) satisfying

{u,v}∈EG⇔{f(u),f(v)}∈EH.

Why degrees must match. Fix u in G. The isomorphism maps each neighbor v of u to a neighbor f(v) of f(u), and the inverse bijection maps every neighbor of f(u) back to a neighbor of u. Thus it gives a one-to-one correspondence between the two neighbor sets, so deg(u)=deg(f(u)). Consequently the sorted degree lists of isomorphic graphs agree. This is a complete necessary-condition proof and gives a fast way to reject many proposed isomorphisms.

Failure of the converse. A six-cycle C₆ and two disjoint triangles each have six vertices, six edges, and every vertex has degree two. Their degree lists are identical. But C₆ is connected, whereas the two triangles form two components. An isomorphism would map any path in one graph to a path in the other, so it preserves connectedness. Therefore these graphs are not isomorphic. Matching degrees is necessary, not sufficient. The same example shows edge count alone is even weaker.

For a larger finite graph, finding f may take systematic search. First compare counts, degree lists, and connected components; if these agree, propose an explicit bijection and verify every unordered pair, or use adjacency tables under a common vertex order. A successful map is a compact certificate. Failure to find one by casual inspection is not proof of nonisomorphism; a differing invariant is one route to a rigorous rejection.

Give a map or a disproof

Exercise 1. Graph K has edges ab,bc,cd on vertices a,b,c,d. Graph L has edges 13,34,42 on vertices 1,2,3,4. Give an isomorphism and check its edges.

Hint for exercise 1

The degree-one endpoints a,d must map to degree-one endpoints in L; trace the path between them.

Full solution to exercise 1

L is path 1–3–4–2. Set f(a)=1, f(b)=3, f(c)=4, f(d)=2. Then ab maps to 13, bc to 34, and cd to 42, exactly L's edges. The four images are distinct and cover L's vertices. Both graphs have three edges; this edge correspondence covers all L edges and hence also preserves nonedges. So f is an isomorphism.

Exercise 2. Two graphs each have five vertices and four edges. One is the path P₅; the other is a star with one center joined to four leaves. Can they be isomorphic?

Hint for exercise 2

Write each sorted degree list.

Full solution to exercise 2

The five-vertex path has degree list 2,2,2,1,1. The star has degree list 4,1,1,1,1. An isomorphism preserves each vertex degree, so the differing lists rule one out. Equal vertex and edge counts alone could not settle the question.

Relabel by relationships, not appearance

An isomorphism is a bijection that preserves both edges and nonedges. An explicit verified map proves sameness; a differing invariant such as degree or connectedness proves difference. Equal degree lists by themselves cannot certify sameness, as the six-cycle and two triangles show. This completes a bounded test for finite graphs; it does not promise a quick visual method for every large instance. For the underlying graph definition, return to graph basics.
