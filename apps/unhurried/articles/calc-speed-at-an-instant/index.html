Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Can a rate belong to one point?

A straight line has one slope. A curved graph changes its steepness as you move along it. To ask for its slope at a particular point, we can first ask a simpler question: what is the slope between that point and a nearby one?

We will answer this exactly for f(x) = x². You need coordinate pairs and ordinary algebra. In a point (x, y), x is the horizontal coordinate and y is the height. The slope between two points is their change in height divided by their horizontal separation. Positive slope means rising as x increases; negative slope means falling.

This is a signed rate. If the graph describes position against time, its derivative is velocity; physical speed is the absolute value of that velocity. Nothing in the mathematics below requires our chosen horizontal coordinate to be positive.

Bring the second point closer

Worked example. Choose the point with horizontal coordinate a = 1. Its height is 1. A second point with coordinate 1 + h has height (1 + h)². Here h is a nonzero horizontal step. The line joining the two points is called a secant.

For h = 0.5, the second point is (1.5, 2.25), so the secant slope is (2.25 − 1)/0.5 = 2.5. A smaller step gives a slope closer to 2:

The optional lab changes a between −2 and 2 and the positive step h between 0.01 and 1. Try a = −1: the slope is −2 + h and approaches −2. The table and this identity give the same experiment without moving a control. The lab only shows positive steps; the proof below also covers negative ones.

Read the tangent prediction carefully. At a=−1 the tangent is y=1−2(x+1). Moving to x=−0.9 gives predicted height 0.8, while the actual square is 0.81. The decrease in height is compatible with a negative derivative, and the error is the positive square (0.1)². Taking h=−0.1 instead gives tangent height 1.2 and actual height 1.21. In both directions the tangent lies below the parabola, although the signed height changes have opposite signs. This separates three quantities that are easy to confuse: the secant slope, the derivative, and the vertical error of a tangent approximation.

The derivative, with its justification

Exact statement. For every real number a, the derivative of f(x) = x² at a exists and equals 2a. The derivative means the limit of the secant slope as the nonzero step tends to zero:

f′(a)=limh→0(a+h)2−a2h=2a

Proof. Expand the numerator: (a + h)² − a² = 2ah + h². Because h ≠ 0, division gives the exact identity (2ah + h²)/h = 2a + h. The difference between the secant slope and 2a is therefore h, whose absolute value is |h|.

For any requested positive tolerance ε, restricting the step to 0 < |h| < ε makes the slope differ from 2a by less than ε. This works whether h is positive or negative. That explicit guarantee proves the limit; the pattern in a finite table alone would not.

We never divide by zero. We divide only for nonzero h, simplify, and then ask what happens as h approaches zero. The tangent line at a is y = a² + 2a(x − a). Its slope is the derivative. The actual curve differs from this line by exactly (x − a)², which explains why the line becomes such a good local approximation.

Two small investigations

1. Calculate. At a = 2, find the secant slope for h = 0.25. Find the derivative and the tangent-line height at x = 2.25.

Hint

Use 2a + h for the secant and a² + 2a(x − a) for the tangent.

Worked solution

The secant slope is 4 + 0.25 = 4.25. The derivative is 4. The tangent height is 4 + 4(0.25) = 5. The curve's height is 2.25² = 5.0625, exceeding the tangent by 0.0625 = 0.25².

2. Explain. Someone sees secant slopes approaching 1 from the right for f(x) = |x| at zero and declares its derivative to be 1. What is missing?

Hint

At zero the quotient is |h|/h. Check a negative h.

Worked solution

Positive h gives 1, but negative h gives −1. There is no single number approached from both sides, so the ordinary two-sided derivative does not exist at zero. A right-sided experiment alone cannot settle the question.

A complete idea to keep

For a parabola, nearby average rates equal 2a + h, and their limiting rate is 2a. We established this with algebra and an explicit error bound. The corner example shows the boundary of the idea: a continuous graph need not have a derivative everywhere. An optional next question is how accumulating many small areas relates to a changing rate.
