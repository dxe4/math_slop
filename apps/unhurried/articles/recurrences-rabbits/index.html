Counting choices

Begin by naming one complete outcome. If you choose one of three drinks and then one of two snacks, an outcome is a drink–snack pair. Each drink has two snack choices, so there are 3×2=6 pairs. This counts combinations of choices; it does not require a probability model.

Worked example. Arrange three distinct postcards in a row. There are three choices for the first place, two remaining choices for the second, and one for the last. The result is 3×2×1=6 arrangements, written 3!.

3!=3×2×1=6

For a positive integer n, n! multiplies every integer from 1 through n. The convention 0!=1 counts the single empty arrangement. Factorials count arrangements of distinct objects when each is used exactly once.

Try one. How many two-letter codes can you make from A, B and C when repetition is forbidden?

Worked answer

Choose the first letter in three ways and the second in two ways, giving six codes: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA and CB. If repetition were allowed, the second position would have three choices too, giving nine.

Watch the rule. If different first choices allow different numbers of second choices, count those branches separately and add. A single fixed product needs the stated number of options at every stage.

A staircase can be climbed in one- or two-step moves.

A stair has five risers. A climb can be 1+1+1+1+1, 2+2+1, 1+2+2, and several others. How many move sequences reach the top exactly? The order of moves matters: 1+2+2 and 2+1+2 describe different paths through intermediate positions. Rather than list all paths for every height, classify a path by its final move. The final move must be one step from the previous riser or two steps from the riser before that.

Prerequisite capsule. A recurrence defines later values from earlier ones. It requires initial values: a rule such as aₙ=aₙ₋₁+aₙ₋₂ alone does not determine a sequence. An addition principle says that if a collection splits into disjoint cases, its size is the sum of their sizes. We will count paths up a staircase, where each move is exactly one or two steps.

Count a concrete case

Worked example. Let aₙ count allowed move sequences that climb exactly n risers. For n=0, the empty sequence does the job once, so a₀=1. For n=1, only the single one-step move works, so a₁=1. For n≥2, paths ending in 1 correspond uniquely to paths up n−1 risers: remove or append that final 1. Similarly, paths ending in 2 correspond uniquely to paths up n−2 risers. The two classes are disjoint because their last moves differ and exhaustive because no other move is allowed. Thus aₙ=aₙ₋₁+aₙ₋₂. The values are 1,1,2,3,5,8 for n=0 through 5, so there are eight ways up five risers. Listing the five-riser paths gives 11111, 1112, 1121, 1211, 2111, 122, 212, 221.

The exact rule and its proof

a0=1,a1=1,an=an−1+an−2

Staircase recurrence. Under the stated move rules, the displayed initial conditions and equation hold for every integer n≥2. The remove-last-move correspondence above is a bijective proof: each shorter path produces one distinct longer path in its case, and every longer path is recovered from exactly one shorter path. No experiment on the first five heights proves the recurrence; the last-move classification works for arbitrary n. These are Fibonacci numbers with a shifted index if the familiar convention sets F₀=0 and F₁=1: aₙ=Fₙ₊₁. The index shift matters, especially at n=0. The recurrence is a counting model of legal stair paths, not a literal population model of rabbits.

Where the rule fails. If a climb may include a three-step move, aₙ₋₁+aₙ₋₂ misses all paths ending in 3. At n=3, the original rule gives three paths—111,12,21—while the expanded rule has a fourth path, 3. A recurrence is valid only when its cases exhaust the allowed objects. Likewise, setting a₀=0 because “no steps means no climb” deletes the empty sequence and breaks later counts: the single two-step climb needs a path of length zero before appending its move.

Worked example: counting the paths that use two long moves

Among climbs of five risers using moves 1 or 2, how many use exactly two moves of size 2? The two long moves contribute four risers, so one move of size 1 supplies the remaining riser. Thus there are three moves in total. Choosing which of their three positions holds the 1 gives 122, 212, and 221: three paths.

The other five paths have either no 2, giving 11111, or one 2 together with three 1s. In the latter case there are four possible positions for the 2. The total is therefore 1 + 4 + 3 = 8, agreeing with the recurrence. This second classification sorts by number of long moves rather than final move. Both count the same legal sequences; their agreement helps check our interpretation of order and exact arrival at the top.

Try two counts

Exercise 1. Count paths up six risers and explain the split by final move rather than merely reading a sequence.

Hint for exercise 1

Use the proved counts for heights five and four. What are their values?

Full solution to exercise 1

The paths ending with a 1 are in bijection with the eight paths up five risers; those ending with a 2 are in bijection with the five paths up four risers. These disjoint groups total a₆=8+5=13.

Exercise 2. Suppose moves of one or three risers are allowed instead. State initial values a₀,a₁,a₂,a₃ and the recurrence for n≥3.

Hint for exercise 2

List short paths, including the empty path. For a general long path, remove its last 1 or its last 3.

Full solution to exercise 2

There is one empty path, one way to climb one riser (1), one way to climb two (11), and two ways to climb three (111 or 3). Hence a₀=1,a₁=1,a₂=1,a₃=2. For n≥3, deleting the last allowed move gives aₙ=aₙ₋₁+aₙ₋₃. At n=3 this yields a₂+a₀=1+1=2, consistent with the list.

What to carry forward

A recurrence is a compressed count supported by a disjoint decomposition and sufficient initial values. The staircase model shows how to prove the rule with remove-and-append bijections. When rules change, redo the case split and inspect the empty case. The first computed terms are useful checks, but the arbitrary-height correspondence is the proof.
