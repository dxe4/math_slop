Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

A square grid under a square

Take every point of a drawn grid and replace its complex coordinate z by z². Straight grid lines curve, two points often land together, and one point behaves exceptionally. We can predict all three effects without trusting a picture.

Background capsule. Write z=x+iy, with real x,y and i²=−1. Its modulus is |z|=√(x²+y²). For nonzero z, polar form is z=r(cos θ+i sin θ), where r=|z| and θ is an angle measured modulo 2π. Multiplying nonzero complex numbers multiplies lengths and adds angles. The origin has modulus zero and no angle.

Track four points and one line

Worked example. Square 1+i: (1+i)²=1+2i−1=2i. Square −1−i and obtain the same 2i. Squaring 1−i gives −2i; squaring 2+i gives 3+4i. The pairs ±z coincide because (−z)²=z². More precisely, if z²=w² then (z−w)(z+w)=0. Complex numbers have no zero divisors, so w=z or w=−z. Conversely, any nonzero output s(cos φ+i sin φ) has inputs √s at angles φ/2 and φ/2+π. Thus the map is exactly two-to-one onto the nonzero plane.

For a vertical grid line x=c, set z=c+iy. Its image is (c²−y²)+2ci. If c≠0 and image coordinates are U,V, then y=V/(2c), giving U=c²−V²/(4c²), a left-opening parabola. If c=0, the whole imaginary axis maps to the nonpositive real axis, traversed twice. This degenerate line shows why the center deserves separate attention.

Compare two different notions of size. For a fixed multiplier w, distances between outputs scale by |w|. In squaring, however, the multiplier is the input itself. The points 1 and 2 are one unit apart, while their squares 1 and 4 are three units apart; the points −1 and 1 are two units apart but have identical squares. The local derivative at a chosen point describes small nearby differences, while these finite comparisons expose the map's changing scale and global folding. Neither a constant global stretch factor nor a global inverse follows from local angle preservation.

Double the angle, square the radius

Distributing (x+iy)² gives x²−y²+2xyi. The polar rule gives a second, equivalent statement for r>0:

(reiθ)2=r2e2iθ

Here eiθ abbreviates cos θ+i sin θ; the formula follows from the angle-addition identities, so it does not require an unproved exponential theory. The radius r becomes r². A point at θ and one at θ+π both land at angle 2θ modulo 2π.

Local angle claim. At a nonzero input z, a small displacement h changes the output by (z+h)²−z²=2zh+h². Divide by h: the complex derivative is 2z. Multiplication by nonzero 2z rotates and scales small displacements. The remaining term h² is of smaller order than h because |h²|/|h|=|h|→0. This proves the first-order shape claim at each z≠0; it does not say finite grid cells remain exact squares or that a globally chosen inverse exists.

At z=0 the derivative is zero. Every first-order displacement collapses there, so the local angle argument cannot use a nonzero rotation. Curves passing through zero can meet differently after mapping. The map is still differentiable at zero, but “conformal” in the angle-preserving local sense requires a nonzero derivative. There is no contradiction between differentiability and failure of this stronger condition.

Calculate and challenge the picture

1. Calculate. Find the two solutions of z²=−3+4i and verify both in Cartesian form.

Hint for 1

Try a+bi with a²−b²=−3 and 2ab=4. Small integers work.

Worked solution for 1

Take a=1 and b=2: a²−b²=1−4=−3 and 2ab=4. Thus (1+2i)²=−3+4i. The other solution is −1−2i. The factorization argument proves there are no further solutions.

2. Explain. A sketch seems to show squaring preserves angles everywhere because it maps rays to rays. Find the flaw at zero and state a correct version.

Hint for 2

Test the two rays with angles 0 and π/2, then compare their images.

Worked solution for 2

At zero the positive real ray and positive imaginary ray meet at a right angle; their images are respectively the positive and negative real rays, which meet at a straight angle. The correct local statement applies at a point z≠0: its derivative 2z is a nonzero rotation and scale. The special point zero cannot be included.

What this map really promises

Squaring doubles angle and squares radius, while pairing z with −z. It preserves infinitesimal angles away from zero but folds the entire plane. In particular, a global square-root choice requires a domain restriction; the logarithm has a related branch problem. A finite drawing can suggest these facts, while the algebra supplies the exhaustive two-to-one claim.
