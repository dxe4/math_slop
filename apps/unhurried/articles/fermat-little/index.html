Why does a power come back to one?

Compute powers of 2 modulo 7: 2, 4, 1, 2, 4, 1, and so on. In particular 26 leaves remainder 1. This pattern is part of a theorem about every prime modulus, though six is not the first returning exponent for 2. The theorem says that for a prime p and an integer a not divisible by p, ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). It does not identify the shortest cycle for each a.

Here is the local background. A prime p>1 has only positive divisors 1 and p. Congruence modulo p means that p divides a difference. A nonzero residue is one of 1 through p−1. If p does not divide a, then gcd(a,p)=1 and multiplication by a has an inverse modulo p: Bézout gives au+pv=1. We will use that fact to prove multiplication by a rearranges, or permutes, the nonzero residues. No infinite sequence experiment is required.

Watch a permutation on six residues

Worked example. Let p=7 and a=3. Multiply each nonzero residue by 3 and reduce modulo 7. The list 1,2,3,4,5,6 becomes 3,6,2,5,1,4. Every number from 1 to 6 appears exactly once, in another order. The product of the original list is 720. The product of the new list is also 720, but it is also 36·720 modulo 7. Since 720 ≡ 6 (mod 7) and 6 has an inverse, cancelling it gives 36 ≡ 1 (mod 7).

Permute, multiply, cancel

Fermat's little theorem. If p is prime and p does not divide a, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Because p is prime and does not divide a, gcd(a,p)=1; hence a has an inverse modulo p. If ar ≡ as (mod p), multiply by that inverse to obtain r ≡ s (mod p). Thus a·1, a·2, …, a·(p−1) are distinct nonzero residues. There are p−1 of them, so they are exactly the nonzero residues in some order. Multiplying these congruences gives the same product on both sides:

ap−1(p−1)!≡(p−1)!(modp)

Here (p−1)! means 1·2·…·(p−1). None of those factors is divisible by p, so their product is coprime to p and has an inverse modulo p. Cancel it to obtain the theorem. This is a complete proof given the elementary inverse criterion, which follows from Bézout. The primality hypothesis matters at two points: it ensures each nonzero residue has an inverse and allows cancellation of the product.

Failure case. Replacing “prime” by “any integer” fails. With modulus 8 and a=3, gcd(3,8)=1, yet 37 ≡ 3 (mod 8), since powers of 3 alternate between 3 and 1. The exponent m−1 is not generally valid for composite m. The converse also fails as a primality test: 2340 ≡ 1 (mod 341) even though 341=11·31. The latter numerical fact can be checked by repeated squaring; it illustrates why a passing Fermat-style test alone does not certify primality.

Use the theorem and test its limits

1. Compute 5101 modulo 13 without multiplying 101 copies of 5. State why the theorem applies.

Hint for question 1

13 is prime, so group the exponent into multiples of 12 and a remainder.

Full solution to question 1

Because 13 is prime and 13 does not divide 5, Fermat gives 512 ≡ 1 (mod 13). Since 101=8·12+5, 5101 ≡ 55. Now 5²=25 ≡ −1 (mod 13), so 5⁴ ≡ 1 and 5⁵ ≡ 5. The requested residue is 5. The theorem reduces the exponent; the final small power is calculated separately.

2. A student argues that 710 ≡ 1 (mod 11) proves 11 is prime. Identify the logical error and test 37 modulo 8.

Hint for question 2

Distinguish a theorem's “if” direction from its converse; then square 3 modulo 8.

Full solution to question 2

The theorem says prime modulus implies the congruence for eligible bases; one successful congruence cannot reverse that implication. Independent divisibility checks show 11 is prime, but the proposed proof does not. Modulo 8, 3²=9 ≡ 1, so 37=(3²)³·3 ≡ 3, not 1. This composite modulus directly fails the naive replacement of p by m.

The permutation is the proof

For prime p, multiplying nonzero residues by a number not divisible by p rearranges them. Multiplying and cancelling that rearranged list proves ap−1 ≡ 1. The theorem is a reliable exponent reduction under its hypotheses, not a complete primality test and not a promise of the shortest period. Repeated squaring makes large residual powers efficient to compute.
