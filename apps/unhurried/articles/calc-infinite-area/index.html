Can a region stretch forever and still have finite area?

The region under y=1/x² from x=1 to the right never ends. Nevertheless, each successive stretch contributes less area. The word “infinite” describes its horizontal extent, not automatically its area. An improper integral answers the area question by first stopping at a finite cutoff and then taking a limit.

Background capsule. For finite R>1, ordinary integration gives a number ∫ from 1 to R of f(x)dx. We define ∫ from 1 to infinity of f(x)dx as the limit of those numbers as R→∞, if the limit is finite. If no finite limit exists, the improper integral diverges. For p≠1, an antiderivative of x−p is x1−p/(1−p); for p=1 it is ln x. The logarithm grows without bound as x→∞.

Watch the cutoff move

Worked example. For p=2, ∫₁ᴿx−2dx=[−1/x]₁ᴿ=1−1/R. At R=2 the area is 1/2; at R=10 it is 9/10; at R=100 it is 99/100. Each finite area is less than 1, and the missing tail is exactly 1/R. The limiting area is 1.

By contrast, for y=1/x, the area through R is ln R. It keeps increasing with no finite ceiling, though very slowly. A plot through x=100 may make the two curves look similarly close to zero. Their total areas behave differently, so a finite window cannot settle convergence.

Choose a cutoff for a requested accuracy. For the worked p=2 curve, stopping at R gives 1−1/R, so an absolute tail below 0.001 requires R>1000. This is an exact certificate even though we never draw the infinite remainder. For p=3/2 the tail is 2/√R; the same requirement instead needs R>4,000,000. Both integrals converge, yet their tails decay at very different rates. The yes-or-no convergence classification does not by itself tell us how much finite computation is needed for a desired accuracy.

The p threshold

Statement. For real p, the improper integral ∫ from 1 to infinity of x−pdx converges exactly when p>1, and then equals 1/(p−1). For p≤1 it diverges to positive infinity. These claims concern this nonnegative power family on this interval.

∫1∞x−pdx=1p−1when p>1

Proof. For p≠1, integrate only to finite R: ∫₁ᴿx−pdx=(R1−p−1)/(1−p). If p>1, then 1−p<0, so R1−p=1/Rp−1 tends to zero. The limit is 1/(p−1). If p<1, the positive power R1−p grows without bound; division by the positive 1−p preserves that divergence. At p=1 the finite expression is ln R, which also tends to infinity. The exceptional logarithm case cannot be obtained by substituting p=1 into the power antiderivative, where its denominator vanishes.

Why is the limit a legitimate definition? Every finite cutoff includes only a bounded interval where the integrand is continuous and ordinarily integrable. The infinite interval itself is not an ordinary Riemann interval. Taking the cutoff limit is an additional operation, and its existence must be checked. For nonnegative x−p, finite areas increase with R, so either they approach a finite ceiling or grow without bound; oscillatory signed examples require more care.

Boundary. A function can approach zero and still have infinite area: 1/x is the counterexample. The size of individual heights does not answer the accumulation question. Conversely, convergence of this area does not imply that the region has finite horizontal length; its x-range remains unbounded.

Classify before calculating

1. Calculate. Find ∫ from 1 to infinity of x−3/2dx, and find the area beyond R=16.

Hint for 1

Here p=3/2. Use the finite cutoff formula to isolate the tail.

Worked solution for 1

The total is 1/(3/2−1)=2. The area from 1 to R is 2(1−R−1/2), so the remaining tail is 2R−1/2. At R=16 it is 2/4=1/2; the area through 16 is therefore 3/2.

2. Explain. Does ∫ from 1 to infinity of 1/√x converge because its integrand tends to zero?

Hint for 2

Identify p in x−p and compute through finite R.

Worked solution for 2

No. Here p=1/2≤1. Its finite integral is [2√x]₁ᴿ=2√R−2, which grows without bound as R→∞. The integrand tends to zero, but the shrinking is too slow for finite accumulated area.

Infinity enters only through a limit

The p-test threshold follows from a finite antiderivative and a cutoff limit. Convergence means the total area approaches a finite value; it does not mean the curve reaches zero or the region stops. Whenever you see an infinite endpoint, replace it by R, calculate, then justify the limit. A later course may compare less elementary tails with this family.
