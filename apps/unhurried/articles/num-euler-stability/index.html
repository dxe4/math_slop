A decaying model, a growing calculation

The equation y′=−λy describes exponential decay when λ>0. Yet a numerical method can turn that decay into alternating growth if its time step is too large. This apparent contradiction is not a failure of the differential equation: the discrete update has its own dynamics. Forward Euler makes the distinction especially clear because both the exact solution and the computed sequence have simple formulas.

Background capsule. An ordinary differential equation specifies a derivative; y′ is the rate of change of y with time. For y′=−λy and y(0)=y₀, the exact solution is y(t)=y₀e^(−λt). A geometric sequence y_n=qⁿy₀ tends to zero for every finite y₀ exactly when |q|<1. A step h>0 places grid time t_n=nh. We first use exact arithmetic to isolate method stability.

Try three time steps

Worked example. Set λ=2, y₀=1. Forward Euler uses the current slope −2y_n over one step: y_{n+1}=y_n−2hy_n=(1−2h)y_n. At h=0.25 the factor is 0.5, so values are 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125: positive decay. At h=0.75 the factor is −0.5, giving 1, −0.5, 0.25, −0.125: decay in magnitude with alternating signs, even though the exact solution remains positive. At h=1.25 the factor is −1.5; values 1, −1.5, 2.25, −3.375 grow in magnitude while e^(−2t) decreases.

Derive the stability interval

yn+1=(1−hλ)yn,|1−hλ|<1

Euler replaces a short interval of the solution curve by a tangent step: y_{n+1}=y_n+h(−λy_n). By induction, y_n=(1−hλ)ⁿy₀. For nonzero y₀, decay of this geometric sequence to zero is equivalent to |1−hλ|<1. With real λ,h>0, that inequality is equivalent to 0<hλ<2. At hλ=2, values alternate without shrinking; at hλ>2, their magnitudes grow. At hλ=1, the numerical value reaches zero after one step, which is stable but generally inaccurate at that time.

For fixed final time T and h=T/n tending to zero, (1−λT/n)ⁿ tends to e^−λT. This gives convergence for this scalar problem in exact arithmetic as the step is refined. Stability at one fixed h and convergence as h→0 are different questions. Local truncation error is of order h² per step for smooth solutions, yielding first-order global accuracy over fixed T under suitable bounds. That accuracy claim does not mean a large stable step is acceptable for a requested tolerance.

Finite precision introduces another layer: repeated multiplication may underflow or accumulate error. The scalar restriction 0<hλ<2 does not automatically cover nonlinear equations, systems with complex eigenvalues, or arbitrary stiff models. A system requires analysis of its update matrix and relevant norms or spectra. The exact exponential decay model is a useful test equation, not a full ODE solver certification.

Separate stability from accuracy

Exercise 1. For y′=−4y, y₀=2, classify h=0.2, h=0.5, and h=0.6 by long-run Euler behavior, and give y₂ for each.

Hint for exercise 1

Compute q=1−4h and y₂=2q².

Solution to exercise 1

At h=0.2, q=0.2 and y₂=2(0.04)=0.08; the sequence decays. At h=0.5, q=−1 and y₂=2; it alternates without decay, the boundary case. At h=0.6, q=−1.4 and y₂=2(1.96)=3.92; its magnitude grows. Exact y(t)=2e^−4t decays for every t≥0.

Exercise 2. A student chooses h=1/λ because Euler then gives zero after one step and calls the result “exact.” Explain the error at t=h and why the method is nevertheless stable for this test equation.

Hint for exercise 2

Compare 0 with y₀e^−1 and inspect the update factor.

Solution to exercise 2

At h=1/λ, q=0, so y₁=0 and all later Euler values are zero. But the exact solution at t=h is y₀e^−1, so the absolute first-step error is |y₀|/e, nonzero unless y₀=0. Since |q|=0<1, the numerical sequence does decay to zero; it is stable in the scalar long-run sense but not exact at finite time.

Check the discrete dynamics

For scalar decay with λ>0, forward Euler’s repeated values decay precisely when 0<hλ<2. Positivity requires the stronger 0<hλ≤1 for nonnegative initial data. Neither property by itself gives a tight finite-time error. A graph of points can reveal trouble, while the factor calculation proves the threshold. Quadrature error gives a contrasting example of a finite-step numerical certificate.
