Why a boundary can remember an interior

Green's theorem turns circulation around a planar boundary into a double integral of local rotation inside. On a rectangle, the identity follows from the one-variable fundamental theorem twice. This proof exposes why the boundary must be counterclockwise and why inner edges cancel when rectangles are tiled.

Background capsule. A field F=(P,Q) contributes P dx+Q dy along a moving path. The scalar curl is Qx−Py. Counterclockwise orientation means the region stays to your left as you walk its outer boundary. A C¹ function has continuous first partial derivatives, allowing us to apply the one-variable fundamental theorem and exchange ordinary integrations on a rectangle.

Walk around a unit square

Worked example. Let P=−y and Q=x on D=[0,1]×[0,1]. Along the bottom edge y=0, travel right: P dx=0. Along the right edge x=1, travel up: Q dy=1 dy, total 1. Along the top edge y=1, travel left from x=1 to 0: P dx=−1 dx, and the reversed x bounds give another 1. Along the left edge x=0, travel down: Q dy=0. Total circulation is 2.

Inside, Qx=1 and Py=−1, so curl is 2 everywhere. The area is 1, giving double integral 2. This match is exact. Clockwise travel would give −2 on the boundary, while the right side would remain +2 unless we also changed the orientation convention. A theorem statement that omits orientation is incomplete.

Follow one shared contribution. Divide the unit square at x=1/2 for the worked field (−y,x). The shared vertical edge contributes ∫₀¹(1/2)dy=1/2 when it is the right edge of the left rectangle. As the left edge of the right rectangle it contributes ∫₁⁰(1/2)dy=−1/2. Their sum is exactly zero, while each rectangle's curl integral is 2 times area 1/2, hence 1. Adding gives the original circulation 2. Cancellation concerns the same geometric edge with opposite traversals; it does not require the field itself to vanish there.

Prove the rectangular case

Take D=[a,b]×[c,d], with P,Q continuously differentiable on an open neighborhood. Bottom and top contributions from P are ∫ₐᵇP(x,c)dx and −∫ₐᵇP(x,d)dx. Together they equal −∫ₐᵇ∫꜀ᵈPy(x,y)dy dx by the fundamental theorem. Right and left contributions from Q are ∫꜀ᵈQ(b,y)dy and −∫꜀ᵈQ(a,y)dy, together ∫꜀ᵈ∫ₐᵇQx(x,y)dx dy. Continuous partials allow both to be written as integrals over D, yielding the formula.

∮∂DPdx+Qdy=∬D(Qx−Py)dA

Proof boundary. We have proved the axis-aligned rectangular case. If two rectangles share an edge, one counterclockwise walk traverses that edge upward and the other downward; their line integrals cancel because reversing a path reverses its integral. This explains a route toward more general regions, but passing to curved boundaries requires approximation and regularity arguments not supplied here. For a standard piecewise smooth planar region and C¹ field on its neighborhood, Green's theorem holds with the correctly oriented boundary; holes add clockwise inner boundaries.

A counterexample to careless use is the vortex field F=(-y/(x²+y²),x/(x²+y²)) on a disk centered at the origin. It has zero curl wherever defined yet nonzero unit-circle circulation because it is undefined inside. The missing smoothness condition blocks Green's theorem on that disk.

Check edges and hypotheses

1. Calculation. On D=[0,2]×[0,1], let P=0 and Q=x². Compute both sides of Green's formula.

Hint for question 1

Only the vertical edges contribute to the line integral.

Worked solution for question 1

On the right edge x=2, Q=4 and upward dy from 0 to 1 contributes 4. On the left x=0, Q=0. The horizontal edges have P=0, so the boundary total is 4. Inside, Qx−Py=2x; integrating ∫₀¹∫₀²2x dx dy gives 4.

2. Explanation. Two adjacent counterclockwise rectangles share a vertical edge. Why does that edge disappear from the boundary integral of their union?

Hint for question 2

Describe the travel direction on each copy of the shared edge.

Worked solution for question 2

The right edge of the left rectangle is walked upward; the left edge of the right rectangle is walked downward. The same field is evaluated at the same points, but dr changes sign, so the two line integrals are negatives and sum to zero. The remaining edges trace the outer boundary.

A proved case and a wider theorem

For a rectangle with a C¹ field, boundary circulation equals integrated scalar curl by two applications of the fundamental theorem. Tiling gives an exact cancellation pattern for interior edges. Stop by checking orientation and whether the field is smooth on the whole filled region. For irregular boundaries or holes, invoke the general theorem only with its geometric and domain hypotheses intact.
