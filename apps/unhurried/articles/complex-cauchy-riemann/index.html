Two coordinate equations for one complex derivative

A complex derivative is a single multiplication that describes the local map from every direction. The real coordinate derivatives cannot vary independently if that is true. The Cauchy–Riemann equations expose the constraint, while a regularity condition tells us when the equations really suffice.

Background capsule. Write f(x+iy)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y), where u and v are real-valued functions. The partial derivative ux changes u with x while y stays fixed; uy changes it with y while x stays fixed. “Continuous first partials near a point” means all four first partial derivatives exist throughout an open neighborhood and vary continuously there. A complex derivative is the limit of [f(z+h)−f(z)]/h over all nonzero complex h→0.

Compare the two axis steps

Worked example. For f(z)=z², u=x²−y² and v=2xy. Consequently ux=2x, vx=2y, uy=−2y, vy=2x. The two requested equalities hold everywhere. The complex derivative computed directly is 2z=2x+2iy, agreeing with ux+ivx.

For f(z)=z̄, u=x and v=−y. Then ux=1 but vy=−1, so the first equality fails; likewise uy=0 and −vx=0. One passing equation is not enough. The real-coordinate mirror is differentiable as a plane map but cannot be represented locally by multiplication by a complex number.

Read the derivative matrix. Under the sufficient hypotheses, put a=ux and b=vx. The real derivative matrix has rows (a,−b) and (b,a), precisely the coordinate rule for multiplying by a+bi. Its determinant is a²+b². When this is positive, the linear approximation preserves orientation and scales every length by √(a²+b²). When both numbers vanish, the derivative is zero and this nonzero rotation-and-scale interpretation ceases to apply. The equations still hold in that exceptional case.

Necessity and the qualified converse

Necessary condition. If f has a complex derivative at an interior point z, then all four indicated partial derivatives at that point exist and satisfy

ux=vyuy=−vx

To prove it, let h=t be real. The quotient tends to ux+ivx. Let h=it; division by i turns its limit into vy−iuy. Both must equal the same f′(z). Equating real and imaginary parts yields the equations. This proves necessity without assuming continuity of partial derivatives.

Sufficient condition with hypotheses. If all four first partials exist and are continuous throughout a neighborhood of z and the two equations hold at z, then f is complex differentiable at z. Real total differentiability follows from the continuous-partials theorem: for h=s+it, the change of f is (ux+ivx)s+(uy+ivy)t+o(|h|). The equations turn the linear part into (ux+ivx)(s+it). Divide the remainder by h to get zero. The cited real total-differentiability theorem is the one ingredient used here without proof.

Equalities at one point alone do not imply a complex derivative. Consider f(0)=0 and, for z=x+iy≠0, f(z)=xy/√(x²+y²), a real-valued function. Along either coordinate axis f vanishes, so all four coordinate partials at zero are zero; both equations hold. Along h=t(1+i), however, f(h)/h has a nonzero constant value depending on the sign of t, so no complex derivative exists. This function lacks the needed total differentiability at zero. It makes the regularity qualification substantive.

Check equations and their scope

1. Calculate. For f(z)=x²−y²+i(2xy+3), verify the equations and determine f′(z).

Hint for 1

The added constant affects neither partial derivatives nor the difference quotient.

Worked solution for 1

Here ux=2x=vy and uy=−2y=−vx. These partials are continuous everywhere, so the qualified converse applies at every point. The derivative is ux+ivx=2x+2iy=2z. Directly, f(z)=z²+3i gives the same result.

2. Explain. Why do equalities at just z=0 fail to settle the example f(z)=xy/√(x²+y²) with f(0)=0? Compute a diagonal quotient.

Hint for 2

Set h=t(1+i) for t>0, find x=y=t, then divide by t(1+i).

Worked solution for 2

For t>0, f(t+it)=t²/(√2t)=t/√2. Thus f(h)/h=1/[√2(1+i)]=(1−i)/(2√2), while along the real axis the quotient is zero. The derivative therefore fails despite the equalities at zero. Continuous partials near zero, which would supply a uniform linear approximation, are absent.

Use both the equations and their premise

Complex differentiability forces ux=vy and uy=−vx. Continuous first partials in a neighborhood make the equations sufficient at a point; if they hold throughout that neighborhood, f is holomorphic there. Bare pointwise equalities need not give a derivative. This precision matters before using contour theorems, which require holomorphy on an open region.
