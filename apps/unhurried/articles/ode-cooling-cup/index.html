Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Powers and logarithms

For a positive integer n, aⁿ means a multiplied by itself n times. For example, 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. For a nonzero base, a⁰=1 and a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. A negative exponent describes a reciprocal.

Worked example. With base 3, the logarithm asks which exponent produces a positive input. Since 3⁴=81, log₃(81)=4. More generally, for a positive base b different from 1, log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with x positive.

34=81log3(81)=4

Multiplying powers with the same base adds their exponents: 2³×2²=2⁵. For positive inputs, this becomes the logarithm rule log₂(uv)=log₂(u)+log₂(v). It is a rule about a product.

Try one. Find log₂(1/8) and explain the negative sign.

Worked answer

Because 2⁻³=1/2³=1/8, the logarithm is −3. For a base larger than 1, inputs between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms.

Watch the operation. Logarithms do not generally turn a sum into a sum: log₂(2+2)=2, while log₂(2)+log₂(2)=2 happens to agree in this case. Using 2+4 instead gives log₂(6), which differs from 1+2=3 because 2³=8.

How can one rate rule draw a whole curve?

A cup at 80°C in a 20°C room cools quickly at first and slowly later. Newton's cooling model says that the rate of change of temperature is proportional to the temperature difference from a constant ambient temperature. Here a prime means differentiation with respect to time: T′(t) is measured in °C per minute, while the constant k has units per minute. The model assumes a well-mixed cup, a fixed room temperature, and an effective heat-transfer coefficient that does not change over the interval being studied.

Local background. The derivative of eat is aeat. An initial condition such as T(0)=80 chooses one curve from a family of possible curves. A negative derivative means decreasing temperature; it does not mean that the temperature itself is negative.

Calculate a prediction

Worked example. Take Ta=20°C, T(0)=80°C and k=0.1 per minute. Write the excess temperature U=T−20. Its starting value is 60°C, and the rule becomes U′=−0.1U. Guess U(t)=60e−0.1t. Differentiating gives U′=−6e−0.1t=−0.1U, so the guess satisfies the equation and the initial value. After ten minutes, T=20+60e−1≈42.1°C. This is a model prediction, not a measurement.

The temperature gap after one time constant 1/k=10 minutes is 1/e of its original size. Its half-life is (ln 2)/k≈6.93 minutes: solving 60e−0.1t=30 gives t=10 ln 2. Neither time depends on the starting gap in this particular model.

Why this curve, and when is it unique?

For constant ambient temperature Ta and k>0, the initial-value problem and its solution are

dTdt=−k(T−Ta),T(0)=T0;T(t)=Ta+(T0−Ta)e−kt

Differentiation verifies the displayed formula. To see why no other differentiable solution with the same initial value exists, multiply U′=−kU by ekt. The product rule gives (ektU)′=0. Thus ektU is constant on an interval, and its initial value fixes that constant as T0−Ta. This is a direct uniqueness proof for this linear equation; it does not assert uniqueness for arbitrary rate rules.

The gap keeps its original sign because an exponential is positive. A cup above room temperature stays above it in the model and approaches it without crossing. The equation can also describe warming from below: then U<0, U′>0, and T rises toward Ta. The equilibrium T=Ta is stable in the precise sense that a small initial temperature error remains no larger later: its magnitude is multiplied by e−kt≤1 for t≥0. It is also attracting because that factor tends to zero.

One can formally separate dU/U=−k dt only where U≠0. The product-rule derivation includes the equilibrium U=0 automatically and avoids dividing by zero. If room temperature changes, U′ also contains −Ta′(t); simply replacing Ta in the displayed answer by a changing function does not solve the modified equation.

Try two checks

1. Recover a rate from two readings

Exercise 1. A cup is 50°C above a fixed ambient temperature at t=0 and 25°C above it 5 minutes later. Find k and predict its excess temperature at t=15 minutes.

Hint for 1

Use U(5)/U(0)=e−5k=1/2.

Worked solution for 1

Taking logarithms gives −5k=ln(1/2)=−ln 2, hence k=(ln 2)/5 per minute. At 15 minutes three five-minute half-lives have elapsed, so U(15)=50(1/2)3=6.25°C. The absolute temperature would require the ambient reading, which was not supplied.

2. Test a claim about crossing

Exercise 2. Someone says that a modeled hot cup becomes cooler than the room after enough time because it keeps cooling. Decide whether the claim follows from this model.

Hint for 2

Check the sign of T(t)−Ta.

Worked solution for 2

If T0>Ta, then T(t)−Ta=(T0−Ta)e−kt>0 at every finite t. The difference tends to zero as t grows, but the predicted temperature never crosses below a constant ambient temperature.

What this model earns

A proportional rate rule yields an exponential approach and a measurable time scale. The calculation also shows a useful boundary: an explicit formula may prove existence and uniqueness for this one problem, while physical validity remains a separate question. Evaporation, a changing room, a thermometer with lag, or a freezing transition can spoil the constant-k rule. To read such a rule without first finding a formula, continue to direction fields.
