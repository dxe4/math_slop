What exactly becomes impossible?

A fictional club wants a rule that turns every member's ranking of at least three designs into one group ranking. Arrow's theorem says no rule of this particular kind can satisfy a specified collection of appealing conditions on every possible preference profile. The theorem is often made too vague: it is neither a proof that ballots are useless nor a statement that every rule fails every condition. To understand its force, specify the input, output and axioms.

Background capsule. A ranking is an ordering of options. A weak ordering can include ties, but must be complete and transitive: any two options can be compared, and x ≥ y together with y ≥ z implies x ≥ z. A profile is a list of one ranking per voter. A social welfare function maps each allowed profile to a social ordering of the same alternatives. We use a finite set of at least two voters and at least three options; the rankings contain only order information, not intensity of preference.

Why conditions pull against each other

Worked example. Consider three voters with A > B > C, B > C > A, and C > A > B. A beats B 2–1, B beats C 2–1, and C beats A 2–1. Pairwise majority honors each local majority but gives a cycle, so it fails the social-ordering requirement on this profile. A dictator rule avoids cycles by copying one person's coherent ranking, but then it fails non-dictatorship. A rank-points rule can produce a complete group order, yet it may violate independence of irrelevant alternatives, abbreviated IIA.

Here is a direct IIA test for Borda's 2–1–0 scores. Five voters have the same A-versus-B views in two profiles: three rank A above B; two rank B above A. In profile P, three submit A > C > B and two submit B > A > C. Borda totals are A = 8, B = 4, C = 3, so A ranks above B. In profile Q, those three submit C > A > B and the other two submit B > C > A. Totals are C = 8, B = 4, A = 3, so B ranks above A. No voter changed their A-versus-B comparison. Only C moved. Therefore this Borda social comparison violates IIA on these two profiles. The computation diagnoses one rule; it is not a proof of Arrow's full impossibility theorem.

State the theorem with its domain

For a fixed finite set of at least three alternatives and at least two individuals, suppose a social welfare function takes every profile of complete, transitive individual rankings and returns a complete, transitive social ranking. Require weak Pareto: unanimous strict preference for x over y forces social x above y. Require IIA: if each person's comparison of x with y is unchanged between two profiles, the social comparison of x with y is unchanged. Then the rule must be dictatorial: some fixed individual always determines every strict social pairwise comparison. Equivalently, no such rule also satisfies non-dictatorship.

(UD∧SO∧P∧IIA)⇒D(|X|≥3)

Proof boundary. The example above proves only that Borda violates IIA. The majority cycle proves only that pairwise majority can fail social ordering. Neither proves that every possible rule fails one of the conditions. The general implication requires a separate argument, commonly using decisive coalitions or pivotal voters to show that Pareto and IIA force one individual to determine every pair. This article states that theorem from the cited mathematical-philosophy reference and illustrates its assumptions; it does not present a complete proof of the universal quantification over rules.

One must not silently switch from a complete group ranking to a rule that returns only one winner. Winner-only methods answer a related but different formal question. Likewise restricting allowed preference profiles, permitting extra intensity information, or relaxing IIA changes the mathematical setup. Those are possible design choices, not counterexamples to a theorem with its hypotheses intact.

Identify the exact violation

1. Calculation. Recompute A's and B's Borda scores in profile Q. Explain which voter-level A/B comparisons changed from profile P.

Hint for exercise 1

Each C > A > B ballot gives A one and B zero; each B > C > A gives B two and A zero.

Full solution to exercise 1

The three C > A > B ballots contribute A = 3, B = 0. The two B > C > A ballots contribute A = 0, B = 4. Thus Borda places B above A, 4 to 3. In P, the three A > C > B voters still ranked A above B, and the two B > A > C voters still ranked B above A. No A/B pairwise comparison changed at all. The social A/B reversal therefore violates IIA.

2. Scope check. A club uses a rule only when all voters rank A at the top. If it finds a non-dictatorial rule satisfying Pareto and IIA on that restricted set, has it refuted Arrow's theorem?

Hint for exercise 2

Ask whether the rule accepts every possible coherent preference profile.

Full solution to exercise 2

No. Requiring every voter to put A first excludes many coherent profiles, including the majority cycle shown above. That violates unrestricted domain, one of the theorem's hypotheses. The rule may work well for that narrower domain, but it does not supply a social welfare function meeting all Arrow conditions for every ranking profile. A theorem's conclusion need not hold after one of its assumptions is removed.

A precise limit on a precise task

Arrow's result concerns aggregation of unrestricted ordinal rankings into a complete and transitive social ranking, while satisfying weak Pareto, IIA and non-dictatorship. The theorem exposes a tradeoff among those requirements. A practical club can choose and explain a rule according to its priorities; it should not claim that the rule has every axiom simultaneously.

Failure case for a casual slogan. “No fair election exists” has no defined meaning of fair and confuses ranked social welfare functions with all conceivable voting procedures. Our Borda profiles illustrate one exact axiom failure, while the theorem supplies the general impossibility only under its stated domain and output requirements.
