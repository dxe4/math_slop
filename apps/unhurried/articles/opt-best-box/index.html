Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

What should be cut away to make more room?

A rectangular sheet can become an open box by cutting a square from each corner and folding up the sides. Larger cuts make taller walls, but also shorten the base. The interesting choice is the cut size, not merely the final shape. We shall optimize an ideal volume and say exactly where that model applies.

Background capsule. Let the sheet have length L and width W, both positive, and let x be the side of each removed square. After folding, the base dimensions are L−2x and W−2x and the height is x. Volume is the product of these three lengths. A feasible choice keeps all lengths nonnegative, so 0≤x≤min(L,W)/2. At either endpoint a dimension is zero. A derivative f′(x) measures the limiting slope of a function; interior maxima of differentiable functions can occur where that slope is zero, but endpoints require separate attention.

A square sheet with exact arithmetic

Worked example. Take L=W=12 centimetres. The ideal volume is V(x)=x(12−2x)², for 0≤x≤6. At x=1 the volume is 100 cubic centimetres; at x=2 it is 128; at x=3 it is 108. These values suggest a maximum near 2, but three samples cannot certify the whole interval.

Expand V(x)=144x−48x²+4x³. Differentiating gives V′(x)=144−96x+12x²=12(x−2)(x−6). On (0,2), both factors are negative, so the derivative is positive. On (2,6), one factor is positive and the other negative, so it is negative. Volume rises to x=2 and then falls. At x=0 and x=6 it equals zero. Thus x=2 is the unique global maximum on the feasible interval, with base 8×8 and height 2.

The word ideal matters. Card has thickness; folds and seams consume material; a real box may need a lid. The model answers a clean geometric question, not a manufacturing specification. Changing the objective to, say, usable interior volume changes the calculation.

Model first, certify second

The central model is V(x)=x(L−2x)(W−2x),0≤x≤min(L,W)2

Exact statement. For a square sheet of side s>0, the ideal volume V(x)=x(s−2x)² on [0,s/2] has its unique maximum at x=s/6, of value 2s³/27. To prove this, differentiate: V′(x)=(s−2x)(s−6x). The first factor is nonnegative throughout the interval and positive before its right endpoint. The second changes sign from positive to negative at s/6. So V increases until s/6 and decreases afterward. Substitution gives (s/6)(2s/3)²=2s³/27. Continuity on the closed interval guarantees some maximum; the sign argument locates it and establishes uniqueness.

This proof applies to square sheets and zero-thickness geometry. For a general rectangle, expand and solve a quadratic derivative, then keep only critical values inside its own feasible interval. A root outside that interval is no physical cut. The derivative test is a candidate finder; comparison with boundaries and feasibility is part of the solution.

Do not infer that "more wall height gives more volume." On a 12-centimetre sheet, increasing x from 2 to 3 raises height by half but lowers volume from 128 to 108. Likewise, a cubic polynomial outside the feasible interval may have other behavior irrelevant to the box.

Check the choice

1. Calculation. An 18-by-18 sheet is folded in the same way. Find the best cut and ideal volume.

Hint for question 1

Use the square-sheet result with s=18, and calculate the remaining base side.

Full solution for question 1

The maximizing cut is s/6=3 centimetres. Each base side is 18−2·3=12 centimetres. The volume is 3·12²=432 cubic centimetres, matching 2·18³/27=432. The endpoints 0 and 9 give zero, so this interior value beats them.

2. Model check. Someone cuts x=5 from a 10-by-14 sheet and reports a large box because the walls are tall. Explain the error and compare x=1 and x=2.

Hint for question 2

Check both base dimensions and the permitted interval before comparing volumes.

Full solution for question 2

The feasible interval is 0≤x≤5. At x=5 the 10-centimetre side becomes 10−10=0, so the box has zero volume. At x=1, V=1·8·12=96 cubic centimetres. At x=2, V=2·6·10=120. Thus x=2 beats x=1, but this comparison alone does not establish the global optimum of the rectangular problem.

A stopping point

You can turn a physical choice into a function and a feasible set, then justify a maximum by checking every type of candidate. The square-sheet formula follows from a derivative sign argument; a sketch or a handful of samples would be weaker evidence. For another way to distinguish a best point from a merely flat point, continue to local and global minima.
