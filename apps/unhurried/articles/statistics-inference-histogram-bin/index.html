Counting choices

Begin by naming one complete outcome. If you choose one of three drinks and then one of two snacks, an outcome is a drink–snack pair. Each drink has two snack choices, so there are 3×2=6 pairs. This counts combinations of choices; it does not require a probability model.

Worked example. Arrange three distinct postcards in a row. There are three choices for the first place, two remaining choices for the second, and one for the last. The result is 3×2×1=6 arrangements, written 3!.

3!=3×2×1=6

For a positive integer n, n! multiplies every integer from 1 through n. The convention 0!=1 counts the single empty arrangement. Factorials count arrangements of distinct objects when each is used exactly once.

Try one. How many two-letter codes can you make from A, B and C when repetition is forbidden?

Worked answer

Choose the first letter in three ways and the second in two ways, giving six codes: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA and CB. If repetition were allowed, the second position would have three choices too, giving nine.

Watch the rule. If different first choices allow different numbers of second choices, count those branches separately and add. A single fixed product needs the stated number of options at every stage.

Can the same times have two different-looking shapes?

Rebin a set of puzzle completion times. The question is what the observed numbers allow us to say, and where the tempting shortcut fails.

Background capsule. A histogram groups numerical values into consecutive, nonoverlapping intervals called bins. Its vertical axis must name count, proportion, or density. A dotplot keeps each observation visible; a histogram sacrifices that detail to show grouped mass. Endpoints need an explicit convention so a value on a boundary belongs to exactly one bin.

Worked example

Ten imaginary puzzle times in minutes are 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 8, 9. With one-minute bins [1,2), [2,3), and so on, the counts are 2, 2, 2, 2, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1. With wider bins [0,5) and [5,10), the counts are 8 and 2. The second picture looks like a steep two-bar decline; the first shows a four-bin shelf, a gap, then two late observations. Neither changed any time. A five-minute boundary at 5 happens to be easy here, but another origin could redistribute observations near edges.

For these exact ten values, the two-minute cluster at 8 and 9 is separated from the eight values at 1 through 4 by empty one-minute bins. The broad [5,10) bin absorbs both late observations into one bar. A graph maker should disclose the bin edges so a reader can see that the gap was grouped away.

Read a boundary explicitly. In [2,3), the left endpoint is included and the right endpoint is excluded. Both recorded times equal to 2 belong there, while neither time equal to 3 does. The next bin receives those observations instead. This convention prevents one observation contributing to two bars; it also lets someone reconstruct the tally from the displayed edges rather than guessing how boundary values were handled.

The claim and its conditions

fj=cjn,∑j=1kfj=1

For equal-width bins, relative frequency is count in the bin divided by total count. The narrow [8,9) bin has one of ten observations, or 10%, and [9,10) has another 10%; [0,5) has eight, or 80%. Check conservation: the bin counts sum to ten because the intervals partition the listed observations. For unequal widths, bar height should be frequency density, count divided by width, if bar area is intended to represent count. Otherwise a broad bin can look deceptively tall merely because it covers more horizontal range. To compare two groups of unequal sample size, relative-frequency or density histograms are more useful than raw counts.

Proof boundary and failure case. The partition identity is exact for the recorded data. Claims such as “this population has two modes” are not proved by changing bins in ten observations. Too few bins erase detail; too many make noise look like structure. Always inspect individual points or several reasonable bin widths before describing a smooth distribution.

The relative frequencies are 0.2 in each of the four occupied early one-minute bins, 0.1 in [8,9), and 0.1 in [9,10). Their sum is 1.0. The broad [0,5) bin has frequency 0.8. Because its width is five minutes, its density height would be 0.8/5=0.16 per minute, if the plot uses area to represent proportion.

Try two checks

1. Calculation. Using the ten times, make bins [0,3), [3,6), [6,9), [9,12). Give counts and relative frequencies.

Hint for exercise 1

A time of 3 belongs to [3,6); a time of 9 belongs to [9,12).

Full solution to exercise 1

[0,3) contains 1,1,2,2: count 4 and frequency 0.4. [3,6) contains 3,3,4,4: 4 and 0.4. [6,9) contains 8: 1 and 0.1. [9,12) contains 9: 1 and 0.1. Counts total 10 and frequencies total 1.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. Two adjacent bins have widths 1 and 4 minutes and each contains four observations. If bar area is count, what are their heights?

Hint for exercise 2

Divide each count by its own width.

Full solution to exercise 2

The one-minute bar height is 4/1=4 observations per minute. The four-minute bar height is 4/4=1 observation per minute. Both areas are 4 because height times width gives count; equal raw-count heights would misrepresent the wider bin’s area.

A useful check is to place each of the ten raw times in exactly one interval. If a boundary time is counted twice or omitted, the bin-count sum will not equal ten. For a rebinning comparison, keep the same data and change only the interval choice.

What you can carry forward

A histogram is a choice of measurement display, not a new dataset. State edges, endpoint rule, vertical scale, and sample size. Rebinning can reveal or hide features, so shape claims should survive sensible choices and be supported by more data when they matter.

These bins suggest a gap in the recorded ten times, but cannot establish a gap in the underlying population of puzzle completion times. More observations, plus a display of their individual locations, would help decide whether that feature persists.
