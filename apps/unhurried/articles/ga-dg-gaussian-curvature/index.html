Question and background

A sphere bends the same way in every direction, while a saddle bends in opposite ways. Gaussian curvature combines two principal normal bends into one signed number. This article calculates that number on two patches and separates the calculation from a deeper theorem about intrinsic geometry.

Local capsule. At a regular surface point choose a unit normal n. Moving along a tangent direction changes n; the negative tangential derivative defines the shape operator S. Its two real eigenvalues k₁,k₂ are principal curvatures under this sign convention. Their product is Gaussian curvature K=det S. Reversing n negates both eigenvalues, so their product remains unchanged. The first fundamental form measures tangent lengths; the second fundamental form involves second derivatives and normal bending.

For a patch X(u,v), let E=Xᵤ·Xᵤ, F=Xᵤ·Xᵥ, G=Xᵥ·Xᵥ, and e=n·Xᵤᵤ, f=n·Xᵤᵥ, g=n·Xᵥᵥ. Where the patch is regular, K=(eg−f²)/(EG−F²). We use that local formula with explicit derivatives.

A worked example

Worked example. First consider the radius-R sphere near its north pole as the graph z=f(x,y)=√(R²−x²−y²). At the pole, fₓ=fᵧ=0, and the tangent vectors are the horizontal coordinate vectors, so E=G=1 and F=0. With the upward normal, second derivatives are fₓₓ=fᵧᵧ=−1/R and fₓᵧ=0. Hence e=g=−1/R, f=0, and K=1/R². Both principal curvatures have the same sign for this orientation.

Now take the saddle graph z=x²−y² at the origin. Again E=G=1,F=0 and n points upward. This time e=2, g=−2, f=0. Therefore K=(2)(−2)=−4. The two principal bends oppose each other. If we reversed the normal, the values would be −2 and 2, while K would stay −4.

These are point calculations. A surface with variable curvature needs the coefficients recomputed at each point; using the origin's values across the whole saddle would be an error.

Calculate the bend of a cylinder

For the unit cylinder use X(u,v)=(cos u,sin u,v), on an angular interval shorter than a full turn. Then Xᵤ=(−sin u,cos u,0) and Xᵥ=(0,0,1), giving E=G=1 and F=0. Choose outward unit normal n=(cos u,sin u,0). The second derivatives are Xᵤᵤ=−n, Xᵤᵥ=0 and Xᵥᵥ=0. Dotting them with n gives e=−1, f=0 and g=0 under the convention used above.

The quotient formula now gives K=[(−1)·0−0]/[1·1−0]=0 at every point of this patch. One principal direction bends around the cylinder; the other runs along a straight generator and has zero normal curvature. Multiplying the two principal curvatures therefore gives zero, although the cylinder visibly bends in space.

Its first fundamental form is du²+dv², the ordinary flat-plane metric on the unrolled coordinate rectangle. This local calculation explains the flat intrinsic curvature without using the unproved general theorem. It does not identify the whole cylinder with the plane: periodicity around the cylinder changes its global topology.

The statement and its boundary

Exact local formula. For a C² regular patch with its first and second fundamental coefficients defined above,

K=k1k2=eg−f2EG−F2

The matrix of S relative to the coordinate tangent basis is obtained from the inverse metric matrix times the second-form matrix, up to the normal/sign convention chosen for S. Taking its determinant gives the quotient. This is an algebraic derivation of the local formula, and the sphere and saddle substitutions are exact. A full derivation that identifies every shape-operator coefficient from normal differentiation needs additional tensor bookkeeping; we have sketched that step.

Gauss's Theorema Egregium says K can be recovered intrinsically from the metric, despite this formula using a normal in ambient space. That surprising theorem is stated here, not proved. Do not infer that any picture of bending alone determines intrinsic curvature. A cylinder visibly bends in ℝ³ yet has K=0 away from any seam, like a flat sheet rolled without stretching.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. a scaled saddle. For z=2x²−3y², compute K at the origin with upward normal.

Hint for exercise 1

At the origin, the first-form matrix is the identity; find the Hessian entries.

Full solution to exercise 1

The first derivatives vanish at the origin, so E=G=1,F=0. The second derivatives are fₓₓ=4, fₓᵧ=0, fᵧᵧ=−6. Thus e=4,f=0,g=−6 and K=(4)(−6)−0=−24. The negative sign reflects opposite principal bends.

Exercise 2. a rolled plane. For X(u,v)=(Rcos(u/R),Rsin(u/R),v), explain why its visible bend need not give positive K.

Hint for exercise 2

Compute E,F,G and the normal bend along the vertical direction.

Full solution to exercise 2

Xᵤ is unit horizontal tangent and Xᵥ=(0,0,1), so E=G=1,F=0. The normal changes as u varies but not as v varies; vertical principal curvature is zero. The other principal curvature has magnitude 1/R, and their product is K=0. The cylinder is extrinsically bent but intrinsically flat locally.

What to carry forward

Gaussian curvature multiplies the two principal bends and can be positive, negative or zero. The exact point calculations rely on regular C² patches; the general intrinsic-curvature theorem is beyond this article's proof. A zero value at one point does not claim the whole surface is globally flat.

Gauss–Bonnet integrates this local quantity over a closed surface and connects it to topology. Applying that theorem requires global hypotheses that no single patch calculation can supply.
