Could four fit inside six?

A group with six elements cannot have a subgroup with four elements. The obstruction is more precise than a failed search through examples: every subgroup cuts a finite group into equal-sized pieces called cosets. Counting those pieces proves a divisibility theorem.

Background capsule. A group G is a set with an associative operation, identity and inverses. A subgroup H uses the same operation and contains its own products and inverses; its identity is the identity of G. Write |S| for the number of elements of a finite set S. For a fixed g in G, the left coset gH is {gh:h∈H}. The map h↦gh is a bijection, because multiplying by g−1 reverses it. Thus every left coset has |H| members, even when G is not commutative.

Partition a six-element clock

Worked example. Take G=Z/6Z under addition and H={[0],[2],[4]}. It is a subgroup: adding two even classes remains even, [0] is present, and the inverse of each listed class is listed. Its translate [1]+H is {[1],[3],[5]}. These two cosets are disjoint and cover all six classes. There are two pieces of three, so 6=2·3. We could choose [3] as a representative of the second coset and obtain the same set; distinct representatives need not produce distinct cosets.

Trying H with four elements would demand either one full piece of four and two leftovers, or some other unequal pieces. Both options conflict with the theorem's equal-piece partition. The theorem does not tell us which allowed sizes actually occur in a particular group: it only rules out sizes that do not divide the total.

The equal-coset proof

|G|=[G:H]|H|

Lagrange's theorem, with proof. If H is a subgroup of a finite group G, its order divides |G|. The symbol [G:H] is the number of distinct left cosets. Every g lies in gH because the identity e belongs to H. Thus the cosets cover G. If aH and bH overlap, write ah₁=bh₂. Then b=a h₁ h₂−1, where h₁h₂−1 belongs to H. For any h∈H, bh=a(h₁h₂−1h) lies in aH, so bH⊆aH. Reversing a and b gives equality. Hence any two cosets are disjoint or equal. Finally, h↦ah bijects H with aH; its inverse on that coset is x↦a−1x. Counting [G:H] disjoint cosets of size |H| proves the displayed formula.

Applying this to the cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ shows ord(g) divides |G| for every g in a finite group. For example, a group of order 10 cannot contain an element of order 3. The argument also shows g|G|=e: since ord(g) divides |G|, a power by the full group order is a multiple of the first return.

The converse “every divisor of |G| occurs as a subgroup order” is false. The alternating group A₄ has order 12 but no subgroup of order 6: such a subgroup would have index 2 and hence be normal; the normal subgroups of A₄ are {e}, the Klein four group, and A₄ itself. That fact takes extra work, so the present proof does not establish it. Here the reliable conclusion is divisibility in one direction only.

Count with care

1. Calculation. A finite group has 15 elements. What are the possible orders of an individual element? Can it have order 6?

Hint for question 1

Apply Lagrange to ⟨g⟩, then list positive divisors of 15.

Full solution for question 1

The order of g equals |⟨g⟩| and must divide 15. Its only possible values are 1, 3, 5 and 15. Therefore order 6 is impossible. The theorem does not assert that every listed value occurs in every group of order 15.

2. Explanation. Why is “aH and bH have a common element” enough to conclude they are the same set?

Hint for question 2

Write the common element in both ways and solve for b in terms of a and an element of H.

Full solution for question 2

If ah₁=bh₂, then b=ah₁h₂−1=ak for k∈H. Every bh equals a(kh), so bH⊆aH. Also a=bk−1, so every ah lies in bH. Therefore aH=bH. This step uses closure and inverses in H, not commutativity of G.

A one-way size test

Cosets partition a finite group into equally sized blocks. This fully proves |H| divides |G| and, by taking H=⟨g⟩, ord(g) divides |G|. A divisor is only a candidate size. For existence questions, another construction or theorem is needed; even a perfectly divisible number need not correspond to a subgroup in every group.

The same cosets will later become elements of a quotient group, but that requires an additional normality condition before multiplying cosets can make sense.
