Why does the same spring settle in different ways?

A mass m>0 displaced by x from equilibrium feels an ideal restoring force −kx with k>0. A linear damper adds force −cx′ with c≥0. Newton's law then gives mx″+cx′+kx=0. The initial displacement and velocity determine a specific motion. The three damping regimes follow from one quadratic discriminant; the labels describe a linear model with constant coefficients.

Local background. A trial function eλt has derivatives λeλt and λ²eλt. Substitution converts the differential equation into a quadratic in λ. Complex roots a±ib correspond to real combinations eatcos(bt) and eatsin(bt). Those formulas can be checked by differentiating.

Three parameter choices

Worked example. Let m=k=1 and x(0)=1, x′(0)=0. With c=0, x(t)=cos t: no decay. With c=1, roots are (−1±i√3)/2, and the solution is e−t/2[cos(√3t/2)+(1/√3)sin(√3t/2)]. Its oscillation envelope decays. With c=2, the repeated root is −1, and x(t)=(1+t)e−t, which returns without crossing zero. With c=3, the two roots (−3±√5)/2 are negative and distinct; the response is a sum of two decays.

Use initial data to interpret the critical example. For x(t)=(1+t)e−t, differentiation gives x′(t)=−te−t. Hence for t>0 the displacement stays positive and decreases, while velocity is negative and eventually returns toward zero. The initial zero velocity does not mean the mass stays fixed: the equation gives x″(0)=−1. Its first acceleration starts the return. These direct checks explain “no crossing” for this particular initial state; a damping label alone does not settle the signs of every solution with different initial velocity.

Derive the classification

Substituting x=eλt gives eλt(mλ²+cλ+k)=0. Because the exponential is never zero, λ must satisfy

λ±=−c±c2−4mk2m

When D<0 and c>0, set α=c/(2m) and β=√(4mk−c²)/(2m). The real solution is e−αt(A cos βt+B sin βt), so it oscillates while its envelope shrinks. When D>0, both roots are negative: √D<c because mk>0, hence −c+√D<0. When D=0, a second independent solution is te−ct/(2m); substitution verifies it. Initial x and x′ determine A,B or the corresponding real-root coefficients by a nonsingular two-equation system.

At c=0, D<0 but the real part is zero. Thus “underdamped” alone is not enough to claim decay if zero damping is included. Energy gives another boundary check. Define E=(m/2)(x′)²+(k/2)x². Differentiating along a solution yields E′=x′(mx″+kx)=−c(x′)²≤0. It decreases when c>0 except at instants of zero velocity; with c=0 it stays constant. This identity supports the qualitative distinction without proving every detailed long-time claim by itself.

Critical damping is often said to be the fastest return without oscillation. That slogan needs a specified initial condition and a definition of “return”; for example a specially chosen overdamped initial velocity may cross equilibrium sooner. What the discriminant proves here is the form of the homogeneous solution and the presence or absence of sinusoidal factors. A real spring may have nonlinear stiffness, dry friction, or changing damping; those require a different equation.

Classify and verify

1. A critical case

Exercise 1. For x″+4x′+4x=0, x(0)=2 and x′(0)=0, find x(t).

Hint for 1

The root is −2 twice, so use (A+Bt)e−2t.

Worked solution for 1

x(0)=A=2. Differentiating gives x′=e−2t[B−2A−2Bt], so x′(0)=B−4=0 and B=4. Hence x(t)=(2+4t)e−2t. The discriminant 4²−4·1·4=0 confirms critical damping.

2. Correct a tempting claim

Exercise 2. A system has m=k=1, c=0. Someone classifies D<0 and concludes its energy tends to zero. Find the error and the actual energy for x(0)=1, x′(0)=0.

Hint for 2

Use E′=−c(x′)² and solve x″+x=0.

Worked solution for 2

D=−4<0 gives imaginary roots, but c=0 means their real part is zero, so there is no decaying envelope. The solution is x=cos t, x′=−sin t. Its energy is E=(sin²t+cos²t)/2=1/2 at every time, not a quantity tending to zero.

What the discriminant settles

The quadratic roots classify homogeneous linear motion when m,k>0 and c≥0. The energy calculation checks whether damping actually removes energy. The case c=0 matters: it is oscillatory but not settling. Periodic forcing asks what changes when energy is supplied continuously.
