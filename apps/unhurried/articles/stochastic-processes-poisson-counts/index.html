Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Count arrivals in a fixed interval

An imaginary museum sees visitors arriving according to a stipulated model at an average rate of two per hour. What is the chance of exactly three arrivals during one hour? A Poisson process offers one answer when arrivals occur independently in disjoint time intervals at a constant rate, with at most one arrival to first order in a very short interval. The formula is a consequence of that idealized rule, not a universal law of museums.

Background capsule. N(t) counts arrivals during an interval of length t, beginning at time zero. The rate λ has units “arrivals per unit time”; λt is a unitless expected count. The factorial k! means k(k−1)…1, with 0!=1. The number e is about 2.718; e⁻ˣ is the decaying exponential, equal to one when x=0. We use independence to multiply probabilities across disjoint small intervals.

Time t must be nonnegative and λ positive for the usual nondegenerate process. If λ=0, there are no arrivals and only the k=0 probability is one.

Three visitors in one hour

Worked example. With λ=2 per hour and t=1 hour, λt=2. The chance of exactly three arrivals is e⁻²2³/3! = (4/3)e⁻², about 0.1804. For zero visitors it is e⁻², about 0.1353. For one visitor it is 2e⁻², about 0.2707. The fact that the expected count is two does not make exactly two certain: P(N(1)=2)=2e⁻², also about 0.2707.

The displayed rows omit four or more arrivals; their probabilities total less than one. In a two-hour interval at the same rate, λt=4, so we must use four rather than two in the formula. Mixing hours and minutes without conversion would silently change the model. A count histogram from finitely many simulated days may look uneven even when the exact probabilities are fixed.

Where the Poisson formula comes from

P(N(t)=k)=e−λt(λt)kk!,k=0,1,2,…

One exact construction partitions an interval into n tiny cells of width t/n and gives each cell an independent arrival indicator with chance λt/n, for n large enough that this is at most one. The count is binomial: P(Nₙ(t)=k)=C(n,k)(λt/n)ᵏ(1−λt/n)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. For fixed k as n→∞, C(n,k)/nᵏ→1/k!, and (1−λt/n)ⁿ⁻ᵏ→e⁻λᵗ. The limit yields the formula. The limiting construction supports the Poisson count law; it does not by itself prove that a particular real arrival stream follows it.

A Poisson process also has independent increments: counts in disjoint intervals are independent. It has stationary increments: their distributions depend on interval lengths, not clock positions. These assumptions imply the same expected count λt in every interval of length t. A rush-hour pattern violates constant rate; arrivals in groups can violate the single-event small-interval behavior or independence. Either makes the simple model questionable. “Random arrivals” alone is too vague to imply a Poisson process.

Proof boundary. We derived the marginal count formula from a binomial rare-event limit. The full continuous-time process needs a compatible construction of counts across all intervals. This article uses the usual Poisson-process axioms for those joint statements. A finite observed timeline can help assess fit but cannot verify exact independence or the limit.

Keep rate and time together

1. Calculation. A toy counter follows a Poisson process at rate six per hour. Find the chance of no arrivals in ten minutes and of exactly one arrival in ten minutes.

Hint for question 1

Ten minutes is 1/6 hour, so find λt first.

Full solution to question 1

λt=(6/hour)(1/6 hour)=1. Thus P(N=0)=e⁻¹≈0.3679 and P(N=1)=e⁻¹1¹/1!=e⁻¹≈0.3679. Using t=10 without converting minutes to hours would produce an expected count of 60 and answer a different question.

2. Model check. A museum admits visitors only in buses of exactly ten at scheduled times. Can its one-hour count be described by a Poisson process merely because the bus timing is uncertain?

Hint for question 2

Ask whether a tiny interval can contain ten simultaneous arrivals and whether individual arrivals are independent.

Full solution to question 2

No. Counts jump by ten when a bus arrives, so the probability of multiple individual arrivals in a short interval is of the same order as a bus arrival. The simple Poisson process assumes single arrivals to first order and disjoint-increment independence. A model for bus arrivals might be Poisson, but the individual visitor count would then be a compound count with multiples of ten, not the displayed Poisson law.

What a count model commits to

The Poisson formula relates the count k to a dimensionless expected count λt. Its small-interval and independence assumptions are substantive. Next, waiting for the first arrival turns the zero-count probability into a survival probability. The direction of that derivation is exact within the same process model.
