How can missing angle measure area?

For a Euclidean triangle the angle sum is π. For a geodesic triangle in a constant curvature −1 hyperbolic plane it is less, and the shortfall equals its area in the model's natural units. This is an exact theorem, but its usual proof invokes Gauss–Bonnet. We will use that theorem with all assumptions visible, check a numerical instance, and distinguish the application from a proof of Gauss–Bonnet itself.

Background capsule. Angles are measured in radians. A geodesic triangle has sides that are shortest-path geodesic segments in the specified metric. Gaussian curvature K describes intrinsic bending; the standard Poincaré disk metric has constant K=−1 when lengths use its chosen unit. An area element derived from that metric measures hyperbolic area, which differs from ordinary Euclidean ink area. The triangle must be nondegenerate and have finite vertices in the hyperbolic plane for the direct form used here.

A triangle with prescribed angles

Worked example. Suppose a hyperbolic geodesic triangle has angles α=π/3, β=π/4, γ=π/6. Their sum is 4π/12+3π/12+2π/12=9π/12=3π/4. The defect is π/4, so its area is π/4 hyperbolic square units at curvature −1. Such an angle triple is possible because all angles are positive and their sum is below π. The number does not mean that a Euclidean drawing of the triangle occupies π/4 square units on paper.

If we keep the same shape in a hyperbolic plane of curvature −1/R², its angle defect remains dimensionless while the natural area scales by R². We must therefore name curvature before converting an angle deficit into an area. Geometry's units are part of the formula.

The theorem and its proof dependency

Exact conditional statement. For a nondegenerate geodesic triangle T in the complete constant-curvature −1 hyperbolic plane, with interior angles α, β, γ,

Area(T)=π−(α+β+γ)

Here is the Gauss–Bonnet route, labelled as a theorem application. For a geodesic triangle, the boundary's geodesic curvature is zero. The local Gauss–Bonnet formula for a topological disk says ∫TK dA plus the sum of exterior turning angles equals 2π. The exterior turn at each corner is π minus its interior angle, so their sum is 3π−(α+β+γ). Since K=−1 throughout T, the curvature integral is −Area(T). Substituting yields −Area(T)+3π−(α+β+γ)=2π, hence the displayed result. The algebra after invoking Gauss–Bonnet is complete; the general Gauss–Bonnet theorem and the proof that the disk metric has K=−1 are deferred.

Positivity of area immediately gives α+β+γ<π for a nondegenerate finite triangle. The converse claim that every positive triple with sum below π is realised by a hyperbolic triangle is a further existence theorem, used as background for the worked example rather than proved here. At curvature K=−1/R², the same substitution gives Area=R²[π−(α+β+γ)]. Replacing R with a number changes the area unit, not the angle deficit.

This result does not use the Euclidean formula with a negative correction guessed from a sketch. It requires hyperbolic geodesic sides and hyperbolic area. A curvy region bounded by arbitrary arcs has nonzero geodesic-curvature boundary terms and cannot be put into this triangle formula without adjustment.

Apply the right formula to the right object

1. Calculation. A curvature −1 geodesic triangle has angles π/2, π/4 and π/8. Find its angle sum and area. What would its area be at curvature −1/9 if the same angles occur?

Hint for question 1

Use eighths of π, then multiply the defect by R²=9 in the second metric.

Full solution for question 1

The sum is 4π/8+2π/8+π/8=7π/8. The defect is π/8, so at K=−1 the area is π/8. At K=−1/9, R=3 and the area is 9π/8 square units in that metric.

2. Failure test. A triangle drawn with Euclidean straight segments has angles 50°, 60°, 70°. A student applies the hyperbolic formula and obtains area zero. Explain precisely why that does not determine its Euclidean area.

Hint for question 2

Which metric and curvature are hypotheses of the area formula?

Full solution for question 2

The angles total 180°, so the numerical defect is zero. But the formula assumes curvature −1 and geodesic sides in that metric. This is a Euclidean triangle with curvature zero. Euclidean triangles with those angles exist at every scale, so their area can vary while their angles remain fixed. The hyperbolic formula cannot be transferred to them.

A precise stopping point

At curvature −1, a hyperbolic geodesic triangle's area equals its positive angular defect. We derived that equation from Gauss–Bonnet and showed the unit correction for curvature −1/R². We have not proved Gauss–Bonnet or the full disk metric theory here. The Euclidean counterexample exposes the missing hypothesis in any blanket “area from angles” claim. A useful next question is why the sphere reverses the sign: spherical triangles have angle excess, but their own area formula also needs curvature and geodesic sides.
