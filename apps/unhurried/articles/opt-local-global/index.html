Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Does stopping downhill mean you are done?

Imagine standing in a valley. No nearby step descends, yet a deeper valley could lie beyond a hill. Optimization needs words that separate these two claims. A local minimum compares a point with sufficiently nearby feasible points. A global minimum compares it with every feasible point. The chosen domain is part of both definitions.

Background capsule. A function f assigns a number to each input x in a domain D. For x* in D, local minimum means that some radius r>0 satisfies f(x*)≤f(x) for all feasible x with |x−x*|<r. Global minimum means f(x*)≤f(x) for all x in D. A derivative is the limit of nearby secant slopes when that limit exists. “Stationary” means f′(x*)=0. We use the derivative only where x* lies inside an open interval of feasible inputs.

Three points that look deceptively similar

Worked example. Take f(x)=x³−3x on [−2,2]. Its derivative is 3x²−3=3(x−1)(x+1), so stationary candidates are −1 and 1. The derivative is positive before −1, negative between −1 and 1, then positive after 1. Thus −1 is a local maximum and 1 is a local minimum. Values are f(−2)=−2, f(−1)=2, f(1)=−2, and f(2)=2. The local minimum at 1 is global too, but the left endpoint −2 is another global minimizer despite a nonzero one-sided slope there. A stationary point need not be a minimum: −1 is a maximum.

For an actual shallow-versus-deep valley, use h(x)=(x²−1)²+x/4 on [−2,2]. On [3/4,1], the derivative h′(x)=4x(x²−1)+1/4 is negative at 3/4 (−17/16) and positive at 1 (1/4). Continuity gives a minimum on that closed interval, and these inward derivative signs rule out both endpoints, so an interior point is a local minimum on the full domain. Yet throughout [3/4,1], h(x)≥x/4≥3/16, while h(−1)=−1/4. This right-hand valley is therefore definitely not global. A local test alone cannot certify the best value.

The necessary condition and its limits

The exact first-order claim is f′(x*)=0 when f is differentiable at an interior local minimum x*. Here is a short proof. For sufficiently small h>0, local minimality gives [f(x*+h)−f(x*)]/h≥0. For h<0, the same numerator is nonnegative but the denominator is negative, so its quotient is ≤0. If both one-sided quotients converge to the same derivative, that number is at once ≥0 and ≤0; hence it is zero.

There are three crucial qualifications. At a domain endpoint, only one side is feasible; f(x)=x on [0,1] has a global minimum at 0 despite f′(0)=1 if the formula is extended to the real line. At a corner, the derivative may not exist; |x| has a global minimum at 0 and slopes −1 and 1 on opposite sides. Finally, zero derivative alone is insufficient: x³ is stationary at 0 but takes negative values just left of 0 and positive values just right.

On a closed interval [a,b], a continuous function attains a global minimum and maximum. If it is differentiable in the interior, compare values at a, b and every interior stationary or nondifferentiable candidate. This is a complete method only when candidates can actually be found or ruled out. It does not imply that an arbitrary nonlinear problem is easy, nor that an iterative method visiting one valley finds a global answer.

Find the gap in a proposed certificate

1. Calculation. Minimize q(x)=x²−4x on [0,5], naming all candidate types and values.

Hint for question 1

Complete the square or solve q′(x)=0, then compare endpoints.

Full solution for question 1

q′(x)=2x−4, so the only interior stationary point is x=2. Values are q(0)=0, q(2)=−4 and q(5)=5. Since q(x)=(x−2)²−4≥−4 on the whole interval, x=2 is the unique global minimizer; the square completion independently certifies the conclusion.

2. Counterexample. A colleague claims every point satisfying f′(x)=0 is at least a local minimum. Give one smooth counterexample and explain both neighboring sides.

Hint for question 2

A cubic can pass through a horizontal tangent without turning.

Full solution for question 2

Let f(x)=x³ on the real line. Its derivative 3x² vanishes at x=0. Yet for every r>0, choose a negative x with |x|<r; then x³<0=f(0). Choose a positive x of the same size and x³>0. Therefore 0 is neither a local minimum nor a local maximum, despite stationarity.

What the derivative actually buys

At an interior smooth local minimum the slope must vanish. That is a filter, not a certificate. You still need feasible-domain checks and a comparison argument. Convexity supplies a special global guarantee; absent that structure, one valley gives little information about another.
