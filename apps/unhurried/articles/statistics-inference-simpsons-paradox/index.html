Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

How can the better route finish worse overall?

A puzzle club records whether members finish a practice board within ten minutes. Members can use Route A or Route B, two ways of approaching a puzzle. The club also labels boards easy or hard. In the fictional record below, A has a higher finish rate than B among easy boards and among hard boards. Yet B has a much higher finish rate when all boards are pooled. Which comparison has changed?

Background capsule. A rate is a count divided by its relevant total. “9 of 10” is 90%; “72 of 90” is 80%. A weighted average combines subgroup rates according to how much of the total each subgroup contributes. Here each route has its own mix of easy and hard boards. We need only fractions and ordinary averages, but we must keep each denominator beside its numerator. These invented data reveal a mathematical effect.

Read four groups before the totals

Worked example. Route A was used on 10 easy boards and 90 hard ones. Route B was used on 90 easy boards and 10 hard ones. Each route was therefore tried 100 times, but on very different collections of boards.

Check each total independently: A has 9 + 36 = 45 finishes in 10 + 90 = 100 attempts. B has 72 + 3 = 75 finishes in 90 + 10 = 100 attempts. Within each difficulty, A exceeds B by ten percentage points. The pooled comparison goes the other way by thirty percentage points. The pooled row has not “corrected” the two difficulty rows; it asks a different descriptive question about two different mixtures.

Try reading the table without the difficulty labels. You might say B did better, 75% versus 45%, and that statement accurately describes these 200 attempts. But it hides that B received far more easy boards. The difficulty labels reveal why that total cannot, on its own, compare how the routes perform on equally difficult boards.

Move only the mixture weights

Before using the controls, predict how much extra easy-board work B needs to cancel A’s within-group advantage. Would any unequal mix suffice? Would a twenty-percentage-point difference suffice? Keep the four subgroup rates fixed while testing your prediction.

The activity is a separate hypothetical model with 1,000 attempts per route, so every permitted setting has integer finish counts. Its initial setting scales the original table by ten. These are constructed counts, with no sampling noise or new evidence about actual puzzle solvers.

B’s easy-board share minus A’s is 80 percentage points. Reversal requires more than 20 points; exactly 20 gives a tie. The observed-mix difference A minus B is -300/1,000.

Common hypothetical mix: 50% easy and 50% hard for both routes. Standardized A = 65%, B = 55%; A stays 10 percentage points higher. These reweighted rates are calculations from the presets, not observations of reassigned people.

To check a boundary on paper, keep A at 10% easy and give B 30% easy. The two pooled totals both become 450 of 1,000.

The weights turn the comparison around

For a route with easy-board share w, its pooled finish rate is an exact weighted average of its easy and hard rates:

rpooled=weasyreasy+whardrhard,weasy+whard=1

Route A’s weights are 10/100 = 0.10 easy and 90/100 = 0.90 hard. Its rate is 0.10 × 0.90 + 0.90 × 0.40 = 0.09 + 0.36 = 0.45. Route B’s weights are 0.90 easy and 0.10 hard. Its rate is 0.90 × 0.80 + 0.10 × 0.30 = 0.72 + 0.03 = 0.75. The formulas reproduce the counts exactly. Each route’s overall number is pulled toward the difficulty category it encountered most often.

For a descriptive like-for-like comparison, suppose we used the same hypothetical mix for both routes: half easy and half hard. A’s standardized rate would be 0.5 × 90% + 0.5 × 40% = 65%; B’s would be 0.5 × 80% + 0.5 × 30% = 55%. This is an arithmetic reweighting, not an observed result for a new set of boards. It makes clear which comparison the rates answer.

This reversal is called Simpson’s paradox. The “paradox” is a warning about aggregation, not a contradiction in arithmetic. Its exact mechanism here is unequal mixture weights. If both routes had the same difficulty weights, A’s ten-point advantage in each difficulty would remain a ten-point advantage in their weighted averages. Whether Route A causes better performance is another question: participants may have chosen routes based on experience, and “easy” versus “hard” may omit other differences. The table cannot settle those issues.

Find the exact boundary, beyond the slider

Write u for A’s easy-board fraction and v for B’s; both lie in [0,1]. The pooled rates are A(u) = 0.9u + 0.4(1−u) = 0.4 + 0.5u and B(v) = 0.8v + 0.3(1−v) = 0.3 + 0.5v. Subtracting gives A(u)−B(v) = 0.1 − 0.5(v−u). Thus B leads precisely when v−u > 0.2. At v−u = 0.2 they tie; for a smaller difference A leads. Unequal weights alone do not guarantee reversal.

For example, with u = 0.1, v = 0.29 gives A = 45% and B = 44.5%; v = 0.30 gives a 45% tie; v = 0.31 gives A = 45% and B = 45.5%. The strict inequality decides the boundary without numerical rounding. The derivation covers every real u and v in [0,1], including fractions unavailable on the integer-percent controls.

Why a common mix preserves an advantage. Let q be any common easy-board fraction. The standardized difference is q(0.9−0.8) + (1−q)(0.4−0.3) = 0.1. More generally, for any finite set of groups, common weights wg ≥ 0 summing to 1 give difference ∑gwg(ag−bg). If every subgroup difference is nonnegative, the sum is nonnegative. It is strictly positive if at least one strictly positive difference receives positive weight. This is a proof from nonnegative terms; checking many slider settings would not establish the general statement.

Optional deeper investigation: the same table can hide opposite causal effects (about 15–25 minutes)

State a different question before answering it

Return to the original 200 attempts, rather than the 2,000-attempt slider model. For this investigation, stipulate that these were 200 different people, each facing one fixed board. Route A and Route B are well-defined instructions; one person’s route does not change another’s outcome. Let Yi(A) be 1 if person i would finish their fixed board using A, otherwise 0. Define Yi(B) similarly. Their recorded outcome equals the potential outcome for the route actually used. This is a hypothetical two-outcome model, not a claim that the original record measured both outcomes.

The causal quantity we want is the average of Yi(A)−Yi(B) over these same 200 people: the finish fraction if everyone used A minus the finish fraction if everyone used B. This compares two assignments to one cohort. The observed 45% minus 75% instead compares two different groups of 100 people.

For the 100 A users we know 45 values Y(A)=1 and 55 values Y(A)=0, but none of their Y(B) values. For the 100 B users we know 75 values Y(B)=1 and 25 values Y(B)=0, but none of their Y(A) values. Difficulty labels retain exactly their original values. Ask whether those missing outcomes can be filled in two ways that preserve every observed cell but change the causal answer’s sign.

Construct two compatible completions

Fill only the outcome under the unused route; the original observations stay fixed

A favorableSet every Y(B) to 0Set every Y(A) to 1
A unfavorableSet every Y(B) to 1Set every Y(A) to 0

In the first completion, assigning everyone A gives 45 + 100 = 145 finishes, while assigning everyone B gives 0 + 75 = 75. The average causal effect is (145−75)/200 = 7/20 = +0.35. In the second, the corresponding totals are 45 + 0 = 45 and 100 + 75 = 175, giving (45−175)/200 = −13/20 = −0.65. Neither construction changes which people actually used which route, their difficulty labels, or their observed successes.

This proves the original table, even with its difficulty rows, does not determine the causal effect’s sign under this setup. The two completions are logical possibilities, not equally plausible explanations or estimated probabilities. The standardized descriptive difference of +0.10 is still correct in both; it uses the same observed conditional rates and common weights.

Say exactly what the missing information permits

Let x count the missing A outcomes equal to 1 among B users, and z the missing B outcomes equal to 1 among A users. Each is an integer from 0 to 100. Then the causal effect is (45+x−75−z)/200 = (x−z−30)/200. It lies between −0.65 and +0.35, and both endpoints are attained by the completions above. All 201 values in steps of 1/200 between those endpoints can occur. These are exact bounds for this fixed fictional cohort with unrestricted missing binary outcomes; they are not confidence limits or a result about a larger sampled population.

To estimate a causal effect from future observations, the club needs a justified design and assumptions. Randomly assigning routes within difficulty before play would make a future experiment informative about effects; finite randomization still has uncertainty. An observational adjustment would require comparable potential outcomes within the chosen groups, positive chance of each route, and the consistency setup above. A two-way table alone cannot certify those assumptions. Nor should one automatically adjust for every recorded variable: a variable changed by the route can answer a different question. This lesson establishes the arithmetic and its identification boundary; it does not develop a full causal-inference method.

The potential-outcome definition follows Larry Wasserman’s causal-inference notes, sections 2–2.1. The general bounded-outcome argument is treated in Richardson, Hudgens, Gilbert and Fine, section 2.1. Their population/sampling setting differs from our finite-cohort construction; the counts and proof here are derived for this fictional table.

Work with the denominators

1. Calculation. Using the same four subgroup rates, imagine a common mix of 20% easy and 80% hard boards for both routes. Calculate each standardized rate. Which route is higher?

Hint for question 1

Multiply each easy rate by 0.2 and each hard rate by 0.8, then add within a route.

Full solution to question 1

A: 0.2 × 0.90 + 0.8 × 0.40 = 0.18 + 0.32 = 0.50, or 50%. B: 0.2 × 0.80 + 0.8 × 0.30 = 0.16 + 0.24 = 0.40, or 40%. A is higher by ten percentage points. These are standardized calculations, not new observed counts.

2. Explanation and counterexample. A friend says, “If A is better in every difficulty group, B cannot possibly have the better overall rate.” Which numbers in the table disprove the claim, and what differs between the routes?

Hint for question 2

Compare both within-group rows, then count the easy-board attempts for A and B.

Full solution to question 2

A leads 90% to 80% on easy boards and 40% to 30% on hard boards, but B leads 75% to 45% overall. A had only 10 easy attempts and 90 hard attempts; B had 90 easy and 10 hard. The different weights allow the pooled reversal. The numbers describe these attempts and do not establish which route caused more finishes.

3. A changed problem, before using the hint. New preset rates are A: 85% easy, 50% hard; B: 75% easy, 30% hard. A’s easy fraction is u = 1/5. Find the exact range of B’s easy fraction v giving a pooled reversal. Test v = 7/10. Does the earlier “twenty extra percentage points” rule still apply? Finally, show whether a common easy fraction q can reverse these new within-group comparisons.

Hint for question 3

Start again from the two weighted averages. This time the easy-to-hard rate gaps differ between routes; do not reuse the old difference formula.

Full solution to question 3

A(u) = 1/2 + 7u/20 and B(v) = 3/10 + 9v/20. Hence B > A exactly when 9v−7u > 4. At u = 1/5, this is v > 3/5. For v = 7/10, A = 57/100 and B = 123/200, so B leads by 9/200 = 4.5 percentage points. The threshold depends on the rates: here B needs more than forty extra easy-board percentage points when A has 20% easy. A common mix gives A(q)−B(q) = 1/5−q/10, between 1/10 and 1/5 for 0 ≤ q ≤ 1, always positive.

4. Find a missing hypothesis. Another table has A rates 80% easy and 30% hard, B rates 70% easy and 40% hard. With a common mix q easy, find when A leads, ties or loses. Why does this not contradict the common-weight proof?

Hint for question 4

Compare 0.1q with 0.1(1−q).

Second hint for question 4

Write the common-weight difference as q times the easy-group rate difference plus (1−q) times the hard-group rate difference. Then check the sign and the hypothesis about both within-group differences.

Full solution to question 4

The difference is q(0.8−0.7)+(1−q)(0.3−0.4)=0.2q−0.1. A leads for q > 1/2, ties at q = 1/2 and loses for q < 1/2. The proof required nonnegative differences in every group; A loses on hard boards here, so that hypothesis fails.

5. Optional causal transfer. In a new fixed cohort of 80 people, 40 used A with 28 finishes, and 40 used B with 16 finishes. Use binary potential outcomes as above. Find the smallest and largest possible causal effects if the unused-route outcomes are unrestricted. The observed difference is positive; can the causal effect be negative?

Hint for question 5

Let x be missing A finishes among B users and z be missing B finishes among A users; each lies between 0 and 40. Keep the denominator 80.

Full solution to question 5

The effect is (28+x−16−z)/80 = (12+x−z)/80. It is smallest at x=0,z=40: −28/80 = −0.35, and largest at x=40,z=0: 52/80 = +0.65. Both preserve all observed data. Thus even the observed +0.30 comparison does not fix the sign of the same-cohort causal effect. These are fixed-cohort logical bounds, with possible values in steps of 1/80; they do not quantify sampling uncertainty.

Keep the question attached to the rate

A pooled rate describes the mix that actually occurred. A subgroup rate describes a restricted set. Before comparing routes, ask which mix matters and whether the groups are comparable. Equalizing the weights can expose the source of a reversal, but it cannot remove every source of bias. You can stop here with a complete explanation: the numerator counts finishes, the denominator counts attempts, and unequal difficulty weights produce the reversal.

For another view, Penn State’s open lessons on categorical relationships and Simpson’s paradox in tables explain the general phenomenon. Their examples are separate from this invented puzzle-club record. A next exploration would ask how to assign routes fairly if the club wanted a causal comparison; this descriptive table alone does not answer it.
