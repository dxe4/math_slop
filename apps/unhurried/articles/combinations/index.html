Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

How many groups can you make?

Five friends want to choose two people to pick a film. If Ada and Ben are chosen, that is the same pair as Ben and Ada. A quick multiplication, 5 × 4 = 20, gives a plausible answer, but it counts Ada-then-Ben and Ben-then-Ada separately. How can we remove precisely the duplication without losing a pair?

We need only two pieces of background. The product rule says that if a first choice has five possibilities and each allows four possibilities for a second choice, there are 5 × 4 ordered outcomes. The factorial notation k! means k × (k − 1) × ··· × 1, with 0! defined as 1. In particular, 2! = 2, and there are 2! ways to order two distinct people. We will choose k distinct members from n distinct people, where 0 ≤ k ≤ n, and where the order of the chosen members does not matter.

That last phrase is a mathematical assumption, not a small detail. Choosing a group is different from assigning people to named jobs or arranging them in seats. Decide what counts as the same outcome before using a formula.

Divide only after checking the duplicates

Claim. The number of k-person groups chosen from n distinct people, with 0 ≤ k ≤ n, is the binomial coefficient

(nk) = n!k!(n−k)!

First count ordered lists with no repeated person. There are n choices for the first place, n − 1 for the second, down to n − k + 1 for the last. The product is n!/(n − k)!. Now take any one chosen group. Its k distinct members can occupy the k list places in exactly k! orders. Crucially, every group has exactly that many ordered lists. Dividing the ordered count by k! therefore counts each group once and gives the formula.

This is a proof by counting the same collection in two ways. It includes the boundary cases. For k = 0, the product of zero stages is 1 and 0! = 1, so we count the unique empty group. For k = n, we choose everyone in one way. For the film-picking pair, the formula gives 5!/(2!3!) = 10, matching the table.

Why does the symbol have two numbers? The top number describes the available people; the bottom describes how many we select. It is often read “n choose k.” It also appears as a coefficient in the expansion of (x + y)n, because choosing which k factors contribute x is again a group-choice problem. That is a next idea, not needed for today's count.

Two ways to check your understanding

1. Calculate. Seven people choose a three-person book-club committee. How many committees are possible? Show the ordered count and its correction.

Hint for question 1

Count first, second and third choices; then ask how many lists describe each three-person committee.

Full solution to question 1

There are 7 × 6 × 5 = 210 ordered lists of three distinct people. Each committee appears in 3! = 6 orders. Thus 210/6 = 35 committees, equivalently 7!/(3!4!) = 35.

2. Explain the limit of the formula. The same five friends choose a chair and a secretary, and one person cannot hold both jobs. Is the answer 5 choose 2? Give the correct count and explain the difference.

Hint for question 2

Would Ada as chair with Ben as secretary be the same assignment as Ben as chair with Ada as secretary?

Full solution to question 2

No. The jobs have different names, so those assignments differ. There are 5 chair choices and then 4 secretary choices, making 5 × 4 = 20 assignments. Five choose two gives only 10 unordered pairs; each pair can fill the jobs in 2! = 2 ways, so 10 × 2 = 20.

A group is an unordered choice

The binomial coefficient counts groups of k distinct objects drawn without replacement from n distinct objects. Ordered lists overcount each group by exactly k!, which is why division works. The formula does not count job assignments as groups, and it needs adjustment when repeats are allowed or objects are indistinguishable. You now have one reliable habit: before counting, say aloud whether swapping the selected objects makes a new outcome. If it does, keep the ordered count. If it does not, check that each group has the same number of orders, then divide.
