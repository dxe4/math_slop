Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

Thirteen people force a shared birth month.

Thirteen people are in a room. Can every person have a different birth month? There are only twelve month names. The answer is no, even without knowing anybody's birthday. This guarantee is qualitatively different from a probability: no independence or uniformly distributed birthdays is needed. The same principle will tell us the exact number of objects needed to force three in some box, and it will reveal when a claim is one object short.

Prerequisite capsule. A box is any category that receives an object; it need not be a physical container. A month is a box for a person's birth month, while a remainder class is a box for an integer. Assignment means each object enters exactly one box. We ask what is forced regardless of how objects are distributed, not what is likely under a random model.

Count a concrete case

Worked example. Put the first twelve people into twelve month boxes, one per month. This extreme arrangement avoids a shared month, so twelve people do not force a collision. Add a thirteenth person. Whichever month box receives them already holds somebody, because all twelve boxes were occupied in the collision-free extreme. More generally, if each of k boxes can contain at most one object, the total capacity is k. Therefore k+1 objects force two together. To force at least three, imagine keeping each box at capacity two; 2k objects might still avoid a triple, but 2k+1 cannot. With twelve months, 25 people force at least three sharing a month; 24 can be arranged as two in every month without a triple.

The exact rule and its proof

What does the ceiling do?

N counts the objects and k counts the boxes. A box’s occupancy is the number of objects in it. The symbol ≥ means “at least.” The brackets ⌈ ⌉ mean round up to the nearest integer: ⌈13/12⌉ = 2, because 13/12 is a little more than 1. A whole number stays unchanged, so ⌈24/12⌉ = 2 as well. The average occupancy N/k need not be whole, but every actual box count is.

some occupancy≥⌈Nk⌉

General pigeonhole principle. Assign N objects to k boxes, with N a nonnegative integer and k a positive integer. At least one box has at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects. Suppose instead that every box had fewer than this number. Then each has at most ⌈N/k⌉−1. By the definition of ceiling, that is strictly less than N/k; summing over k boxes would yield a total strictly less than N, a contradiction. The bound is sharp: distribute objects as evenly as possible, with box counts differing by at most one, and the largest occupancy equals the ceiling. For the simpler two-in-one conclusion, set N=k+1, so the ceiling is two. The statement says at least one box reaches the threshold; it does not identify which one.

Where the rule fails. Do not claim that thirteen people must have the same birthday. There are many more than twelve possible day-and-month labels, so the month argument does not apply. Nor does the principle say the first two people encountered share a month. A guarantee about some pair cannot be strengthened to a particular pair. Finally, “twelve boxes” must be a genuine fixed list of categories; allowing a new box for each object destroys the bound.

Try two counts

Exercise 1. How many integers must you select to guarantee two have the same remainder on division by five? Give a sharp example one below the threshold.

Hint for exercise 1

There are five remainder boxes: 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4. Can five selected integers occupy all five separately?

Full solution to exercise 1

Six integers force a repeated remainder because six objects enter five boxes. The threshold is sharp: selecting 0,1,2,3,4 gives five different remainders, so five are insufficient.

Exercise 2. How many socks must you draw to force four socks of one of three colors, assuming each sock has exactly one color?

Hint for exercise 2

How many can you draw while holding at most three of each color?

Full solution to exercise 2

At most three of each color allows 3+3+3=9 socks without four matching. The tenth sock enters one of the three color boxes and makes that box's occupancy at least four. The answer is ten. A possible nine-sock counterexample is three red, three blue, and three green.

What to carry forward

Forcing a collision is a capacity argument. Name the objects and boxes, state the desired occupancy, then count how many objects the boxes could hold if the desired event never happened. One more object is the sharp threshold when an even distribution is possible. The principle guarantees existence but supplies no location or randomness claim.
