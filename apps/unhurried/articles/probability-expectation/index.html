Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Can an average be a score nobody gets?

Roll a fair die for paper points: six earns eight, odd faces earn two, and other even faces earn zero. Averaging only eight and zero omits a score and its frequency. Write the reward for each face before calculating.

Background capsule. A random variable assigns a numerical score to an outcome: here a reward to a die face. Several faces may share a score. Its distribution lists the scores and probabilities. Expected value is their probability-weighted average, which need not be obtainable in one play.

A name for the average

Write the score as X and its expectation as E[X], “expectation of X.” Lower-case x names a possible score, such as 0, 2 or 8; P(X=x) is its probability. Negative scores represent losses.

Build the average from the six faces

  1. List the scores. Faces 1 through 6 earn 2, 0, 2, 0, 2, 8. All six faces have equal chances, so their ordinary average is (2 + 0 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 8)/6 = 14/6.
  2. Group equal scores. Zero occurs on two faces; two points occurs on three; eight points occurs on one. Their probabilities are 2/6, 3/6 and 1/6, which add to one.
  3. Weight each score by its chance. Multiply each score by its probability, then add the contributions below. The result is 14/6 = 7/3 points, about 2.33.

Worked example. No play scores 7/3 points. Two points is the most likely score, but 7/3 is the mean. Those answer different questions. For 60 independent plays under this same model, the expected total is 60 × 7/3 = 140 points. An actual total may differ; the calculation does not promise a particular sequence of rolls.

Averaging score labels gives (0+2+8)/3=10/3, incorrectly assigning equal chances to unequal-frequency scores. With a biased die, even the six-face average fails; use the actual face probabilities.

The general rule, read slowly

Exact statement. For a random variable with finitely many possible values, its expected value is the sum of each value multiplied by its probability:

E[X]=∑xxP(X=x)

The symbol ∑ means add, visiting each distinct score x once. Here it gives 0P(X=0)+2P(X=2)+8P(X=8), the table's last column. Unequal probabilities are allowed; equal elementary-outcome chances are unnecessary.

Changing every score has a predictable effect. If each play costs two points, subtract two from every payoff. The new mean is the old mean minus two: 7/3 − 2 = 1/3 point. If every payoff doubles, its mean doubles too. In symbols, E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b, where a and b are fixed numbers.

Why does changing the scores work this way?

Replace each score x by ax + b in the weighted sum. Distributing gives a sum of ax × its probability and a sum of b × its probability. Pull the fixed a out of the first sum to get aE[X]. The second sum is b times all the probabilities added together, which is b × 1. Adding the two parts gives the rule.

For finite scores on one experiment, E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]. Weight the combined score at each elementary outcome by its probability, then split each contribution into X and Y parts. Their sums are the individual means. Independence is unnecessary, as exercise 2 demonstrates.

If “fair” means zero expected net points, entry cost must equal expected reward. A mean reward alone specifies neither that cost nor reward variability.

The optional investigation removes the fixed last toss. It uses geometric series and introduces martingales.

Optional investigation: can a fair process finish at one?

A paper-score model with a stopping rule

Toss an independent fair coin repeatedly for imaginary points, starting at 0. On toss k, add 2k−1 for heads or subtract it for tails; stop at the first head. Thus amounts begin 1,2,4,…, chosen from past tosses before seeing the next result. Neither amounts nor possible negative scores are bounded.

Let T be the index of the first head. Let Gn be the accumulated score after at most n tosses, keeping the score fixed after the first head. Thus G0=0. For example, H finishes at 1; TH finishes at −1+2=1; TTH finishes at −1−2+4=1. If all of the first n tosses are tails, the current score is −(1+2+⋯+2n−1)=1−2n.

Predict first. At a cap of four tosses, what are the two possible scores? If you replace four by twenty, does the negative branch's contribution to the mean become negligible? Keep its probability, score and probability-weighted contribution in separate columns.

Worked setting: cap K=4. Fifteen of the sixteen four-toss continuations contain a head. The remaining TTTT continuation has score −15.

At cap K=4, the mean is exactly 0. A head by the cap leaves score 1. The one all-tail continuation leaves a negative score. The terminal score with no deterministic cap is 1 almost surely; these are different random variables.

A “paper amount” is the magnitude assigned to an unresolved toss, not the number of tosses. These exact means count every continuation; no simulated trials, discarded tails, or financial constraints are part of this mathematical model.

Increasing the cap to 10 gives no-head probability 1/1024 and score −1023. Their product is −1023/1024: a small probability is not enough to discard a score from an expectation.

Two finite facts, then an infinite conclusion

  1. The stopped score is a martingale. This means it is integrable at each fixed time and, given the whole history so far, its next conditional mean is its current value. If a head has already occurred, the next score stays 1. Otherwise, after n tails, the next two equally likely scores are 1 and 1−2n+1. Their average is 1−2n, the current score. At any fixed n there are only finitely many scores, so integrability holds. Iterating this one-step equality gives E[Gn]=0.
  2. The stopping time is almost surely finite and has finite mean. The event T>n is exactly n initial tails, with probability 2−n. These probabilities tend to 0, so P(T<∞)=1. Also P(T=t)=2−t for t≥1. Counting one unit for each toss that is needed gives E[T]=∑n≥0P(T>n)=1+1/2+1/4+⋯=2. All terms in this tail sum are nonnegative.
  3. The terminal score is 1 almost surely. A first head at time t adds 2t−1 after total previous subtraction 2t−1−1. Therefore GT=1 on every finite stopping path. Set its value arbitrarily, say 0, on the probability-zero infinite all-tail sequence. The terminal score is integrable and E[GT]=1.

Almost-sure stopping gives GK→GT almost surely as the deterministic cap K increases. This does not imply convergence of expectations.

The exact place where the limiting argument fails

At any fixed cap K, the score is 1 with probability 1−2−K, and 1−2K with probability 2−K. The second branch includes paths whose first head comes later; it has not been discarded. The two contributions to the mean cancel:

E[GK]=(1−2−K)+(1−2K)2−K=0

The rare branch has probability tending to 0 but magnitude tending to infinity. Its negative contribution tends to −1. More decisively, on T≤K the current and terminal scores agree; on T>K they differ by exactly 2K almost surely. The assigned value on the probability-zero infinite all-tail sequence does not affect an expectation. Hence, even at K=0,

E[|GK−GT|]=2K2−K=1

The mean absolute error never shrinks. Thus the convergence is not in L1, which means convergence in mean absolute error. In particular, E[lim GK]=1 while lim E[GK]=0. One cannot interchange expectation and this limit.

Which optional-stopping assumptions fail?

For martingales, sufficient optional-stopping hypotheses include a bounded stopping time; an almost-surely finite stop with a common process bound; finite E[T] with uniformly bounded increments; or uniform integrability of the stopped family. These are alternative sufficient conditions for equality of initial and stopped expectations.

No deterministic bound holds: P(T>K)>0 for every K. Scores 1−2K have no common bound. After n unresolved tails, the next increment has magnitude 2n, also unbounded. Neither E[T]=2 nor terminal-score integrability supplies the missing hypothesis.

Uniform integrability means that the expected absolute contribution from scores above a sufficiently high magnitude cutoff can be made small uniformly over all caps. It fails here. For any cutoff C>1, choose larger and larger K with 2K−1>C. The no-head branch alone contributes (2K−1)2−K=1−2−K to that absolute tail expectation, approaching 1. There is also no single integrable random variable dominating every |GK|; such a bound would give convergence of expectations by dominated convergence, contrary to the exact calculation.

Even the unconditional means of absolute increments are bounded: E|Gn+1−Gn|=2−n2n=1. This is weaker than a common bound on their conditional means given the past, which equal 2n on the unresolved history. It is not the conditional-increment hypothesis in that more general stopping theorem.

Bounded stopping gives mean 0 at every finite cap. Passing to the unbounded stop fails without tail control. In the fair-walk lesson, increments are bounded but mean absorption time is infinite; here mean stopping time is finite but increments grow. Check the actual hypotheses in each case.

Try two cases

Exercise 1. A game gives 5 points with chance 1/4 and loses 1 point otherwise. Find its expected score. Then subtract a two-point entry fee. Can either mean occur as a single-play net score?

Hint for exercise 1

The remaining chance is 3/4. Use −1 for the loss. A fixed fee is subtracted on every outcome.

Full solution to exercise 1

The mean before the fee is 5 × (1/4) + (−1) × (3/4) = 1/2 point. After the fee it is 1/2 − 2 = −3/2 points. You can check directly: net scores are 3 and −3, so their mean is 3 × (1/4) + (−3) × (3/4) = −3/2. Neither mean is an available single-play score.

Exercise 2. Roll one fair die. Let X be its face number and let Y be seven minus that number. Knowing X determines Y, so these scores are dependent. Find the means of X, Y and their sum. Does dependence break the addition rule?

Hint for exercise 2

In face order, Y takes values 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1. What is X + Y on every roll?

Full solution to exercise 2

Both scores have mean (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 21/6 = 7/2. Their sum is always 7, so its mean is 7 too. Thus E[X + Y] = 7 = 7/2 + 7/2. Dependence does not break addition. For example, learning X = 1 forces Y = 6, but the weighted sums still add.

Optional tasks: audit the cap, then change the growth rule

Three tasks for the stopping investigation

Task A: two meanings of “short”. At cap K=3, find the exact score law, expected number of tosses used and expected total of the amounts assigned to those tosses. An amount is counted positively whether the toss adds or subtracts it. Why can the first expectation stay below 2 as the second grows without bound?

Hint for task A

The third toss is used only after two tails. Count amounts 1, 2 and 4 with their respective probabilities of being used.

Full solution to task A

The score is 1 with probability 7/8 and −7 with probability 1/8, so its mean is 0. The mean toss count is 1+1/2+1/4=7/4. The mean total amount is 1×1+2×(1/2)+4×(1/4)=3. More generally, toss k is used with probability 2−(k−1). Each used amount has expected contribution 2k−12−(k−1)=1, so the mean total through cap K is K. In contrast, the mean toss count is 2(1−2−K)<2. Time and score magnitude are different quantities.

Task B: change the mathematics. Keep independent fair tosses and stop at the first head, but replace doubling by growth factor 3/2: amounts 1, 3/2, 9/4, …. Rational paper scores are allowed. Derive the score at the first head. What is the probability that the terminal score is positive? Find its mean and explain why it exists. Does the terminal mean still differ from every capped mean?

Hint for task B

For a=3/2, the subtraction before toss t is (1+a+⋯+at−2)=2(at−1−1), including a zero empty sum when t=1. The first-head probabilities are still 2−t. Sum a geometric series with ratio 3/4.

Full solution to task B

The terminal score is HT=2−(3/2)T−1. The first three possible values are 1, 1/2 and −1/4; later values are more negative. Thus positivity means T≤2 and has probability 1/2+1/4=3/4. Also E[(3/2)T−1]=(1/2)∑j≥0(3/4)j=2. The bound |HT|≤2+(3/2)T−1 proves integrability, so subtraction of these expectations is legitimate and E[HT]=0. Every capped mean is also 0, by the same one-step fair conditional-mean argument. A terminal score is positive with probability 3/4 here, yet its mean is zero.

Let D sum the positive amounts used up to T. Then E[D]=∑k≥1(3/2)k−1P(T≥k)=∑k≥1(3/4)k−1=4. Every absolute capped score is bounded by D, so dominated convergence applies. Unbounded increments alone do not force failure: the stated stopping conditions are sufficient, and this model has another integrable bound.

Task C: quantify the changed limit. For the 3/2 rule in task B, first show E|HT|=5/4. Then find E|HK−HT|. Does it tend to zero? You may use independence of future tosses after a fixed initial string of K tails.

Hint for task C

The positive part has contributions 1×(1/2)+(1/2)×(1/4)=5/8. The mean is 0, so the negative part has the same expected magnitude. On the no-head-by-K branch, let S be the additional waiting time for a head.

Full solution to task C

The positive and negative parts both have expectation 5/8, hence E|HT|=5/4. After K tails the current score is HK=2−2(3/2)K. The fresh delay S has the original first-head law. Subtracting the two scores gives HT−HK=(3/2)K[2−(3/2)S−1] on this branch; the difference is zero on all other branches. Multiplying its conditional absolute mean by probability 2−K gives E|HK−HT|=(5/4)(3/4)K→0. This is convergence in L1, unlike the doubling rule's constant error 1.

A good place to stop

Separate outcomes, scores and their probability-weighted mean. Explain why the carnival's three score labels cannot be averaged equally. To compare equal-mean games with different spread, continue to variance.

A fixed cap and an almost-surely finite stop can have different limiting expectations. Track the rare branch's weighted contribution: its small probability and E[T]=2 did not control the doubling example's score tails.
