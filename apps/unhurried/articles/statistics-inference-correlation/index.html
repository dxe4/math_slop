Does a shared trend reveal a shared cause?

Track umbrella drawings and rainy days on a shared calendar. The question is what the observed numbers allow us to say, and where the tempting shortcut fails.

Background capsule. A scatterplot places each paired observation at its two measured values. Correlation r summarizes linear association after centering and scaling both variables. Positive r means larger x values tend to pair with larger y values in a linear sense; it does not specify a mechanism. Both variables must vary for the usual r denominator to be nonzero.

Worked example

Across four fictional days, a club records rainy-hour counts x=(0,1,2,3) and umbrella sketches y=(1,2,3,4). The averages are x-bar=1.5 and y-bar=2.5. Centered deviations are the same sequence, (−1.5,−0.5,0.5,1.5). The cross-product sum and both squared-deviation sums are 5, so r=5/sqrt(5×5)=1. Yet drawing umbrellas did not cause the rain. The weather plausibly affects both the opportunity or mood to sketch umbrellas and the measured rainy hours. Correlation captures an arrangement of pairs, not an arrow of influence.

For the four rainy days, the numerator is 5 because deviations in rainy hours and umbrella sketches have matching signs and magnitudes. The denominator is also 5. The computation is exact for these four paired observations; changing any one pair could change the coefficient substantially.

Worked comparison: change the measurement unit. Double every sketch count in the four-day example, giving y=(2,4,6,8). Its mean doubles from 2.5 to 5 and every centered y deviation doubles. The cross-product numerator therefore becomes 10, while the two squared-deviation sums are 5 and 20. The new correlation is 10/√(5×20)=1. The slope and the numerical counts changed, but the standardized linear arrangement did not. This invariance concerns a positive change of scale. Multiplying every y by a negative number would reverse its order and change the correlation's sign.

The claim and its conditions

r=∑(xi−x¯)(yi−y¯)∑(xi−x¯)2∑(yi−y¯)2

The numerator adds paired products of deviations. A pair above both averages contributes positively; a pair above one average but below the other contributes negatively. The denominator makes r dimensionless, so changing minutes to hours for one variable without reversing its order does not change r. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality bounds r between −1 and 1 when both centered sums are positive. Equality at r=1 means all centered pairs lie on a positive-slope line. It does not imply the line is a causal law, that it will hold in new data, or that all possible relationships are linear.

Proof boundary and failure case. A single unusual pair can dominate r, and a curved relationship can have r near zero. For example x=(−1,0,1), y=(1,0,1) has a clear U shape but cross-product sum zero and r=0. Scatterplots and context are needed before interpreting the coefficient. No calculation of r from observational pairs alone identifies a causal effect.

One possible causal account is that rain encourages umbrella drawings. Another is that a third calendar factor affects both counts, or that the fictional club schedules drawing sessions on rainy days. The same r=1 does not choose among these stories. A design that separates alternatives is needed for a causal question.

Try two checks

1. Calculation. For pairs (1,2), (2,4), (3,6), calculate r and state only the supported geometric conclusion.

Hint for exercise 1

Check whether centered y deviations are a positive constant times centered x deviations.

Full solution to exercise 1

The x deviations are −1,0,1; the y deviations are −2,0,2. Numerator is 4; squared sums are 2 and 8, giving r=4/sqrt(16)=1. The three observed points lie exactly on a positive-slope line. The calculation alone says nothing about causation.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. For x=(−1,0,1) and y=(1,0,1), calculate r and describe what a scatterplot adds.

Hint for exercise 2

The means are 0 for x and 2/3 for y; calculate the cross-product sum.

Full solution to exercise 2

The cross-product sum is (−1)(1/3)+0(−2/3)+(1)(1/3)=0. The denominator is positive, so r=0. A scatterplot reveals the U shape: y is high at both x extremes and low in the middle. Zero linear correlation has not shown absence of association.

The U-shaped counterexample has zero cross-product because the left and right contributions cancel. Its scatterplot still gives a deterministic pattern. This is why a plot should precede the sentence “there is no relationship.”

What you can carry forward

Read r with a scatterplot, variable definitions, and a plausible data-generating account. A coefficient can efficiently summarize linear arrangement, but cannot replace shape checks, sampling design, or a causal design.

Correlation is undefined if every x or every y value is identical, because the denominator is zero. Reporting r=0 in that case would incorrectly suggest evidence about a lack of linear association; there is no variation to assess it.
