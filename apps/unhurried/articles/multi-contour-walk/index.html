Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

A map of height without a picture of the hill

A contour joins points with the same height. If a walker follows it, does the height change? If the walker instead crosses it, what can the map tell us about the change? We can answer exactly for the landscape f(x,y)=x²+2y², and learn where a contour map stops short of measuring slope.

Background capsule. A point (x,y) records east and north coordinates. Squaring removes signs: x² is the same at x and −x. A level set of f at height c is the collection of points satisfying f(x,y)=c. A displacement (a,b) means moving a units east and b units north. The derivative of a one-variable function measures a limiting height change per unit of that movement; a two-variable slope must also specify a direction.

Walk around and across an oval

Worked example. At height 2 our level set satisfies x²+2y²=2. Its east and west tips are (±√2,0); its north and south tips are (0,±1). At (1,1/√2) the same equation holds, so these points all lie on one oval. Moving among them changes position but leaves height at 2. The axes have different spans because a unit of northward distance contributes twice as much squared height as a unit eastward distance.

Now start at (1,0), where height is 1. An eastward step of length h changes height to (1+h)², a difference of 2h+h². A northward step of the same length changes it to 1+2h², a difference of 2h². At h=1/10 those changes are 21/100 and 2/100. Near this point eastward movement has a first-order effect; northward movement has only a second-order effect. What the level equation proves

Exact statement. For every c>0, the level set is an ellipse with horizontal semiaxis √c and vertical semiaxis √(c/2). Divide its equation by c to obtain x²/c+y²/(c/2)=1. At c=0 there is only the origin; at c<0 there are no real points because both squares are nonnegative. This algebra proves the shape and intercepts, assuming ordinary Cartesian coordinates with equal physical units on both axes.

f(x,y)=c⟺x2c+y2c/2=1

For a smooth path r(t)=(x(t),y(t)) kept on one contour, f(r(t))=c at every t; therefore its derivative is zero wherever the path is differentiable. This is an exact claim about travel along a level set. It does not say that every move from a point has zero slope. In fact, at (1,0), moving east by h produces (2h+h²)/h=2+h, tending to 2, while moving north produces 2h²/h=2h, tending to zero.

Beware a tempting visual shortcut. The distance between contours of heights 1 and 2 depends on which route crosses them, on whether the heights were sampled at equal increments, and on the scale of the map. Close drawn curves suggest rapid change only after those choices are fixed. A finite gap is an average change, not a derivative at a point. The next lesson extracts all first-order directions from a gradient.

Use the level equation

1. Calculation. Find the intercepts of the height-8 contour. Starting at (2,0), compare the height changes from steps (1/4,0) and (0,1/4).

Hint for question 1

Set one coordinate to zero for intercepts; substitute the two new points directly.

Worked solution for question 1

x²+2y²=8 gives (±2√2,0) and (0,±2). At (2,0) height is 4. East gives (9/4)²=81/16, a rise of 17/16. North gives 4+2(1/4)²=33/8, a rise of 1/8. The east step rises more.

2. Explanation. A map draws the height-1 and height-4 contours. A reader says their spacing alone determines the directional derivative at (1,0). Give two reasons this fails.

Hint for question 2

Ask what direction and what limiting operation a derivative requires.

Worked solution for question 2

A directional derivative specifies a direction: at (1,0) the east and north derivatives are 2 and 0. It also takes a limit as the step shrinks to zero. Two separated contours provide only finite changes. Their drawn spacing additionally depends on map scaling, so it cannot supply either missing ingredient.

What the map lets you conclude

A level equation locates exactly where the height agrees, and its algebra reveals the shape for this quadratic landscape. Equal-length steps can still produce very different height changes. Stop once you can distinguish a level from a slope and calculate a finite difference before claiming a limiting one. As a failure case, a constant function has every point on one level set and zero slope everywhere; our oval geometry does not apply to it. Continue with the gradient when you want a rule for all directions at once.
