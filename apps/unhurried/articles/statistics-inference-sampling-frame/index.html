Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

Which club members did the poll actually reach?

A club polls only people who attend a meeting. The question is what the observed numbers allow us to say, and where the tempting shortcut fails.

Background capsule. A percentage is a part divided by a stated whole. A population is the set about which the question is asked; a sampling frame is the list or mechanism from which people can be contacted. A sample is the people who actually supply data. These three sets need not coincide.

Worked example

A fictional drawing club has 100 members: 40 attend a Saturday meeting and 60 do not. Of the attendees, 30 prefer pencils; among nonattendees, only 12 do. A meeting-only poll sees 30 pencil votes among 40 responses, giving 75%. The whole club has 42 pencil preferences among 100 members, giving 42%. Both arithmetic statements are true. Only the latter describes all members in this invented census. The 33-point difference arose because attendance and preference were associated.

Notice that 30/40 has a complete count of attendees in its denominator, while 42/100 has a complete count of members. The numerators differ because the latter includes twelve pencil preferences among the sixty people who did not attend. Calling both numbers “the club percentage” conceals the change in who was counted.

The claim and its conditions

pencil repliesall replies=3040=0.75

Write I=1 when a member enters the sample and Y=1 when that member prefers pencils. The observed 30/40 estimates a preference among attendees, P(Y=1|I=1), if every attendee answers honestly. The club-wide quantity is P(Y=1). Equality would need a justified selection condition, such as the special fact that preference has the same rate inside and outside the frame. Simply increasing the number of Saturday respondents does not add absent members. A random draw from the attendees can reduce random fluctuation within that frame while leaving the frame mismatch intact. Nonresponse creates another selection stage: being invited is not the same as answering.

Proof boundary and failure case. No universal adjustment follows from the meeting poll alone. If the 60 nonattendees could have any pencil rate from 0% to 100%, the population count could range from 30 to 90, hence the population proportion from 30% to 90%. The observed 75% therefore does not pin down the target. Weighting works only with credible group sizes and within-group measurements or assumptions.

If the club selected members with unequal known inclusion chances, an appropriately weighted estimator could target the full membership without making the raw respondent rate equal the population rate. That still requires coverage, measured inclusion chances, and attention to nonresponse. In this example, no such design information is supplied, so the meeting fraction cannot be repaired by a formula alone.

A fixed population and a random sampling design

The club example has no stated probability-sampling mechanism. We now supply one for a different, fully specified four-member population. The fixed outcomes are (y₁,y₂,y₃,y₄)=(1,1,0,0), so its fixed mean is ȳ=2/4=1/2. We reveal this small teaching census so we can check the estimators against their target. A real sample would reveal only the included members' outcomes.

Extra background. A sampling design gives probabilities to possible subsets. Repeating that design hypothetically keeps every yᵢ fixed and varies which subset is observed. The design expectation Ed[Z] is the probability-weighted average of an estimate Z across those possibilities. Its bias is Ed[Z]−ȳ. Unbiased means that bias is zero; it does not mean every sample gives the right answer. Here there is no prior or posterior distribution on the outcomes. This expectation is over sample choices.

Choose one of four equally likely tickets labelled 12, 12, 13, 24. Ticket 13 means sample members 1 and 3, without replacement, each counted once. The duplicate 12 tickets give the same subset twice as much probability: Pr(S={1,2})=1/2, while Pr(S={1,3})=Pr(S={2,4})=1/4. They do not create four different subsets or count any selected member twice.

A member's inclusion chance is πᵢ=Pr(i∈S), obtained by adding the probabilities of all subsets containing that member. Thus π₁=π₂=3/4 and π₃=π₄=1/4. These are not the probabilities of the particular sampled subset. The inclusion chances add to 2, the fixed sample size, rather than to 1.

Three estimators with two different denominators

Let Ŷ=∑i∈Syᵢ/πᵢ be the inverse-inclusion weighted total and N̂=∑i∈S1/πᵢ the corresponding estimate of population size. We know that the actual population size is N=4. Compare the ordinary sample mean R, the Horvitz–Thompson mean H that divides Ŷ by known N, and the normalized inverse-weight, or Hájek, mean A that divides Ŷ by random N̂.

R=12∑i∈Syi,H=14∑i∈Syiπi,A=∑i∈Syi/πi∑i∈S1/πi

Predict before inspecting. Will all three estimates have design expectation 1/2? For subset 12, does the weight sum equal the known population size? When you inspect another sample, which quantities should change and which must stay fixed?

One realization is different from a design expectation. The fixed target stays 1/2. The complete-law expectations are E[R]=3/4, E[H]=1/2, E[A]=5/8. Even an unbiased estimator need not equal the target in one sample.

The instrument enumerates the complete four-ticket design exactly. It generates no observed data. Local tables can be focused and scrolled with arrow keys on narrow screens. The changed population in exercise 3 is deliberately outside these controls.

The exact four-ticket averages

The raw estimates for subsets 12,13,24 are 1,1/2,1/2, so Ed[R]=(1/2)1+(1/4)(1/2)+(1/4)(1/2)=3/4. Members with outcome 1 are more likely to be included. The HT estimates are 2/3,1/3,1/3; their expectation is (1/2)(2/3)+(1/4)(1/3)+(1/4)(1/3)=1/2.

The normalized estimates are 1,1/4,1/4, giving Ed[A]=1/2+1/16+1/16=5/8. Normalization produces a weighted average between the observed outcomes, but it changes the unbiasedness calculation. Here Ŷ takes values 8/3,4/3,4/3 with expectation 2; N̂ takes values 8/3,16/3,16/3 with expectation 4. The mean of their ratio is 5/8, while the ratio of their means is 2/4=1/2. A random denominator cannot generally be moved outside an expectation.

Why the known-size HT mean is unbiased

For a general fixed population of N members, assume each inclusion chance πᵢ is known and positive and each included outcome is measured. Let Iᵢ be 1 if member i is included and 0 otherwise. Since Ed[Iᵢ]=πᵢ, linearity gives

Ed[H]=1N∑i=1NEd[Ii]yiπi=1N∑i=1Nyi=y¯

No independence of the inclusion indicators is required. They are dependent here because every sample contains exactly two members. The actual πᵢ, not an arbitrary convenient set of weights, cancel the expectation. This proves a repeated-design property for every fixed outcome vector. It supplies no posterior probability statement after one sample.

Accuracy needs a separate calculation. Mean squared error is MSEd(Z)=Ed[(Z−ȳ)²]. In this teaching census, the full design gives MSE(R)=1/8, MSE(H)=1/36, and MSE(A)=5/32. Those are exact calculations from the revealed census and all possible samples. They are not variance estimates available from one realized sample. Exercise 3 changes the census and reverses which estimator has the smaller MSE.

Coverage boundary. The proof needs πᵢ>0 for every member whose unknown outcome contributes to the target. If some are never observed, no inverse weight can recover their unrestricted outcomes. Also distinguish first-order inclusion πᵢ from pair inclusion πᵢⱼ=Pr(i and j both enter S). Here π₁₄=π₂₃=π₃₄=0. These zeros do not affect the first-order unbiasedness proof, but the usual sample-based unbiased variance formula involving division by πᵢⱼ cannot be used for those pairs. We make no such variance-estimation claim.

Source scope. Zong, Zhu and Zou, §2 defines the finite-population HT total; dividing it by known N gives our mean. Their §4.1 constructs a ratio of HT totals; setting the auxiliary variable to one yields A. Oliva-Aviles, Meyer and Opsomer, equation (2.2) names that normalized estimator Hájek. We use its definition, without importing that paper's positive pair-inclusion or asymptotic assumptions. The finite design, calculations and exercises here are original.

This fixed-population experiment differs from random hidden-bag draws, where dependence changes a sampling distribution, and from conditioning on a report, where likelihoods change posterior world weights. In the queue observation lesson, normalizing exact inverse-rate probability weights recovers an exact clock law. That identity for complete laws does not claim that a ratio computed from a finite sample is unbiased.

Try two checks

1. Calculation. Suppose a club has 80 members, 20 attend, and 16 attending members say yes. Ten of the 60 absent members later say yes in a complete follow-up. Find the meeting rate and the club rate.

Hint for exercise 1

Keep the two denominators separate: 20 for the meeting and 80 for the club.

Full solution to exercise 1

The meeting rate is 16/20=80%. The complete club count is 16+10=26 yes responses, so the club rate is 26/80=32.5%. The meeting result is 47.5 percentage points higher.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. A second club polls every person on its email list, but the list excludes people who joined by paper form. Does a 100% response rate from the list ensure a representative estimate?

Hint for exercise 2

Name the target population, frame, and response set.

Full solution to exercise 2

No. The target includes every member, while the frame and response set contain only email-listed members. Perfect response removes nonresponse inside that frame; it cannot reveal the excluded members’ preferences. If paper-form members differ, the frame estimate differs from the club estimate.

One further check is to bound the unknown total before accepting a claimed correction. With no data about absent members, at least the 30 known pencil preferences remain and at most all 60 absent people add to them. That yields the 30%–90% range, wide enough to include many different club stories.

Two further tasks for the optional weighting investigation

3. Change the fixed population, keep the design. Set (y₁,y₂,y₃,y₄)=(1,0,1,0), still with samples 12,13,24 of probabilities 1/2,1/4,1/4. Without using an explorer preset, find the fixed target and all three estimator values for each sample. Compute each design expectation and MSE. Does an unbiased estimate of this population proportion have to lie in [0,1]? If H is clipped to 1 whenever it exceeds 1, what is its new expectation?

Hint for exercise 3

The πᵢ stay unchanged. For sample 13 the weighted numerator is 1/(3/4)+1/(1/4)=16/3. Divide by known 4 for H and by the weight sum 16/3 for A. Square each error around the fixed target before averaging it with the design probabilities.

Full solution to exercise 3

The new target is still (1+0+1+0)/4=1/2. In order 12,13,24, R takes values (1/2,1,0), H takes (1/3,4/3,0), and A takes (1/2,1,0). Thus Ed[R]=Ed[A]=(1/2)(1/2)+(1/4)1=1/2, and Ed[H]=(1/2)(1/3)+(1/4)(4/3)=1/2. The raw and normalized estimators happen to be unbiased for this particular population. That does not prove unbiasedness for every fixed population; the original (1,1,0,0) already disproves it.

MSE(R)=MSE(A)=(1/2)0²+(1/4)(1/2)²+(1/4)(−1/2)²=1/8. For H, MSE(H)=(1/2)(−1/6)²+(1/4)(5/6)²+(1/4)(−1/2)²=1/4. An unbiased estimator can have larger mean squared error than another estimator. The value H=4/3 is a possible estimate of the fixed proportion, not a probability assigned to an event; known-N inverse weighting is not constrained to be a convex average of observed outcomes. Clipping changes its three values to (1/3,1,0), whose expectation is 1/6+1/4=5/12. Clipping has destroyed unbiasedness.

4. A frame that never reaches two members. Change the design so the only possible sample is 12. The sample reveals y₁=y₂=1. Compare fixed populations (1,1,0,0) and (1,1,1,1). What are their means and inclusion probabilities? Could any estimator using only the observed sample and this design be unbiased for the population mean for every binary outcome vector? Explain rather than assigning infinite weights.

Hint for exercise 4

Ask whether either possible population can change the observed data. An expectation over a design with one possible sample is the estimate for that sample.

Full solution to exercise 4

The means are 1/2 and 1, while the design has π=(1,1,0,0) in either case. Every possible observation is identical: members 1 and 2 with outcomes 1 and 1. Any sample-only estimator therefore has the same value, and hence the same design expectation, under the two populations. It cannot equal both targets. Even adding independent randomization would give the same distribution under both populations. The missing outcomes are not identified from this frame without extra assumptions; dividing by zero cannot repair that missing information. This is the finite-population version of the original club's frame problem.

What you can carry forward

Before trusting a percentage, write down who could enter the numerator and denominator. This is a design question before it is a calculation question. The example establishes the possibility of selection bias, not its size in a real club. A useful next step is to plan a sampling frame that covers the target and record how invitation and response occur.

The 75% estimate is perfectly accurate for the forty attendees because everyone there was counted. The error appears only when its label silently changes to all members. An honest report can state the narrow attendee result while seeking a better frame for the wider question.
