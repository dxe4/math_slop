Question and background

A globe cannot be covered by one ordinary latitude-longitude grid without trouble at the poles. A local surface patch gives two coordinates for a piece of a surface, and its derivatives tell us whether the grid really describes a two-dimensional piece there.

Local capsule. A map X(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)) takes a region of the coordinate plane into space. Partial derivative Xᵤ changes position as u changes with v fixed; Xᵥ does the same for v. Their cross product is perpendicular to both, and it vanishes exactly when they are linearly dependent. For a regular patch, we require a smooth map that is locally one-to-one, with Xᵤ and Xᵥ independent at every point in its domain.

Coordinates are names, not the surface itself. A coordinate singularity can reflect a failed naming scheme while the underlying sphere stays smooth. Checking the derivative vectors keeps us from mistaking a grid collapse for a physical spike.

A worked example

Worked example. For a sphere of radius R>0, use colatitude θ and longitude λ: X(θ,λ)=(Rsinθ cosλ,Rsinθ sinλ,Rcosθ). Restrict 0<θ<π and choose an open longitude interval shorter than 2π. Then Xθ=(Rcosθ cosλ,Rcosθ sinλ,-Rsinθ) and Xλ=(-Rsinθ sinλ,Rsinθ cosλ,0).

The two derivatives are orthogonal: their dot product is zero. Their lengths are R and Rsinθ, respectively. Consequently ||Xθ×Xλ||=R²sinθ>0 on the stated domain. This is a regular coordinate patch there. At θ=0 or π, Xλ=(0,0,0): changing longitude no longer changes the pole. The coordinate grid collapses. A different chart centered at the pole can describe a smooth neighborhood, so the sphere itself has no corner at either pole.

A simpler patch is a graph X(u,v)=(u,v,f(u,v)). Its derivatives are (1,0,fᵤ) and (0,1,fᵥ); their cross product is (-fᵤ,-fᵥ,1), never zero when f is differentiable. Graphs therefore supply many local patches, including a small neighborhood of a sphere pole written as z=√(R²-u²-v²) on u²+v²<R².

The statement and its boundary

Exact rank test. A differentiable parametrization X on an open region is regular at a point if its two tangent vectors are linearly independent there. In ℝ³ that is equivalent to:

Xu×Xv≠0

The cross-product equivalence is elementary linear algebra: two vectors have zero cross product exactly when their spanned parallelogram has zero area. For the sphere calculation, orthogonality and the two derivative lengths give cross-product magnitude R²sinθ, which proves the rank claim away from poles. A full surface atlas would also organize overlapping charts and their smooth transition maps. We use the local rank test here, without claiming that one patch covers the entire sphere.

Rank alone does not guarantee a globally one-to-one map. Longitudes differing by 2π name the same point, so a complete 0≤λ≤2π strip duplicates a seam even where rank is nonzero. Restricting the coordinate domain avoids that duplication locally. At a true cone tip, by contrast, the surface is not smoothly patchable in this sense; a vanished derivative cannot always be repaired by a new chart.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. a graph patch. For X(u,v)=(u,v,u²−v²), calculate Xᵤ×Xᵥ and decide where it is regular.

Hint for exercise 1

Use Xᵤ=(1,0,2u) and Xᵥ=(0,1,-2v).

Full solution to exercise 1

The cross product is (-2u,2v,1). Its third component is always one, so it never vanishes. Thus every point in any open (u,v) domain is regular. At (0,0), the two tangent directions are (1,0,0) and (0,1,0), and the tangent plane is horizontal.

Exercise 2. a coordinate collapse. Consider the cylinder X(u,v)=(cos u,sin u,v). Is u=0 a singularity? What happens if u ranges across a full 2π interval?

Hint for exercise 2

Compare nonzero rank with duplicate coordinate names.

Full solution to exercise 2

Xᵤ=(-sin u,cos u,0), Xᵥ=(0,0,1), and Xᵤ×Xᵥ=(cos u,sin u,0), with norm one everywhere. In particular u=0 is regular. But u and u+2π name the same cylinder point, so a closed full-period domain repeats a seam. A smaller open angular interval gives a genuine local patch.

What to carry forward

Two independent derivative directions make a local coordinate grid into a regular surface patch. Coordinate rank failure at a sphere pole and repeated longitudes are different issues: one collapses a derivative, the other duplicates names. Both warn us to restrict or replace coordinates before calculation.

The derivative vectors also carry measurements. In the surface metric, their dot products determine lengths of paths drawn in the patch. Nothing about the grid's visual spacing alone tells us those lengths accurately.
