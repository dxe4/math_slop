Question and background

A sphere can be made larger or smaller, so its curvature at each point changes. Yet the total curvature obtained by integrating over the whole sphere stays fixed. Gauss–Bonnet connects that total with the Euler characteristic, a topological count that does not depend on radius.

Local capsule. Gaussian curvature K is the product of principal curvatures of a smooth surface. The area element dA records physical surface area; for a patch it is ||Xᵤ×Xᵥ||du dv. The Euler characteristic χ can be computed from a suitable cell decomposition as V−E+F, with vertices, edges and faces counted once after identification. For a sphere χ=2. A “closed surface” here is compact and without boundary; an oriented surface has a consistent choice of normal direction.

Gauss–Bonnet is a theorem about a complete surface under those conditions, not a local identity for any convenient coordinate rectangle. Boundary versions include geodesic-curvature and corner-angle terms. Omitting them changes the answer.

A worked example

Worked example. On a round sphere of radius R>0, K=1/R² everywhere. Its area is 4πR². Since K is constant, ∫K dA=(1/R²)(4πR²)=4π. A tetrahedral cell decomposition of a sphere has V=4, E=6 and F=4, so χ=4−6+4=2 and 2πχ=4π. The two calculations agree for every positive R.

As a second model, a standard embedded torus has χ=0, and Gauss–Bonnet predicts total signed curvature zero. The torus has positively and negatively curved regions, whose integrals cancel. This prediction is conditional on the theorem; a single sketch of a doughnut does not compute the integral.

Why does the tetrahedron count apply to a smooth sphere? The boundary of a tetrahedron is topologically a sphere, and Euler characteristic is unchanged under suitable subdivisions. Proving full topological invariance requires more work than counting one model. Here the topological fact χ(S²)=2 is supplied as background, while the round-sphere curvature integral is calculated in full.

See the torus cancellation in an integral

A round torus with major radius a and tube radius b, where a>b>0, has parametrization X(u,v)=((a+bcos u)cos v,(a+bcos u)sin v,bsin u). Each parameter runs through one full turn, with opposite edges of the parameter square identified. Its first fundamental coefficients are E=b², F=0 and G=(a+bcos u)², so the area element is b(a+bcos u) du dv. The factor a+bcos u stays positive by a>b.

With outward normal (cos u cos v,cos u sin v,sin u), the second fundamental coefficients are e=−b, f=0 and g=−(a+bcos u)cos u. The local curvature formula therefore gives K=cos u/[b(a+bcos u)]. Multiplying K by the area element cancels the denominator and leaves cos u du dv. Integrating for 0≤u,v≤2π gives (∫₀2πcos u du)(∫₀2πdv)=0·2π=0.

This computes the cancellation that a doughnut sketch only suggests. The outer region has cos u positive and the inner region negative. The total agrees with 2πχ=0 for a torus, whose usual one-vertex, two-edge, one-face decomposition gives χ=1−2+1=0. These are calculations for the round torus; they still do not prove the theorem for every compact surface.

The statement and its boundary

Stated global theorem. If M is a compact, oriented, smooth two-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary, then:

∫MKdA=2πχ(M)

The round-sphere calculation above verifies this theorem in one example; it is not a proof for arbitrary M. A general proof may triangulate the surface, relate curvature and boundary turning within pieces, and cancel internal-edge contributions. Carrying that out rigorously needs local Gauss–Bonnet, controlled triangulation and topology. Those steps are deferred. Our exact claims here are the theorem statement with hypotheses, the sphere area and curvature calculation, and the combinatorial χ value for a sphere model.

Orientation is stated to fit the standard theorem and its area form. There are formulations for nonorientable surfaces using an unsigned area measure, but we do not silently substitute those. If a surface has a boundary, the displayed formula alone generally fails; it needs boundary curvature and possible corner terms. If a surface is noncompact, convergence and ends must be addressed separately.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. scale a sphere. A round sphere has radius 7. Find K, area, total curvature and the Gauss–Bonnet right side.

Hint for exercise 1

Use K=1/R², area=4πR² and χ(S²)=2.

Full solution to exercise 1

K=1/49 and area=4π·49=196π. Therefore ∫K dA=(1/49)(196π)=4π. The right side is 2πχ=2π·2=4π. Radius cancels because local curvature decreases exactly as area increases.

Exercise 2. an invalid shortcut. Someone applies ∫K dA=2πχ to a flat unit disk and concludes 0=2π. Identify the missing hypothesis and the correcting phenomenon.

Hint for exercise 2

Does a disk have a boundary? What turns along its circular edge?

Full solution to exercise 2

A disk has a boundary, so the closed-surface theorem does not apply. Its interior Gaussian curvature is zero, but its boundary curve has total geodesic curvature 2π in the flat plane. The boundary version gives 0+2π=2πχ(D²), and χ(D²)=1. The original contradiction came from dropping a required boundary term.

What to carry forward

Gauss–Bonnet turns an integral of local curvature into the topological number 2πχ under precise global conditions. The round-sphere example is a complete calculation; the general theorem is deliberately stated without a full proof. It is useful only when its compactness, orientability and no-boundary hypotheses are checked.

For a related consequence, parallel transport around a spherical triangle detects enclosed curvature as a rotation. That application also needs an orientation convention and a warning about rotations counted modulo a full turn.
