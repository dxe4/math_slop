When does a single-server queue have a steady size?

Visitors arrive at rate λ to one artist serving at rate μ when busy. Independent exponential arrival gaps and service times, with arrivals independent of service, define the M/M/1 queue: memoryless arrivals, memoryless service, one server. A stationary distribution exists only for λ<μ.

Background capsule. The state n counts people in the system, including the one being served. At n≥1, an arrival increases n by one at rate λ and a service completion decreases n by one at rate μ. At n=0 there is no service completion. A rate is an average event count per unit time, so 1/μ is mean service time. The dimensionless load ρ=λ/μ compares demand with capacity. A stationary distribution is a list πₙ that is unchanged by the queue's stochastic evolution.

Assume unlimited waiting space, first-come first-served service, no abandonment, and λ,μ>0. Observation comparisons require λ<μ. At λ=0 the empty stationary clock law exists, but no ongoing arrival/change record can be normalized. Real desks may violate these assumptions. Clock-time fractions and event-record fractions need not agree.

A postcard desk at half load

Worked example. Let λ=2 arrivals per hour and μ=4 completions per busy hour, so ρ=1/2. In the stationary M/M/1 model the chance of n people in the system is (1−ρ)ρⁿ. Thus P(0)=1/2, P(1)=1/4, P(2)=1/8, P(3)=1/16. The chance of at least three people is ρ³=1/8, since the geometric tail sums to that value. Mean system size is ρ/(1−ρ)=1 person. This is an ensemble or long-run mean under stationarity, not a guarantee that a particular observed line never grows beyond one.

Which clock does the recorder use?

One recorder counts minutes empty; another records size after every actual arrival or departure, weighting rows equally. Should both find 50% empty? An empty queue changes only at arrivals; a busy queue can also change at departures, shortening visits.

A separate finite-index check

Start empty at jump index 0, then count only actual arrivals/departures. At index 2, every reachable state is even. Exact empty probability = 2/3 (≈ 0.666667). The stationary event-frequency value is 1/4 (≈ 0.25).

At every odd index the empty probability is zero. Even and odd laws remain separated by parity. A stationary initialization of the jump chain would instead use α; long-run event frequencies and fixed-index distributions are different claims.

After the first actual change from empty, the queue contains 1 person. After the second, the empty probability is 4/(2+4)=2/3. It is not the stationary event-frequency value 1/4.

Balance neighboring states

ρ=λμ<1,πn=(1−ρ)ρn

In a short time Δt, a state n moves upward with probability approximately λΔt; from n+1 it moves downward with probability approximately μΔt. At stationarity, flow across the edge between n and n+1 balances: πₙλ=πₙ₊₁μ. Therefore πₙ₊₁=ρπₙ and recursively πₙ=ρⁿπ₀. For ρ<1, the geometric series Σₙ≥0ρⁿ=1/(1−ρ), so normalization forces π₀=1−ρ. This derives the stationary probabilities within the M/M/1 model. Summing Σnπₙ gives ρ/(1−ρ); it can be obtained by differentiating the finite geometric sum and taking its limit, valid for ρ<1.

For ρ≥1 the geometric series cannot normalize, so this irreducible birth-death queue has no stationary probability law. At λ=μ it is critical, with no finite stationary mean; individual paths need not steadily grow. At λ>μ demand exceeds capacity and long-run growth is typical. Finite animations do not prove these asymptotic statements.

Failure case. Ten scheduled hourly arrivals and fixed six-minute services both average ten/hour, yet violate memoryless arrivals and service. The geometric M/M/1 law does not follow from mean rates alone. Even at load below one, changing service variability can change waiting behavior.

Derive the event-frequency law

The geometric probabilities π describe the state at an ordinary stationary clock time, equivalently long-run fractions of elapsed time in this stable model. To count actual state changes, let qn be the rate of leaving state n: q0=λ, while qn=λ+μ for n≥1. At 0 no service is completed, because there is nobody to serve.

At stationary time fraction πn, exits from n occur at mean rate πnqn. Inflow equals outflow, so after-change frequencies are proportional to these rates. Their total is λπ0+(λ+μ)(1−π0)=λ(1−ρ)+(λ+μ)ρ=2λ. Therefore:

αn=πnqn2λ

Explicitly α0=(1−ρ)/2 and αn=(1−ρ)(1+ρ)ρn−1/2 for n≥1. The positive-state probabilities sum to (1+ρ)/2, so the total is one. At λ=2 and μ=4, π0=1/2 but α0=1/4; π1=1/4 but α1=3/8. Both recorders can be correct. The second observes busy states more often because more state changes occur there per hour.

Summing nαn gives actual-change mean (1+ρ)/[2(1−ρ)], exactly 1/2 above clock-time mean ρ/(1−ρ). At half load these are 3/2 and 1. Each expectation requires its observation law.

The mean stationary actual-change rate 2λ does not make changes a homogeneous Poisson process: instantaneous rate depends on emptiness. Equal long-run arrival and departure rates do not establish independence. Berestycki and Sousi, Theorem 2.10 proves the invariant-measure/jump-chain relationship used in our geometric calculation.

Recover time weights, with the correct boundary

Because αn/qn=πn/(2λ), normalization recovers π; ∑α/q=1/(2λ) hours/change. At half load mean visits last 1/2 hour when empty and 1/6 when busy. Equal visit weights discard this difference; inverse exit rates restore model-mean durations.

Finite records need a separate denominator. Consider seven consecutive complete visits with states 0,1,0,1,2,1,0 and durations 10/20,2/20,5/20,8/20,6/20,4/20,10/20 hours. These invented exact values illustrate a record, not a simulation. Each visit begins after an actual change; the next unlisted state is 1.

Before an arrival, after an arrival, or at a null tick?

Not every event sample has law α. At an external Poisson arrival immediately before it joins, the stationary queue size has law π: Poisson arrivals see time averages, or PASTA. This uses stationarity and a nonanticipating arrival mechanism, with future arrival increments independent of past queue/service history and arrivals independent of service. The arrivals help create the queue, so it would be wrong to call the arrival process independent of the entire queue process. We use PASTA here as a named theorem; Sigman’s continuous-time notes, the PASTA section gives the assumptions and statement.

After that arrival, the size has increased by one. Its distribution is therefore π shifted upward: empty probability zero and mean 1/(1−ρ). At λ=2 and μ=4 that mean is 2, rather than the prearrival mean 1 or all-change mean 3/2. Which side of the event is recorded is part of the question.

A different construction uses one Poisson clock of rate R=λ+μ. Each tick proposes an arrival with chance λ/R or a service attempt with chance μ/R. At a positive state both proposals change the size. At 0 a service attempt is a null event, leaving the size at 0; it is not a departure. Retaining every tick gives transition probabilities P00=μ/R, P01=λ/R, and the usual up/down probabilities elsewhere. Direct substitution verifies πP=π. Removing the null ticks gives the actual-change chain and its law α. Thus software that logs all proposed ticks and software that logs only actual changes can produce different histograms for one correct model.

Optional: why the kth change from empty does not settle to the event-frequency law

Frequency across visits is not a fixed-index distribution

Let Yk be the queue size after k actual changes, with Y0=0. At every change the size increases or decreases by exactly one. Consequently Yk has the same parity as k. Empty probability is zero at every odd index, whereas α0>0 in the stable model. Fixed-index distributions from empty therefore cannot converge to α. In fact their even/odd mass alternates forever.

Initialized with α, the jump chain stays distributed as α at every index. From empty, long-run visit proportions still converge to α in this irreducible positive recurrent model. Aperiodicity is required for convergence of fixed-index distributions, not these proportions. The stationarity lesson separates invariance from attraction.

Stationary clock-time initialization uses π, not automatically jump-chain law α. Specify clock and initialization separately. The optional fixed-index control starts empty. Uniformized ticks retain a self-transition at 0, avoiding the parity obstruction; deleting null ticks changes that behavior.

Calculate and inspect the model

1. Calculation. For M/M/1 rates λ=3/hour and μ=6/hour, find ρ, stationary P(0), P(2), and the mean system size.

Hint for question 1

Find the rate ratio first, then use the normalized geometric formula.

Full solution to question 1

ρ=3/6=1/2<1. Therefore P(0)=1−ρ=1/2 and P(2)=(1−ρ)ρ²=(1/2)(1/4)=1/8. The mean number in the system is ρ/(1−ρ)=(1/2)/(1/2)=1. These are stationary values; a queue just opened empty has P(0)=1 initially, so the formula does not describe its first instant.

2. Boundary and scope. An artist serves at μ=5/hour while arrivals are λ=5/hour. A colleague substitutes ρ=1 into πₙ=(1−ρ)ρⁿ and concludes every queue length has probability zero. What went wrong?

Hint for question 2

Ask whether the infinite geometric series is finite at ρ=1.

Full solution to question 2

The stationary derivation normalized Σρⁿ, which diverges at ρ=1. The factor 1−ρ=0 is only the limiting normalization from below, not a probability distribution at ρ=1. Assigning zero to every n sums to zero rather than one. This critical M/M/1 model has no normalizable stationary queue-length distribution; finite-time probabilities still exist and sum to one.

3. Changed load, unchanged reasoning. Use λ=3 and μ=5 per hour. Find the clock-time and actual-change empty probabilities, the corresponding mean sizes, and the prearrival and postarrival means. Recover the clock law’s empty probability by inverse-rate weighting.

Hint for question 3

ρ=3/5, q0=3, qn≥1=8, and ∑α/q=1/6 hour per change.

Full solution to question 3

Clock empty probability is 2/5 and mean size 3/2. Actual-change empty probability is 1/5 and mean size (1+3/5)/(2(1−3/5))=2. Prearrival mean is 3/2 by PASTA under the stated assumptions; postarrival mean is 5/2. The inverse-rate empty weight is (1/5)/3=1/15. Dividing by the total weight 1/6 gives (1/15)/(1/6)=2/5, recovering clock time.

4. Change speed without changing load. Compare (λ,μ)=(2,4) with (4,8). Which of the observation laws and mean sizes change? What happens to mean holding times at empty and positive states, and to the mean stationary actual-change rate? Does “eight actual changes per hour on average” imply a Poisson change process?

Hint for question 4

Separate the ratio λ/μ from the absolute rates. Divide both mean holding times by two, then reconsider which distribution formulas use only the ratio.

Second hint for question 4

Keep the same embedded sequence of states while rescaling its clock. Which stationary weights depend only on the rate ratio, and which waiting-time and event-count quantities also depend on the common rate scale?

Full solution to question 4

Both loads are 1/2, so π, α, the prearrival and postarrival laws, the uniformized invariant law and inverse-rate recovery all stay the same. Empty holding time falls from 1/2 to 1/4 hour; positive holding time from 1/6 to 1/12 hour. Mean actual-change rate doubles from 4 to 8 per hour. This is a mean rate, not a claim of homogeneous Poisson changes; instantaneous changes remain faster in positive states than at 0.

5. Transfer to a different process. A two-state continuous-time chain moves 0→1 at rate 1/hour and 1→0 at rate 3/hour. Find its clock-time stationary law and its actual-change visit frequencies. Show inverse-rate recovery. Can its fixed jump-index law from 0 converge?

Hint for question 5

Balance π0·1=π1·3. Actual changes alternate deterministically between the two states.

Full solution to question 5

Balance and normalization give π=(3/4,1/4). The alternating event visits have frequencies α=(1/2,1/2). Weighting by inverse exit rates gives (1/2,1/6); normalization by 2/3 recovers (3/4,1/4). At a fixed jump index from 0, the state is 0 at even indices and 1 at odd ones, so the distributions do not converge. The M/M/1 mean-size difference of 1/2 was specific to that queue law; the rate-weighting principle transfers more broadly.

6. Audit a recorder. One program logs the size just before each Poisson arrival. Another logs after every rate-(λ+μ) proposal tick, including unsuccessful service attempts at 0. A third logs after actual changes only. At λ=2,μ=4, which equilibrium empty fractions should they target? If all start empty, must the first ten rows already have those fractions?

Hint for question 6

Identify whether each recorder includes null ticks or shifts the size at arrivals. Keep a stationary law distinct from a short record started empty.

Second hint for question 6

For each observation rule, first identify the probability law it samples. Compare the rate-weighted actual-change law with the time-stationary law, and check whether the initial state is already drawn from either law.

Full solution to question 6

The first targets 1/2 under the prearrival PASTA assumptions. The second also has invariant empty fraction 1/2 because it retains the null ticks. The third targets actual-change frequency 1/4. Starting empty is not stationary; finite records also fluctuate. None must equal its equilibrium value in ten rows. For the third, even the distribution of a fixed kth row from empty has a parity obstruction, while long-run visit frequencies can still converge.

Read load together with assumptions

M/M/1 balance requires a specific birth-death model and observation protocol. Clock-time π, actual-change α, and pre/postarrival laws answer different questions; retaining null ticks changes event frequencies. Normalization requires λ<μ and tail size depends on ρ. Finite records and fixed-index distributions need separate interpretation. Revisit violated assumptions; exponential waits supplies the memoryless construction.
