Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Can dots measure a polygon's area?

Draw a polygon whose vertices sit on the integer grid. Count grid points strictly inside it and grid points on its boundary. Pick's theorem turns those two whole-number counts into its exact Euclidean area, even if its edges slant. The formula has hypotheses: the polygon must be simple, have lattice vertices, and have no holes in the basic form.

Background capsule. A lattice point has coordinates (m,n) with integers m,n. A simple polygon's boundary does not cross itself. Interior points are strictly inside; boundary points include all grid points on edges, not merely the listed corners. The area here is ordinary Euclidean square units for a grid with unit spacing. A triangle with lattice vertices has half-integer area by the determinant or shoelace formula. We will use that observation to sketch why point counting could work.

Count a three-by-two rectangle

Worked example. Take corners (0,0), (3,0), (3,2), (0,2). Its ordinary area is 3·2=6. Interior lattice points are (1,1) and (2,1), so I=2. The boundary has four points along each horizontal length-three edge and three along each vertical length-two edge, but counting all corners twice would overcount. A compact count is B=2·3+2·2=10. Pick's expression gives 2+10/2−1=6, matching the ordinary area.

A slanted triangle gives a less rectangular check. For vertices (0,0), (2,0), (0,1), area is one. Its only boundary lattice points are (0,0), (1,0), (2,0), (0,1), so B=4 and I=0. Pick gives 0+4/2−1=1. Counting grid points along slanted edges requires care: if an edge changes coordinates by (Δx,Δy), it has gcd(|Δx|,|Δy|) lattice intervals.

The formula and a proof sketch

Exact theorem. For a simple lattice polygon with connected interior and no holes,

A=I+B2−1

A primitive lattice triangle has no lattice points except its three vertices. A substantive lattice lemma says its area is 1/2; half-integrality alone does not prove that lemma. Assuming it, the triangle's Pick expression is 0+3/2−1=1/2. If two lattice polygons are glued along a full edge, their areas add. The boundary and interior counts also combine so that the Pick expressions add: points in the shared edge cease being exterior boundary points and become interior or are handled at its endpoints. One can triangulate a simple lattice polygon and then subdivide triangles that contain lattice points until primitive triangles remain. Each split lowers positive half-integer area, so the process terminates. This indicates a proof route. The primitive-triangle lemma, general triangulation and gluing bookkeeping, including awkward nonconvex cases, are deferred rather than hidden behind a picture.

There is a particularly useful boundary count. A segment from (x₁,y₁) to (x₂,y₂) contains gcd(|x₂−x₁|,|y₂−y₁|)+1 lattice points including endpoints. Summing the gcd terms over polygon edges counts each boundary lattice point once because each vertex is the end of one interval and the start of the next. Thus B is the sum of these gcd values. For the rectangle it gives 3+2+3+2=10, agreeing with the direct list.

With h holes, the corrected relation for a suitable lattice polygonal region is A=I+B/2−1+h, where B includes inner and outer boundaries. The elementary formula above does not cover holes. The correction reflects topology: an extra boundary component changes the Euler count underlying the gluing argument.

Count accurately and catch a missing hypothesis

1. Calculation. Find the area of the lattice triangle (0,0), (4,0), (0,3) using Pick's theorem. First determine B and I.

Hint for question 1

Boundary count is gcd(4,0)+gcd(4,3)+gcd(0,3). Compare with base-times-height area.

Full solution for question 1

B=4+1+3=8. Count interior rows: at y=1 the points are (1,1) and (2,1); at y=2 only (1,2) lies strictly inside. Thus I=3, and Pick gives A=3+8/2−1=6. The independent base-times-height check is (4·3)/2=6.

2. Failure test. Remove the open unit square 1<x<2, 1<y<2 from the 3-by-3 lattice square, keeping both boundaries. Why does the basic formula give the wrong area?

Hint for question 2

Count the outer and inner boundary points, then use h=1.

Full solution for question 2

The region's area is 9−1=8. The outer boundary has 12 lattice points and the inner square adds 4, so B=16. No integer lattice points lie strictly in the remaining region, so I=0. Basic Pick gives 0+16/2−1=7, one short. There is one hole, and the corrected expression 0+8−1+1=8 agrees. The missing hypothesis is “no holes.”

Count the right dots in the right region

Pick's theorem gives exact Euclidean area from I and B for a simple hole-free lattice polygon. The rectangle and slanted triangle are checks; the primitive-triangle argument explains the proof strategy while leaving full triangulation details explicit as deferred work. An edge may contain lattice points between its corners, so use the gcd rule when counts are not obvious. A hole changes the constant term, as the square-ring counterexample shows. Grid plots can help find points, but the polygon's simplicity and boundary components must be established before applying the formula.
