What does a payoff table actually say?

Two friends independently choose a path through a tiny maze. Each can take the upper path U or the lower path L. A table records the points each receives for every pair of moves. It describes a stipulated game: who moves, which choices are possible, and which score follows. It does not tell us what the friends will choose. First learn to read one cell without silently treating the first number as a shared reward.

Background capsule. A table has rows and columns. An ordered pair (a,b) keeps its order: here a is the row player's score and b is the column player's score. A payoff is a numerical representation of a player's preference among outcomes in this model. Higher means that player prefers the outcome; adding the two scores is meaningful only if an extra assumption makes their scales comparable. We assume choices are simultaneous, so neither player sees the other's current move before choosing.

Read four outcomes, one player at a time

Maze points (row player's score, column player's score)
Column UColumn L
Row U(3,2)(0,4)
Row L(2,0)(1,1)

Worked example. If both choose U, the row player gets 3 and the column player gets 2. If row chooses U while column chooses L, the outcome is (0,4). The row player's score falls from 3 to 0 when column switches from U to L; the column player's score rises from 2 to 4. If row alone switches from U to L while column stays U, compare (3,2) with (2,0). That move changes both scores, but row chooses according to the first coordinate. The column player's score is a consequence of the move, not row's payoff.

A payoff table can be read from either player's viewpoint. With row fixed at U, column compares its second-coordinate scores 2 under U and 4 under L, so L is preferable. With row fixed at L, column compares 0 under U and 1 under L, again preferring L. No probability was needed. We merely held one action fixed and compared the two available outcomes.

A table is a function on pairs of actions

Let SR = {U,L} and SC = {U,L}. The notation uR(r,c) means row's payoff when actions are r and c. Likewise uC(r,c) is the column payoff. A cell records both functions at the same input:

u(r,c)=(uR(r,c),uC(r,c))

Exact claim and scope. Under this two-player, two-action description, the table exhausts all four pure action profiles because 2 × 2 = 4. The proof is enumeration: row has two choices and each can be paired with either of column's two choices. This is a description of outcomes, not a theorem about behavior. If a friend can choose a third path, the four-cell table omits possibilities. If points are merely labels that fail to reflect preferences, comparing numbers will not predict preferred outcomes. A higher payoff can mean a greater prize or simply a stipulated rank; the interpretation must be supplied by the game designer.

Changing every row-player payoff by the same positive scaling and shift preserves row's ordering of cells. Yet subtracting or adding both players' payoffs to pronounce one outcome “best for the group” needs a welfare rule. The table alone does not give one. In this example (U,U) sums to 5 and (L,L) sums to 2, but sum maximization is a separate criterion.

Test your reading

1. Calculation. The column player chooses U. What are row's two possible payoffs, and which row move does row prefer? Then repeat when column chooses L.

Hint for exercise 1

Stay in one column, and compare only the first coordinate.

Full solution to exercise 1

When column chooses U, row's payoff is 3 from U and 2 from L, so row prefers U. When column chooses L, row's payoff is 0 from U and 1 from L, so row prefers L. The favored row move changes with column's choice, so the table alone does not support saying “row always chooses U.”

2. Interpretation. Someone says that (U,U) must occur because its scores add to the largest total. Identify two assumptions that statement would need, and show one reason it can fail as a prediction of independent moves.

Hint for exercise 2

Ask whose preference a sum measures and whether each player would want to stay at (U,U).

Full solution to exercise 2

The claim would need a rule that adds the two players' scores on comparable scales, and a mechanism or agreement that makes players choose the sum-maximizing pair. Without these, column sees a profitable unilateral switch from U to L when row uses U: its payoff rises from 2 to 4. Thus (U,U) is not stable under independent payoff-seeking, even though 3 + 2 = 5 is the largest sum in the table. A statement about welfare and a statement about incentives answer different questions.

What the grid gives you

A payoff cell names one outcome and assigns a separate score to each player. To compare moves, hold the other player's choice fixed and inspect only the decision maker's coordinate. In this example, that calculation is enough to see that row's preferred move depends on column's move, while column prefers L in either row. The next lessons formalize those comparisons as dominance and best response.

Failure case. The grid has no entry for communication, uncertain scoring, or a third action. Conclusions drawn from its four cells are conditional on the two-action simultaneous game as stated. A payoff matrix is a precise small model, not a survey of all possible human motives.
