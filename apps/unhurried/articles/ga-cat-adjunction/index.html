How can a set freely generate an operation?

Give me a set X of letters. All finite words in those letters, including the empty word, form a monoid under concatenation. A choice of an image in a monoid M for each letter extends uniquely to a monoid homomorphism on all words. This correspondence is the concrete content of the free-monoid/underlying-set adjunction.

Background capsule. A monoid is a set M with an associative binary operation · and an identity e. A homomorphism φ:M→N preserves the operation and identity: φ(x·y)=φ(x)·φ(y), φ(eM)=eN. Let F(X) be finite words on X, with concatenation and empty word ε. For a function r:X′→X, F(r):F(X′)→F(X) replaces each letter x′ by r(x′), preserving order. Let U(M) be M considered only as a set; U sends a monoid homomorphism to its underlying function. The function i:X→U(F(X)) sends each letter to its one-letter word. HomMon and HomSet mean sets of homomorphisms and functions of the indicated type.

Choose images of two generators

Worked example. Let X={a,b} and M={0,1} with addition modulo 2, whose identity is 0. Choose h:X→U(M) by h(a)=1 and h(b)=0. The induced homomorphism ĥ:F(X)→M evaluates a word by adding its letter values. Thus ĥ(ε)=0, ĥ(a)=1, ĥ(b)=0, ĥ(aba)=1+0+1=0 mod 2, and ĥ(abb)=1+0+0=1. Splitting a word into two pieces does not change the total sum, so ĥ respects concatenation.

There are four functions X→M: each of a and b can independently map to 0 or 1. The universal property says there are correspondingly four monoid homomorphisms F(X)→M, since a homomorphism is forced by its values on the one-letter words. This finite count concerns the maps; the free monoid F(X) itself is infinite.

Existence, uniqueness and the bijection

Exact statement and full extension proof. For any set X and monoid M, restriction along i gives a bijection

HomMon(F(X),M)≅HomSet(X,U(M))

Given h:X→U(M), define ĥ(ε)=e and ĥ(x1…xn)=h(x1)···h(xn) in that order. Associativity makes this expression unambiguous, and ε maps to e. A concatenated pair of words has exactly the first word's letters followed by the second's, so ĥ(uv)=ĥ(u)·ĥ(v). Thus ĥ is a monoid homomorphism with U(ĥ)∘i=h. Conversely, if φ:F(X)→M is a homomorphism, it sends ε to e and each word x1…xn to φ(x1)···φ(xn). Its values on single letters therefore determine it uniquely. Restricting an extension returns h, and extending a restriction returns φ. These operations are inverse bijections.

The bijection is natural in X and M. For r:X′→X, extension of h∘r equals ĥ∘F(r):F(X′)→M, since both send a word x′1…x′n to the ordered product h(r(x′1))···h(r(x′n)). If k:M→N is a homomorphism, k∘ĥ equals the extension of U(k)∘h, because both multiply the sequence k(h(xj)). These identities also hold on ε by preservation of identities. This is the named adjunction F⊣U. General adjunction machinery, including units and counits for arbitrary functors, is beyond this proof.

There is a crucial failure case: if a proposed word map sends ε to 1 in M={0,1} under addition mod 2, it cannot be a monoid homomorphism, even if it seems to preserve some nonempty concatenations. Preservation of identity is part of the definition.

Extend and test

1. Calculation. With X={a,b} and M=({0,1}, addition mod 2), choose h(a)=1, h(b)=1. Compute ĥ(abba) and ĥ(ε).

Hint for question 1

Add one copy of each letter's assigned value, retaining repetitions.

Full solution for question 1

The word abba has four letters, each valued 1, so ĥ(abba)=1+1+1+1=0 mod 2. The empty word is the monoid identity, so ĥ(ε)=0. These answers also follow by grouping abba as ab·ba: both halves evaluate to 0.

2. Explanation. Why can two homomorphisms F(X)→M that agree on every one-letter word never disagree on a longer word?

Hint for question 2

Write a longer word as a concatenation of one-letter words.

Full solution for question 2

For w=x1…xn, the homomorphism law forces φ(w)=φ(x1)···φ(xn) and the same formula for ψ. Their factors agree letter by letter, hence their products agree. Both send ε to the identity. Therefore φ and ψ agree on every word.

The universal arrow

The one-letter inclusion i:X→U(F(X)) is universal: every function h:X→U(M) factors uniquely as U(ĥ)∘i. Our proof covers all sets X and all monoids M, not only the two-letter example. It does not say every construction called “free” is a word monoid. The distinctive data are the categories, the forgetful functor and the hom-set correspondence, with the arrow directions intact.
