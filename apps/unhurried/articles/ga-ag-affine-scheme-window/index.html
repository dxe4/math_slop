How can one point carry extra arithmetic?

The rings k[x]/(x) and k[x]/(x²) both have one classical k-point when k is a field. In the second ring, however, the class of x is nonzero and squares to zero. The spectrum construction keeps this difference in its ring even though the underlying set of prime ideals has one point in either case. We will compute both spectra completely and state what this small example does not teach about general schemes.

Background capsule. A prime ideal p of a commutative ring A is a proper ideal with the property that ab∈p implies a∈p or b∈p. Equivalently, A/p has no zero divisors. Spec(A) is the set of all prime ideals of A, equipped with a topology: V(I) consists of primes containing I, and basic opens D(f) consist of primes not containing f. A nilpotent element u has um=0 for some positive m. Every prime ideal contains each nilpotent, because um∈p forces u∈p by repeated use of primality.

Two quotients, one visible point each

Worked example. Let A=k[x]/(x). Every polynomial class is determined by its constant term, so A≅k. A field has exactly one prime ideal, (0): it is proper and its quotient is the field itself. Thus Spec(A) is a single point. The class [x] in A is zero because x belongs to the defining ideal.

Let B=k[x]/(x²), and write ε=[x]. Every class has a unique form a+bε with a,b∈k and ε²=0. Uniqueness follows because a+bx belongs to (x²) only when a=b=0. So ε is genuinely nonzero and nilpotent. The ideal (ε) consists of bε; quotienting B by it gives k, hence (ε) is maximal and prime. Every prime of B must contain ε because ε²=0; any ideal containing (ε) corresponds to an ideal of B/(ε)=k, and only (0) and k occur there. Properness leaves only (ε). Therefore Spec(B) also has one point.

What Spec records, and what the set forgets

Definition and proved calculation. For any commutative unital ring A,

Spec(A)={𝔭⊂A:𝔭is a prime ideal}

Our proof gives exactly one prime for each quotient above. Their underlying topological spaces are consequently both the one-point space: the only opens are empty and the whole set. Indeed, for B the only prime contains ε, so D(ε)=∅; D(1)=Spec(B). Yet the rings are not isomorphic. A field has no nonzero nilpotent, while B has ε≠0 with ε²=0. Any ring isomorphism would preserve that property, so none exists.

An affine scheme is more than the bare set Spec(A): it includes a structure sheaf of rings on the open subsets. For this one-point example, the ring of global sections is A itself, so the two affine schemes retain different arithmetic even though their point sets and topologies look identical. We have not constructed the structure sheaf here or proved the general global-sections theorem. Those are named facts from scheme theory; the verified part of this article is the elementary ring and prime-ideal calculation.

For k algebraically closed, the sole maximal prime in each example corresponds to the classical solution x=0. If k is not algebraically closed, the calculation of these particular rings still works, but a general affine spectrum may have prime ideals that are not k-valued coordinate points. Spec is not simply a way to draw all graph points. It also includes nonmaximal primes in larger rings, such as (0) in k[x], and records algebra through its sheaf. Compare the radical-ideal theorem to see why ordinary point sets discard the square in (x²).

Find primes and nilpotents

1. Calculation. For C=k[t]/(t³), write every class in a normal form, identify a nonzero nilpotent, and find all prime ideals.

Hint for question 1

Reduce powers t³ and above. Every prime contains [t] because [t]³=0, then quotient by ([t]).

Full solution for question 1

Every class has a unique form a+bτ+cτ² with τ=[t] and a,b,c∈k. The class τ is nonzero but τ³=0. Any prime contains τ. The ideal (τ) is prime because C/(τ)≅k is a field. A proper ideal strictly containing (τ) would correspond to a proper nonzero ideal of k, which does not exist. Thus (τ) is the unique prime and Spec(C) has one point.

2. Explanation. Why is (0) not a prime ideal of B=k[ε]/(ε²), even though it is prime in k?

Hint for question 2

Use the product ε·ε and the fact ε itself is nonzero.

Full solution for question 2

In B, ε·ε=0 belongs to (0), yet ε does not belong to (0). This violates the defining property of a prime ideal. In the field k, a product can be zero only when a factor is zero, so (0) is prime there. The nilpotent distinguishes the two rings even though each spectrum has one point.

A small window into schemes

The two example spectra each have one prime, but their rings differ by a nonzero nilpotent. This proves that the underlying point set and topology alone do not retain all affine-scheme information. Stop once you can compute the sole prime of k[x]/(x²) and explain the precise role of ε; the structure sheaf and general scheme theory remain further study.
