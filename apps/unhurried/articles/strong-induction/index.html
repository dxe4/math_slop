When one predecessor is not enough

Every integer at least 2 can be made by multiplying primes. A small factor tree makes the claim plausible: 84 = 2·2·3·7. But why can no larger integer get stuck with a composite factor that never reaches primes? The proof needs access to potentially different smaller factors, not just the immediately preceding integer. Strong induction makes exactly that permission explicit. Our goal is existence of a prime factorization; uniqueness is a separate theorem and will not be smuggled into the conclusion.

Background capsule. A prime is an integer greater than 1 with no positive divisors except 1 and itself. A composite integer greater than 1 can be written as ab with integers 1 < a, b < n. A prime factorization is a product of one or more primes, with repetitions allowed. Strong induction assumes a property for all earlier indices from the base through n when proving it for n + 1.

Follow both branches of a factor tree

Worked example. For 84, choose 84 = 6·14. The factor 6 becomes 2·3 and 14 becomes 2·7, yielding 84 = 2·3·2·7. Another first split, 84 = 4·21, gives 2·2·3·7. The two trees illustrate that a composite can split into two strictly smaller integers. Their ends are prime. They do not by themselves show that every integer has such an ending, nor do they prove that all factor trees give the same multiset of primes.

The decreasing size is the useful measure of this construction. Every nonprime branch has children at least 2 and smaller than its parent. Since positive integers cannot decrease forever, repeated splitting must stop at primes. That intuition can be organized into a precise strong-induction proof with a base and a step.

The optional induction display separates the available earlier cases from the new target. An observed finite set of factor trees is only illustration; the proof below covers all integers at least 2.

Use exactly the smaller factors you need

n=ab,1<a,b<n

Theorem. Every integer n ≥ 2 is a product of primes. Base. The integer 2 is prime, so it is a one-factor product of primes. Strong-induction step. Suppose every integer from 2 through n has a prime factorization. Consider n + 1. If it is prime, its one-factor product suffices. If it is composite, write n + 1 = ab with 1 < a, b < n + 1. Both a and b are integers between 2 and n, so the induction hypothesis supplies a prime factorization for each. Multiplying those products gives a prime factorization of n + 1. The base and step prove existence for every integer at least 2.

Why not assume only the factorization of n? If n + 1 = ab, neither a nor b need equal n. At 85 = 5·17, knowledge solely about 84 would not directly factor either child. Strong induction collects the earlier claims we actually need. It is logically equivalent in power to ordinary induction but often matches recursive structures more cleanly.

This proof establishes existence only. It never compares two different factorizations. The familiar uniqueness assertion requires a divisibility result such as Euclid’s lemma: a prime dividing a product divides one factor. Without that extra argument, two trees ending in the same primes are examples, not a uniqueness proof. Keep the theorem’s conclusion exactly as strong as its reasoning.

Trace the dependencies

Exercise 1. Factor 90 by first splitting it as 9·10, then explain which earlier values the induction step would invoke if the target were 90.

Hint for exercise 1

Both 9 and 10 are smaller than 90; factor each separately.

Full solution to exercise 1

Since 9 = 3·3 and 10 = 2·5, we get 90 = 3·3·2·5, a product of primes. A strong-induction proof at 90 may invoke the already established factorization claims for 9 and 10, among all integers from 2 through 89. Knowledge only about 89 would not directly supply those two branches.

Exercise 2. Someone claims the proof above also establishes uniqueness because every splitting process ends. Explain why that inference fails.

Hint for exercise 2

Termination says a tree ends, but what would compare two different trees?

Full solution to exercise 2

Termination and the induction proof ensure that each tree produces some prime product. They do not show that two distinct first splits produce the same prime factors. For 12, both 2·6 and 3·4 end in 2·2·3, but this one agreement is not a general proof. A comparison needs an additional prime-divisibility argument; uniqueness remains outside this theorem.

Existence is enough for this stop

Strong induction lets the step use any earlier cases, which fits a factorization split into two smaller pieces. We proved that every integer at least 2 has at least one prime factorization. We did not prove uniqueness, and a drawn factor tree is only a sample. For another way to rule out outcomes by tracking what a process preserves, visit invariants and reachability.
