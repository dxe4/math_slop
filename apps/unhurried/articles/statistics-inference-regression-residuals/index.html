What do the misses tell us about a fitted line?

Fit a line to the tempo and length of invented tracks. The question is what the observed numbers allow us to say, and where the tempting shortcut fails.

Background capsule. A fitted line predicts y from x as a+bx. A residual is observed y minus predicted y, so a positive residual lies above the line. Ordinary least squares chooses a and b to minimize the sum of squared residuals for the recorded pairs. A residual plot puts each residual against x or fitted value to reveal structure the line did not capture.

Worked example

Consider five invented pairs x=0,1,2,3,4 and y=2,1,2,5,10. They follow y=2−2x+x² exactly: 2,1,2,5,10. Their mean x is 2 and mean y is 4. The centered cross-product sum is 20, while centered x squared sum is 10, so the least-squares slope is b=2 and intercept a=4−2×2=0. The fitted values are 0,2,4,6,8; residuals are 2,−1,−2,−1,2. That symmetric U pattern is systematic curvature, despite the line passing through the data center.

The line y-hat=2x predicts zero at x=0 even though the observed value is 2, and eight at x=4 even though the observed value is 10. The central point at x=2 has residual −2. Reading residuals in x order reveals the U pattern that a mere sum of zero would conceal.

The claim and its conditions

ei=yi−(a+bxi)

A small algebra bridge makes the minimization readable without prior calculus: for any number u, changing it to u−h changes its square by (u−h)²−u²=−2uh+h². Applied to each residual, this shows how a small shift in intercept or slope changes the total squared error; at a minimum the linear-in-h part must vanish. At an unconstrained least-squares minimum with an intercept, differentiating S(a,b)=Σ(y_i−a−bx_i)² with respect to a gives Σe_i=0; differentiating with respect to b gives Σx_i e_i=0. Solving yields b=Σ(x_i−x-bar)(y_i−y-bar)/Σ(x_i−x-bar)² when the x values are not all equal, and a=y-bar−b x-bar. These are algebraic properties of the fitted sample, not evidence that errors are independent or that the line predicts well outside the observed x range. A U-shaped residual pattern warns that the mean response is not well described by a straight line over this range.

Proof boundary and failure case. Classical standard errors for a simple linear model also require a suitable error model, often independent errors with constant conditional variance; normality is used for exact small-sample t inference. Residual plots can flag curvature, changing spread, or outliers, but cannot certify every assumption or prove independence. The exact parabola here is a constructed failure case for a line. Fitting a more flexible curve may improve in-sample fit while increasing overfitting risk.

The normal equations only say the residual vector is orthogonal to the constant column and the observed x column. Curvature can still remain because x² is a different direction of variation. A quadratic fit would represent these five constructed values exactly, but perfect in-sample fit is not a guarantee for new tracks.

Try two checks

1. Calculation. For the fitted line y-hat=1+2x, find residuals at (x,y)=(0,2),(1,2),(2,6). What is the sum?

Hint for exercise 1

Calculate each fitted value first, then subtract it from y.

Full solution to exercise 1

At x=0, fitted 1 and residual 1. At x=1, fitted 3 and residual −1. At x=2, fitted 5 and residual 1. The sum is 1. This line was supplied, not necessarily fitted by least squares with an intercept, so the residual sum need not be zero.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. A least-squares line has residuals that widen in spread as fitted values rise, with no obvious curve in their center. What concern does this raise, and what does the plot not prove?

Hint for exercise 2

Think about whether prediction-error variance is constant across the range.

Full solution to exercise 2

The widening suggests heteroscedasticity: error variance may increase with fitted value. Standard-error formulas assuming constant conditional variance may then be unreliable. The plot is diagnostic, not proof of a particular variance function; it also cannot establish independence, a causal relation, or reliable extrapolation.

If residuals are plotted against fitted values, retain their signs and a visible horizontal zero reference. A run of positive, then negative, then positive residuals suggests the line systematically misses the center relative to the ends.

What you can carry forward

Use residuals to test what the line leaves behind. Their algebraic sum can be zero by construction while their pattern is plainly nonrandom. Inspect shape, spread, and extreme points before using a line for prediction or inference, and keep causal claims outside a descriptive fit.

Prediction beyond x=4 is extrapolation. The original points cannot show whether a quadratic physical mechanism continues there. A residual check diagnoses a mismatch within the observed range; it does not establish a future law.
