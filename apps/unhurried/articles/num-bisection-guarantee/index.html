Certify a root by trapping it

A numerical root is a point r where f(r)=0. A calculator may print many digits for a proposed root, but the digits alone do not prove a root lies nearby. Bisection offers a different output: an interval known to contain at least one root, provided a continuous function has opposite signs at its endpoints and those signs are determined reliably. Every step halves the uncertainty without needing a derivative.

Background capsule. A continuous function cannot move from a negative value to a positive value across an interval without taking zero somewhere; this is the intermediate value theorem. The midpoint of [a,b] is (a+b)/2, and its width is b−a. “Bracket” means endpoints with opposite signs, or an endpoint that is already a root. The promise concerns a root’s location, not which root is found if several occur.

Trap √2 with exact signs

Worked example. Set f(x)=x²−2 on [1,2]. Here f(1)=−1 and f(2)=2, so some root lies inside. The midpoint is 1.5; f(1.5)=0.25 is positive, leaving [1,1.5]. Next midpoint 1.25 gives f(1.25)=−0.4375, leaving [1.25,1.5]. Next midpoint 1.375 gives −0.109375, leaving [1.375,1.5]. The width is now 0.125. These decimals are exact terminating rational calculations; no uncertain sign is concealed.

If we report the midpoint of the final bracket as an approximation, its absolute error from some enclosed root is at most half the width, or 0.0625. The midpoint is 1.4375, and √2 is indeed closer, but the certificate does not need to know √2’s decimal expansion. To obtain width at most 0.001 from an initial width 1, choose n with 2⁻ⁿ ≤ 0.001; ten halvings suffice because 1/1024 < 0.001.

Why the interval promise persists

bn−an=b0−a02n

Assume f is continuous on the original closed interval and its endpoint values have opposite signs. At a midpoint m, either f(m)=0 and the task is finished, or its sign agrees with exactly one endpoint sign. Retain m and the endpoint of opposite sign. Continuity remains valid on this smaller closed interval, so the intermediate value theorem again places a root inside. Its width is exactly half the previous width. Induction proves the displayed width formula and bracket invariant for every completed step.

The width certificate is independent of a small residual. A nearly flat function such as f(x)=10⁻⁹(x−100) can have a tiny residual far from 100. Bisection certifies a location because it encloses a root, not because |f(m)| is small. If f is not continuous, opposite signs do not suffice: f(x)=−1 for x<0 and +1 for x≥0 has opposite endpoint signs on [−1,1] but no zero. If endpoint signs are produced by uncertain rounded arithmetic, an apparent bracket may be false. Interval arithmetic or exact rational evaluations can help certify signs; ordinary displayed decimals do not automatically do so.

A bracket does not establish uniqueness. For example, x³−x changes sign over [−2,2] and has three roots there. A monotonicity proof, as for x²−2 on [1,2], supplies uniqueness when needed.

Make a certificate

Exercise 1. For f(x)=x²−3 on [1,2], perform two bisections with exact midpoint signs. Give the final bracket and a midpoint error bound.

Hint for exercise 1

Evaluate f(3/2), then f at the midpoint of the retained half.

Solution to exercise 1

f(1)=−2 and f(2)=1. At 3/2, f=9/4−3=−3/4, so retain [3/2,2]. Its midpoint is 7/4; f=49/16−48/16=1/16, so retain [3/2,7/4]. The width is 1/4; midpoint 13/8 is within 1/8 of an enclosed root. Since f increases on this positive interval, the root √3 is unique there.

Exercise 2. A student claims f(−1)<0<f(1) proves a root exists on [−1,1], without saying anything about continuity. Construct a function violating the conclusion and say exactly which proof step fails.

Hint for exercise 2

Use a jump across zero and keep every function value away from zero.

Solution to exercise 2

Define f(x)=−1 for x<0 and f(x)=1 for x≥0. Its endpoint signs are opposite, but f never equals zero. The intermediate value theorem cannot be applied because f is discontinuous at zero. Halving intervals around the jump gives small widths but certifies no root.

A guarantee with visible assumptions

For a continuous function with reliable opposite endpoint signs, bisection keeps a root in a shrinking bracket. After n halvings the bracket width is the initial width divided by 2ⁿ, and its midpoint has at most half that positional error relative to an enclosed root. This is a strong guarantee but can be slow. Newton’s method can accelerate near a suitable simple root, while losing this global bracket promise unless guarded.
