Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Powers and logarithms

For a positive integer n, aⁿ means a multiplied by itself n times. For example, 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. For a nonzero base, a⁰=1 and a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. A negative exponent describes a reciprocal.

Worked example. With base 3, the logarithm asks which exponent produces a positive input. Since 3⁴=81, log₃(81)=4. More generally, for a positive base b different from 1, log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with x positive.

34=81log3(81)=4

Multiplying powers with the same base adds their exponents: 2³×2²=2⁵. For positive inputs, this becomes the logarithm rule log₂(uv)=log₂(u)+log₂(v). It is a rule about a product.

Try one. Find log₂(1/8) and explain the negative sign.

Worked answer

Because 2⁻³=1/2³=1/8, the logarithm is −3. For a base larger than 1, inputs between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms.

Watch the operation. Logarithms do not generally turn a sum into a sum: log₂(2+2)=2, while log₂(2)+log₂(2)=2 happens to agree in this case. Using 2+4 instead gives log₂(6), which differs from 1+2=3 because 2³=8.

How many bits is a surprise?

A fictional printer chooses one glyph from a fixed alphabet on each turn. A circle appears on half the turns; a star appears on one eighth. Which arrival tells you more about what happened? We can answer without pretending that a symbol carries a fixed number of bits in every setting. The answer depends on the probabilities in the specified source. If the printer changes its habits, the same star may carry a different amount of information.

Background capsule. A probability lies between zero and one. Independent turns multiply probabilities: an event with chance one half followed by another independent event with chance one half has chance one quarter. A base-two logarithm asks what power of two produces a number: log₂ 8 = 3 and log₂(1/8) = −3. We use bits because doubling the odds against an event adds one to its surprise. Probabilities here are stipulated, not estimated from a sample.

A small printer with unequal glyphs

Worked example. Suppose circle has probability 1/2, square 1/4, triangle 1/8, and star 1/8. These sum to one, so they define a complete distribution. Circle has surprise −log₂(1/2) = 1 bit. Square has 2 bits. Triangle and star each have 3 bits. A surprising event is one to which the model assigned a small probability; it need not be emotionally surprising to a reader.

Imagine four independent turns, with circle then circle then star then square. Under this model the sequence probability is (1/2)(1/2)(1/8)(1/4) = 1/128. Its surprise is 7 bits. Adding the individual surprises gives 1 + 1 + 3 + 2 = 7 as well. The agreement is the reason a logarithm is useful for describing successive independent observations.

The measure and its boundary

For an outcome x assigned positive probability p(x), define its self-information in bits by the following formula. The base matters: base ten gives decimal digits of surprise, and natural logarithms give nats. Numerical values in different units must not be compared without conversion.

I(x)=−log2p(x)

Exact claim. If X and Y are independent and the observed pair (x,y) has positive probability, then I(x,y) = I(x) + I(y). Independence gives p(x,y) = p(x)p(y). Applying log₂(ab) = log₂ a + log₂ b and changing signs proves the claim. If observations are dependent, use their joint probability: the sum of separate surprises can be wrong. For example, if a second glyph always repeats the first, seeing circle twice has probability 1/2, so the pair has 1 bit of surprise, not 2.

A possible outcome with probability zero never occurs under the stipulated model. Formally −log₂ 0 has no finite value; one may describe its limiting surprise as infinite, but cannot insert that infinity into an ordinary observed finite calculation. An outcome with probability one has zero surprise. These edge cases describe the model, and an impossible observed glyph would signal that the model or observation rule needs reconsidering.

Calculate and challenge the model

Exercise 1. A four-color wheel has probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, and 1/8. What is the surprise of the sequence second color then fourth color if spins are independent? Give both its probability and bit value.

Hint for exercise 1

Multiply the two probabilities, then ask which power of two is their reciprocal.

Full solution to exercise 1

The sequence probability is (1/4)(1/8) = 1/32. Since 32 = 2⁵, −log₂(1/32) = 5 bits. Adding the individual values, 2 + 3, checks the result. It would be incorrect to add probabilities or to give 5 bits without the independence assumption.

Exercise 2. A machine emits a fair bit and then repeats that bit exactly. A reader says the observation 00 has surprise 2 bits because it contains two zeroes. Test the claim and explain the source of any difference.

Hint for exercise 2

List all possible two-character outputs of the machine and their probabilities.

Full solution to exercise 2

Only 00 and 11 are possible, each with probability 1/2. Therefore I(00) = −log₂(1/2) = 1 bit. The second zero is completely determined after the first, so multiplying two marginal probabilities of 1/2 would assume independence that the machine lacks. Outputs 01 and 10 have probability zero in this model and cannot be given ordinary finite surprise values.

What this measure can say

Self-information describes how unlikely a specified outcome was under a specified distribution. For the printer, the star carries 3 bits and the circle 1 bit; for a different printer those numbers may change. This is a measure of an event, not yet the average uncertainty before observing a turn. That average is entropy, which weights each event's surprise by its probability. The distinction also matters for coding: a rare symbol can merit a long codeword, while a common one merits a short one.

As a stopping check, choose two events whose probabilities multiply to 1/16 and verify that their surprises add to 4 bits. Then ask whether the multiplication is licensed by independence. The MIT information and entropy text develops the logarithmic measure for specified probability models; our printer and exercises are original finite examples. A frequency table from a short run would be evidence about the printer, but it would not automatically equal the stipulated distribution used here.
