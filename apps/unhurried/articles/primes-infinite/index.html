Could someone finish the list of primes?

Suppose a collector claims to have listed every prime number. Euclid's idea is to use that list to make a number that forces trouble. The trick needs one line of arithmetic and careful reasoning.

For this exploration, a prime is a positive integer greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself. A number greater than 1 that is not prime is composite. We say a divides b when b = ak for some integer k. Thus 3 divides 12, while 5 does not divide 12. The number 1 is neither prime nor composite. Every integer greater than 1 has a prime divisor: if it is prime, it is its own prime divisor; if it is composite, split it into smaller positive factors greater than 1 and continue. Because the factors strictly decrease and cannot descend forever among positive integers, this process reaches a prime.

That last finite-descent fact is the only background result we need. It says nothing about how quickly a prime divisor can be found, just that one exists. We will prove there are infinitely many primes without trying to list them.

Try the construction on a short list

Worked example. Take the finite prime list 2, 3, 5. Multiply its entries and add 1: 2 × 3 × 5 + 1 = 31. Division by any listed prime leaves remainder 1: 31 = 2 × 15 + 1, 31 = 3 × 10 + 1, and 31 = 5 × 6 + 1. So none of those three primes divides 31. In this particular example, 31 itself is prime. That may suggest a stronger rule, but it is a false one.

Use the six primes 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 instead. Their product plus one is 30,031, which factors as 59 × 509. It is composite, yet none of the six primes on the starting list divides it. The construction promises a new prime divisor, not necessarily a prime constructed number. Here it supplies at least 59.

These examples let us inspect the move, but even a million examples would not prove a claim about every possible finite list. The proof below makes the word every do the work.

Euclid's contradiction, carefully stated

Claim. There are infinitely many prime numbers. Assume, for contradiction, that there are only finitely many, and that p1, …, pk is the complete list. Form the integer

N=∏i=1kpi+1

Read the product sign as “multiply all the primes on the alleged complete list,” then add 1. Since N is greater than 1, it has at least one prime divisor q. By the assumption that the list is complete, q must equal one of the listed primes, say pj. But pj divides the product of all listed primes, and if it also divided N, it would divide their difference, 1. No prime divides 1. This contradiction shows that the complete finite list cannot exist.

Equivalently, N leaves remainder 1 when divided by every listed pi. Every prime divisor of N therefore lies outside the list. We did not need to decide whether N itself is prime. The proof works for any proposed finite complete list, however long, because finite multiplication and the prime-divisor fact still apply. It is a proof of unending supply, not a formula that produces primes in increasing order.

Notice the exact role of the assumption. We are not saying that no finite list of primes can be written; the two lists above are perfectly good finite lists. We are saying that no such list can contain all primes. This distinction is what the contradiction establishes.

Use the idea yourself

1. Calculate and explain. Starting from the primes 2, 3, 5 and 7, compute their product plus one. Show without testing its primality that none of the four listed primes divides it.

Hint for question 1

First multiply 2 × 3 × 5 × 7. What remainder does a product plus one leave on division by any factor of the product?

Full solution to question 1

The product is 210, so the new number is 211. Each of 2, 3, 5 and 7 divides 210. If any also divided 211, it would divide 211 − 210 = 1, which is impossible for a prime. Thus none divides 211. We need not determine whether 211 is prime to make that conclusion.

2. Repair an argument. A reader writes: “Take all the primes, multiply them, add one, and the answer is a new prime.” Identify two problems with this sentence and state a correct version.

Hint for question 2

Think about what is being assumed for contradiction, then recall the factorization of 30,031.

Full solution to question 2

First, “all the primes” cannot be used as an already known finite list; the proof temporarily assumes a finite complete list and then refutes it. Second, the product plus one may be composite: 30,031 = 59 × 509. Correctly stated: for any proposed finite list containing every prime, its product plus one has a prime divisor outside that list, contradicting completeness.

The list can always be challenged

Whatever finite list claims to contain every prime, its product plus one has a prime factor missing from the list. That is enough to show that the primes never run out. The construction does not claim its output is prime, does not order the primes, and does not address how often primes occur among large integers. You can stop with a complete proof here. If you return later, divisibility and the Euclidean algorithm provide another small world of questions about how integers fit together.
