Different dates, one weekday

A community hall runs an event every seven days. Two dates can be different numbers but occupy the same place in this weekly cycle. We want a rule that groups dates correctly without accidentally putting a date in two incompatible groups.

Use integers to label days, with day 0 some chosen Monday. Say a~b when 7 divides a−b; in other words a−b=7k for some integer k. A relation is a rule that can hold between pairs of objects. A relation is an equivalence relation when it is reflexive (a~a), symmetric (a~b implies b~a), and transitive (a~b and b~c imply a~c). These definitions and integer divisibility form the local prerequisite capsule.

Our goal is to prove that same-weekday relation meets all three conditions and to see how its equivalence classes form a partition. The calendar story motivates the rule, but the proof is arithmetic about all integers, including negative day labels.

Three dates in one class

Worked example. Days 2, 9, and −5 are in the same weekly position. The differences 9−2=7 and 2−(−5)=7 are divisible by 7; also 9−(−5)=14. If day 2 was a Wednesday under our starting convention, then so are day 9 and day −5. The labels can be arbitrarily far apart; the relation records only remainder modulo 7.

Why same weekday is an equivalence relation

Exact statement. On ℤ, the relation a~b defined by 7|(a−b) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. Consequently its classes are either identical or disjoint and partition ℤ into seven classes.

a∼b⇔7|(a−b)

Complete proof of the three properties. Reflexivity: a−a=0=7·0, so a~a. Symmetry: if a−b=7k, then b−a=7(−k), so b~a. Transitivity: if a−b=7k and b−c=7ℓ, add the equations to get a−c=7(k+ℓ), so a~c. Each step uses only that integers are closed under negation and addition.

For the partition claim, every a belongs to [a] by reflexivity. If a class [a] and [b] share some c, then c~a and c~b. Symmetry gives a~c, and transitivity gives a~b. For any x~a, transitivity with a~b yields x~b; the reverse direction follows by symmetry. Thus [a]=[b]. Classes that meet cannot be partly overlapping. Division with remainder gives every integer a exactly one remainder r in {0,…,6}; hence exactly seven different classes occur. The last statement uses the standard integer division algorithm, stated here but not reproved.

A failure case shows why all three tests matter. Define aRb on integers by a≤b. This relation is reflexive and transitive, but not symmetric: 2≤3 while 3≤2 is false. The “class” of 2 under this rule would not behave like a same-weekday group. Merely seeing overlapping arrows in a drawing cannot establish equivalence.

Test a rule, then a class

1. For relation a~b when 5 divides a−b, decide whether −7 and 8 are related, and list three other integers in [−7].

Hint for question 1

Compute the difference, then add or subtract multiples of 5.

Full solution to question 1

8−(−7)=15=5·3, so they are related. Examples in [−7] include −12, −2, and 3, because each differs from −7 by a multiple of 5. The full class is {−7+5k:k∈ℤ}.

2. On ℤ define aRb when a and b have the same sign, with zero called neither positive nor negative. If “same sign” means both positive or both negative, is R an equivalence relation?

Hint for question 2

Check reflexivity at zero first.

Full solution to question 2

No. For a=0, neither “both positive” nor “both negative” holds for (0,0), so 0R0 is false. Reflexivity fails. One could repair the rule by putting zero in its own class, but that would be a different relation requiring a fresh check of all three properties.

A correct classification states the underlying set and the exact rule. Otherwise a special case such as zero can be silently lost. In the weekday relation, every integer, including zero and negative labels, passes reflexivity.

A relation earns its classes

The divisible-difference rule for seven-day cycles is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. Those properties make its classes disjoint or identical, and integer remainders supply seven of them. We proved the relation properties and the class-overlap implication; the standard division algorithm supplies the exact count of seven.

Stop when you can test all three properties on a proposed grouping rule and exhibit a member of a class. This finite-cycle grouping is distinct from the infinity question of whether every infinite collection can be listed.
