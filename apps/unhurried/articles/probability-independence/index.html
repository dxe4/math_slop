Probability and conditioning

A probability model names the possible outcomes and their probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. In a finite model where every outcome is equally likely, an event's probability is its number of outcomes divided by the total number.

Worked example. Roll a fair six-sided die labelled 1 through 6. The event “greater than 3” has outcomes {4,5,6}, so its probability is 3/6=1/2. If you learn that the result is even, only {2,4,6} remain possible. These three outcomes are equally likely under the condition, and two of them are greater than 3.

P(greater than 3∣even)=23

The vertical bar reads “given.” Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes and renormalizes their probabilities. The event you condition on must have positive probability for this ordinary ratio definition.

Try one. Given that the die result is odd, what is the probability it is less than 4?

Worked answer

The possible odd results are {1,3,5}. Two of them, 1 and 3, are less than 4, giving probability 2/3. The denominator counts the conditioned possibilities.

Watch the assumption. Counting outcomes equally works here because the die is fair. For unequal probabilities, add the assigned probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

Does the first colour change the second chance?

Draw twice uniformly from three red and two blue tokens. Does the first colour inform the second? Specify whether the first token is replaced and the bag mixed between draws.

Background capsule. An event is a collection of outcomes. Let A mean “first draw is red” and B mean “second draw is red.” We call them independent if learning whether the first event occurred leaves the chance of the second unchanged. A uniform draw means each token currently in the bag has the same chance of being chosen.

Read the symbols in words

P(A) is the probability of A. A ∩ B means both events occur, here red twice. The bar in P(B | A) reads “given”: second red given first red. Juxtaposed probabilities are multiplied. Before the first-colour clue the probability is unconditional; after it, conditional.

Keep the two procedures separate

  1. With replacement. Put the first token back and mix. The bag again has three red tokens out of five, whatever the first colour was. A fresh uniform draw gives P(B | A) = 3/5. The chance of red twice is (3/5) × (3/5) = 9/25.
  2. Without replacement. Keep the first token out. After a red, two red tokens remain among four, so P(B | A) = 2/4 = 1/2. The chance of red twice is (3/5) × (2/4) = 3/10. After a blue instead, three of the four remaining tokens are red: a different second-red chance, 3/4.

Worked example. The four colour sequences below cover every possible result. They do not have equal chances. Each row multiplies the chance of its first colour by the chance of its second colour after that first draw.

Both procedures give unconditional second-red probability 3/5. Only the fresh-uniform replacement procedure makes these events independent: without replacement, a first red changes that chance to 1/2. Equal probabilities at two positions do not establish independence.

A test that settles the question

Exact statement. Events A and B are independent exactly when the chance of both equals the product of their individual chances:

P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)

In both token procedures, the individual chances are 3/5 and 3/5, whose product is 9/25. With replacement the chance of both is 9/25: the equality holds. Without replacement it is 3/10, which differs from 9/25: the equality fails.

For P(A)>0, P(B|A)=P(A∩B)/P(A). Dividing the product equality by P(A) gives P(B|A)=P(B); multiplying recovers it. Reversing A and B gives the corresponding equivalence when P(B)>0.

Replacement here includes a fresh uniform draw from the restored bag. Physical replacement alone, particularly without mixing, does not specify that rule. Independence belongs to specified events under a specified probability model; unrelated-sounding descriptions do not establish it.

Disjoint events share no outcomes. On a fair die, “one” and “six” are disjoint but dependent: one rules out six, with joint chance 0 versus product (1/6)(1/6)=1/36. Positive-probability disjoint events cannot be independent.

Why use the product definition for a zero-chance event?

If P(A) = 0, the chance of A ∩ B is also zero. The product equality holds for any B. But the conditional fraction would divide by zero, so P(B | A) is undefined in this finite model. The product definition covers this edge case without that division.

Optional investigation: can every pair pass while the triple fails?

Three events require another question

The two-event test above settles exactly the two-event question. Now consider three events A, B and C. They are pairwise independent when each of the pairs A,B; A,C; and B,C passes its product test. They are mutually independent when those three equalities hold and the triple also satisfies P(A∩B∩C)=P(A)P(B)P(C). More generally, mutual independence requires the product equality for every nonempty selection of the events. A triple equality alone does not replace the pair tests.

Extra background. A bit takes values 0 and 1. Let A={X=1}, B={Y=1}, C={Z=1}. A word such as 101 records the three bits in order. The joint law assigns probabilities to all eight words. A one-bit marginal sums words with that bit fixed; a pair table sums over the third bit. These are exact model probabilities.

A classical parity construction

Choose X and Y as two independent fair bits, so their four pairs are equally likely. Set Z to 1 exactly when X and Y differ: Z=X xor Y. The resulting complete words are 000, 011, 101 and 110, each with probability 1/4; the other four words have probability zero. This is the classical pairwise-but-not-mutual parity example, also developed using two dice in the MIT source below.

Check all three pairs. Reading XY across those four words gives 00,01,10,11. Reading XZ gives 00,01,11,10, and reading YZ gives 00,11,01,10. Each pair therefore has four cells of probability 1/4. Each individual bit is fair, so every pair cell equals (1/2)(1/2). Nevertheless P(X=1,Y=1,Z=1)=0, whereas the product of the three one-bit probabilities is 1/8. Every pair passes; the triple fails.

Predict before inspecting. Does one bit leaving Z fair imply two bits do? Do matching one-bit and pair laws force matching count laws? Compare the same word under XOR and independent fair bits; which probabilities fail to factor?

Why the combined clue matters

Pairwise independence gives P(Z=1|X=1)=1/2 and P(Z=1|Y=1)=1/2. But the combined event X=1,Y=1 has probability 1/4, and XOR makes Z=0 there. Hence P(Z=1|X=1,Y=1)=0. The conditioning event is possible even though the triple 111 is impossible. Independence from either clue separately does not imply independence from their combined information.

For binary X,Y,Z, mutual independence of A,B,C is equivalent to factorization of every word probability. Complements follow by subtraction: P(110)=P(A∩B)−P(A∩B∩C)=P(A)P(B)(1−P(C)); P(100)=P(A)−P(A∩B)−P(A∩C)+P(A∩B∩C)=P(A)(1−P(B))(1−P(C)). Permutation gives the other cases, and inclusion-exclusion gives P(000)=(1−P(A))(1−P(B))(1−P(C)). Conversely, summing factorized words gives the pair and triple equalities. The independent preset assigns 1/8 per word; XOR does not.

Which conclusions survive the weaker assumption?

Let K=X+Y+Z count the ones. XOR permits only K=0 with probability 1/4 and K=2 with probability 3/4. Independent fair bits instead give probabilities (1,3,3,1)/8 for K=0,1,2,3. Both counts have mean 3/2 and variance 3/4. These are different count laws with the same first two moments.

The mean needs no independence: linearity gives E[K]=E[X]+E[Y]+E[Z]=3/2. Since each bit squares to itself, K²=X+Y+Z+2XY+2XZ+2YZ. Pairwise independence is enough to give E[XY]=E[XZ]=E[YZ]=1/4, hence E[K²]=3/2+2(3/4)=3 and Var(K)=3−(3/2)²=3/4. Those pair products do not determine P(111) or all the individual word probabilities.

The binomial derivation uses the stronger full-sequence product rule: a specified word with k successes has probability pᵏ(1−p)³⁻ᵏ, and adding the words with the same k gives the count law. Matching a mean, variance, or even the whole binomial count law does not establish independence of the underlying trials.

A complete binomial count can still hide dependent trials

Assign lexicographic words 000,001,010,011,100,101,110,111 the probabilities (1,1,0,2,2,0,1,1)/8. Each bit is fair. Adding by number of ones gives the exact binomial count probabilities (1,3,3,1)/8. But P(X=1,Z=1)=P(101)+P(111)=1/8, not 1/4, so those two bits are dependent. Even the triple probability is 1/8 here: the triple-product equality alone is insufficient. The ordering information discarded by K matters.

Try two cases

Exercise 1. A fair six-sided die is rolled twice independently. Let A be “first roll is even” and B be “second roll exceeds four.” Check the product equality.

Hint for exercise 1

Count choices for each roll separately. Exceeds four means five or six.

Full solution to exercise 1

A has chance 3/6 = 1/2 and B has chance 2/6 = 1/3. Of 36 equally likely ordered pairs, 3 × 2 = 6 satisfy both. Thus P(A ∩ B) = 6/36 = 1/6 = (1/2) × (1/3). The events are independent under the stated independent-roll assumption.

Exercise 2. One fair die is rolled. Are “even” and “greater than three” independent? Predict first, then list the overlap.

Hint for exercise 2

List both sets and count their shared faces. Compare that probability with the product of the two separate probabilities.

Full solution to exercise 2

Even is {2,4,6}, and greater than three is {4,5,6}; each has chance 1/2. Their overlap is {4,6}, chance 2/6 = 1/3. Since 1/3 differs from (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4, they are dependent. Learning “even” makes “greater than three” have chance 2/3 instead of 1/2.

A changed task: pairwise independent bits with chance one third

Exercise 3. Now X,Y,Z each have success probability 1/3, and every pair is independent. No exchangeability assumption is supplied. Write t=P(111). (a) Derive all eight word probabilities from these constraints, and find the full possible range of t. (b) Derive the law, mean and variance of K=X+Y+Z. At t=0, realize the model using nine equally likely tickets and compare its count law with Binomial(3,1/3). (c) Find exactly when the bits are mutually independent within this constrained family. (d) Compute P(Z=1|X=1,Y=1) throughout the family and compare it with conditioning on X alone. This task is outside the fair-source controls.

Hint for exercise 3

Every pair has both-success probability 1/9. Subtract t to get each two-success word, then use a one-bit success probability to get each one-success word. Normalize to get 000. Require every mass to be nonnegative. Add the rows with the same success count; for moments, use K²=X+Y+Z+2XY+2XZ+2YZ. For mutual independence, 111 must have probability (1/3)³, and all the other words must agree too. The combined conditioning event always has probability 1/9.

Full solution to exercise 3

(a) Derive, rather than assume, the symmetry. Pairwise independence gives P(X=1,Y=1)=P(X=1,Z=1)=P(Y=1,Z=1)=1/9. Removing 111 leaves P(110)=P(101)=P(011)=1/9−t. For example, the X-success marginal gives P(100)=1/3−P(101)−P(110)−P(111)=1/9+t. The other two marginals similarly give P(010)=P(001)=1/9+t. Normalization leaves P(000)=1/3−t. Thus the constraints themselves force the equal masses in each success-count class:

Nonnegativity of 111 and the two-success words requires 0≤t≤1/9. These bounds also make every other mass nonnegative, with total 1. They suffice: each pair table in 00,01,10,11 order is (4,2,2,1)/9, the Bernoulli(1/3) marginal products. For example XY=00 has mass (1/3−t)+(1/9+t)=4/9 and XY=11 has (1/9−t)+t=1/9. The displayed words give the remaining cells and pairs.

(b) Count and moments. Grouping words gives P(K=0,1,2,3)=(1/3−t,1/3+3t,1/3−3t,t). Linearity gives E[K]=3(1/3)=1; the three pair expectations 1/9 give E[K²]=1+2(3/9)=5/3, hence Var(K)=2/3 throughout. At t=0, nine equally likely tickets realize the law: three labelled 000 and one each of 001,010,100,011,101,110; none is 111. K is uniform on {0,1,2}, unlike Binomial(3,1/3)'s law (8,12,6,1)/27, despite matching mean 1 and variance 2/3.

(c) Full independence within this family. Necessarily t=P(111)=(1/3)³=1/27. Conversely this value gives masses 8/27 for 000, 4/27 per one-success word, 2/27 per two-success word and 1/27 for 111. Each factors as (1/3)ᵏ(2/3)³⁻ᵏ, proving mutual independence. Within this constrained family, K is binomial exactly at this same t, since P(K=3)=t. The earlier counterexample prevents extending that converse to arbitrary trial laws.

(d) One clue versus two. Pairwise independence gives P(Z=1|X=1)=1/3. The combined clue X=1,Y=1 has probability 1/9>0 throughout, giving P(Z=1|X=1,Y=1)=9t. This ranges from 0 to 1 with fixed marginals, pair tables and count moments: it is 1/3 at t=1/27, forces Z=0 at t=0, and Z=1 at t=1/9. These endpoint conditionals remain defined because their clue has positive probability.

One question to take away

Check independence by comparing the joint probability with the product of marginals in one model. Reconstruct why the bag's second-red marginal stays 3/5 without replacement. The binomial model needs independence across full trial sequences, beyond one successful pair test.
