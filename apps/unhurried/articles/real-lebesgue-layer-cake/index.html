Integrate by grouping values

Riemann sums divide the input axis into intervals. The Lebesgue approach can instead ask how much measurable set receives each height. This is easy to compute for a simple function with finitely many values, and it suggests the definition for any nonnegative measurable function. We will calculate a simple integral and state the definition precisely, while leaving construction of Lebesgue measure itself outside this gateway lesson.

Background capsule. A σ-algebra is a collection of subsets containing X and closed under complements and countable unions; its members are measurable sets. A measure μ assigns nonnegative sizes to those sets, with μ(∅)=0 and μ of a countable disjoint union equal to the sum of their sizes. The indicator 1A(x) equals one on A and zero elsewhere. A nonnegative simple function takes finitely many nonnegative values and is measurable when its value sets are measurable. A function f is measurable if {x:f(x)>t} is measurable for each real t. We assume a measure space (X,Σ,μ) is already given.

Three heights on one interval

Worked example. On X=[0,1] with Lebesgue measure, let s=2 on [0,1/4), s=5 on [1/4,1/2), and s=0 on [1/2,1]. The first two pieces each have length 1/4. Group by value: two units of height over the first quarter contribute 2·1/4=1/2, five units over the next quarter contribute 5·1/4=5/4, and the rest contributes zero. Thus ∫s dμ=7/4. Whether the first and second sets are contiguous is irrelevant; only their measures and assigned values enter this simple-function integral.

We used disjoint value sets. If a simple function has an overlapping representation, refine the sets into disjoint pieces before summing. Standard measure additivity then shows the result does not depend on the particular representation; we do not reconstruct that general well-definedness argument here.

From simple functions to a nonnegative integral

For s=Σj=1maj1Aⱼ with disjoint measurable Aⱼ and aⱼ≥0, define ∫s dμ=Σaⱼμ(Aⱼ), using the convention 0·∞=0 when a zero-height set has infinite measure. For a nonnegative measurable f, define:

∫fdμ=sup{∫sdμ:0≤s≤f,s simple}

The supremum is allowed to be +∞. For the worked s itself, the supremum equals 7/4: choosing the simple function s gives at least 7/4, while monotonicity for simple functions bounds every simple t≤s above by ∫s. That monotonicity follows by refining both value partitions into common disjoint measurable pieces and comparing heights piece by piece. This explains why the definition extends the earlier finite calculation rather than assigning a different answer.

As a second worked comparison, let f=1ℚ∩[0,1]. The rational set is countable and Lebesgue null. Its indicator is a simple measurable function. Its integral is 1·μ(ℚ∩[0,1])=0, although the Riemann integral does not exist. This is possible because the new definition uses measured sets of positions, not the supremum in every input interval.

The word “layer cake” is sometimes used for the identity ∫f dμ=∫₀∞ μ({f>t})dt for nonnegative measurable f. We can verify it for our finite example without claiming the general theorem: the set where s>t has measure 1/2 for 0≤t<2, measure 1/4 for 2≤t<5, and zero above 5. The area under those height-distribution levels is 2·(1/2)+3·(1/4)=7/4. Proving the identity for arbitrary measurable f needs approximation and a convergence theorem; that proof is beyond this article.

For signed functions, the usual integral uses positive and negative parts and requires care when both integrals are infinite. Nothing here declares ∞−∞ meaningful. Likewise, the existence of Lebesgue measure and its countable additivity are assumptions of this gateway, not consequences of a picture or a finite table.

Compute and check definitions

1. Calculate. On [0,2], let u=3 on [0,1/2), u=1 on [1/2,3/2), and u=0 elsewhere. Find ∫u and its height-distribution calculation.

Hint

The two nonzero value sets have measures 1/2 and 1.

Worked solution

The simple integral is 3·(1/2)+1·1=5/2. For 0≤t<1, {u>t} has measure 3/2; for 1≤t<3 it has measure 1/2; above 3 it has measure zero. The distribution-area calculation is 1·(3/2)+2·(1/2)=5/2. Endpoint choices for t affect only isolated heights and hence do not change the latter ordinary integral.

2. Explain a boundary. Why can we not define ∫v by simply adding a negative coefficient to the nonnegative formula and then allowing arbitrary +∞ and −∞ terms?

Hint

Consider the arithmetic expression +∞−∞.

Worked solution

Infinite positive and negative contributions may each diverge, and their difference has no defined value. A signed measurable v is handled by v⁺=max(v,0) and v⁻=max(−v,0); ∫v=∫v⁺−∫v⁻ is defined only when at least one side is finite, and absolute integrability requires both finite. The nonnegative supremum definition alone does not settle this cancellation problem.

A precise gateway, not a measure construction

Simple functions convert measured value sets into finite sums. The nonnegative Lebesgue integral is the supremum of those sums from below. We have calculated examples and marked the assumed machinery: measurable sets, countable additivity, and extension to signed functions. The next optional question is when limits commute with this integral.
