Question and background

Two routes can look equally long on a flat coordinate map yet have different lengths on a sphere. The first fundamental form repairs that mismatch: it translates small coordinate changes into actual distances along a surface.

Local capsule. A regular patch X(u,v) maps two local coordinates into ℝ³; its partial derivatives Xᵤ and Xᵥ are independent tangent vectors. If a route has coordinates u(t),v(t), its spatial velocity is Xᵤu′+Xᵥv′ by the multivariable chain rule. The dot product gives squared length: ||w||²=w·w. We call E=Xᵤ·Xᵤ, F=Xᵤ·Xᵥ, and G=Xᵥ·Xᵥ. These values depend on the chosen coordinates, but the route's physical length does not.

This metric is local. To measure a path crossing a chart boundary, split it into chart pieces or use another patch. At a coordinate singularity, the displayed coefficients may be misleading because the two tangent vectors no longer form a valid basis.

A worked example

Worked example. Use the sphere patch X(θ,λ)=(Rsinθ cosλ,Rsinθ sinλ,Rcosθ), with 0<θ<π and R>0. Its derivative vectors are orthogonal, with ||Xθ||=R and ||Xλ||=Rsinθ. Therefore E=R², F=0, and G=R²sin²θ. For a route at constant θ=θ₀, only longitude changes, and length between longitudes λ₁ and λ₂, along the chosen monotone interval, is Rsinθ₀ |λ₂−λ₁|.

At the equator θ₀=π/2, a quarter turn in longitude has length Rπ/2. At colatitude θ₀=π/6, the same longitude change has length Rπ/4, because the latitude circle has radius R/2. A map with equal longitude spacing can hide this difference. The metric coefficient G carries it explicitly.

For a meridian, keep λ fixed and vary θ. Then the length is R|θ₂−θ₁|. The coefficients thus recover both familiar circle-arc formulas from one local rule. They also allow routes where θ and λ change together, by integrating the combined squared speed.

Why the mixed term matters

Use the tilted coordinate grid X(u,v)=(u+v,v,0) on a plane. Its tangent vectors are Xᵤ=(1,0,0) and Xᵥ=(1,1,0). Thus E=1, F=1 and G=2. They are independent, and EG−F²=2−1=1>0, so this is a regular patch. The coordinates are slanted even though the surface is flat.

Follow u=t and v=−t for 0≤t≤1. In space, γ(t)=(0,−t,0), a vertical unit segment with speed one. The metric gives the same result: squared speed is 1·1²+2·1·1·(−1)+2·(−1)²=1−2+2=1. Omitting the mixed term would incorrectly give squared speed three and length √3.

The negative contribution does not make a squared length negative. It compensates for the overlap of the two nonorthogonal coordinate directions. The full positive quadratic expression, rather than either coefficient separately, measures a tangent displacement. Orthogonal coordinates such as the sphere grid have F=0; general coordinate grids need the cross term.

The statement and its boundary

Exact statement. For a C¹ regular surface patch and a piecewise C¹ coordinate route c(t)=(u(t),v(t)), its spatial image γ(t)=X(c(t)) has length given by the metric coefficients:

ds2=Edu2+2Fdudv+Gdv2

Derivation: the chain rule gives γ′=u′Xᵤ+v′Xᵥ. Expanding its dot product with itself yields ||γ′||²=E(u′)²+2Fu′v′+G(v′)². Taking the nonnegative square root and integrating in t gives the route length. This is a full derivation from the Euclidean length formula. The shorthand ds² records the same quadratic expression for a tiny coordinate displacement; it is not an ordinary algebraic square of a finite distance between arbitrary points.

Because Xᵤ and Xᵥ are independent, E>0, G>0 and EG−F²>0. This positivity makes the quadratic expression strictly positive for every nonzero tangent displacement. At a sphere pole G=0 in these coordinates, and the rank test fails. That is a coordinate failure, not proof that distances vanish near the pole.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. a cylinder helix. For X(u,v)=(2cos u,2sin u,v), find E,F,G and the length of c(t)=(t,t), 0≤t≤π.

Hint for exercise 1

Find the two partial derivatives and substitute u′=v′=1.

Full solution to exercise 1

Xᵤ=(-2sin u,2cos u,0), Xᵥ=(0,0,1). Thus E=4, F=0, G=1. The route has squared speed 4+1=5, hence constant speed √5 and length ∫₀^π√5 dt=π√5. The route circles halfway around while rising by π units.

Exercise 2. coordinate units. Why does G tend to zero near a sphere pole, and why is “eastward distance is always zero there” a wrong conclusion for a neighborhood?

Hint for exercise 2

Look at the physical radius of a small latitude circle and the exact rank condition at the pole.

Full solution to exercise 2

A change of longitude at fixed θ follows a circle of radius Rsinθ, so the squared speed per unit longitude is G=R²sin²θ. As θ→0 this circle shrinks. Exactly at the pole every longitude labels one point and Xλ=0, so longitude is no longer an independent coordinate. Nearby points still have positive distances; we must use a valid chart or compare their spatial positions.

What to carry forward

The first fundamental form measures tangent lengths through three dot products. It tells us how to compute route lengths once the patch is regular, but it does not by itself choose the shortest route. That question leads to geodesics.

Keep checking the coordinate domain. A formula derived in one chart can be trusted where its tangent vectors remain independent. Smooth surfaces often need several overlapping charts, with calculations agreeing wherever both charts describe the same path.
