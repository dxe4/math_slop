Can a clock multiplication be undone?

On a seven-position clock, multiplying a position by 3 sends 0,1,2,3,4,5,6 to 0,3,6,2,5,1,4. Every output appears once. Multiplying by 3 is reversible: multiplication by 5 reverses it because 3·5=15 has remainder 1 modulo 7. On an eight-position clock, multiplying by 2 sends both 0 and 4 to 0. That collision makes reversal impossible. The question is to predict the difference without trying every position.

Local background: a ≡ b (mod m) means m divides a−b, with m a positive integer. The gcd of a and m is their greatest positive common divisor. Bézout's identity says that integers u,v satisfy au+mv=gcd(a,m); it follows by reversing the Euclidean remainder equations. We will use that identity and show the exact condition for an inverse. The clock picture is for intuition; the divisibility definition is what supports a proof.

Find one inverse and one collision

Worked example. Take a=5 and m=12. The Euclidean ledger is 12=2·5+2, then 5=2·2+1. Reverse it: 1=5−2·2=5−2(12−2·5)=5·5−2·12. Thus 5·5 ≡ 1 (mod 12), so the inverse of 5 is also 5. For example, if 5x ≡ 7 (mod 12), multiply both sides by 5: 25x ≡ 35, hence x ≡ 11 (mod 12). Check 5·11=55 ≡ 7.

The coprimality test

Inverse criterion. An integer a has a multiplicative inverse modulo m≥2 exactly when gcd(a,m)=1. If the gcd is 1, Bézout supplies integers u,v with au+mv=1. Reducing modulo m removes mv, giving au ≡ 1; u is an inverse. Conversely, suppose au ≡ 1. Then m divides au−1, so au−mv=1 for some integer v. Any common divisor of a and m divides the left side and therefore divides 1. Their greatest common divisor is 1. Both directions are proved.

(∃u∈ℤ:au≡1(modm))⇔gcd(a,m)=1

An inverse is unique as a residue: if au ≡ av ≡ 1, multiply the first equation by v and the second by u to obtain u ≡ v. Or use the inverse to cancel a from au ≡ av. That cancellation only works when a has an inverse. More generally, multiplication by a permutes all m residues exactly when gcd(a,m)=1. If an inverse exists, equal outputs ax ≡ ay imply x ≡ y, so the finite map is one-to-one and onto. If d=gcd(a,m)>1, the distinct residues 0 and m/d both map to 0 because a(m/d)=(a/d)m.

Failure case. From 2x ≡ 2 (mod 8), cancelling 2 would claim x ≡ 1 (mod 8). Yet x=5 also works: 2·5=10 ≡ 2. Dividing a congruence by a noninvertible number can lose solutions. One may reduce the modulus carefully in some problems, but ordinary cancellation modulo the original m is invalid.

Worked example: solving a congruence with a negative inverse

Solve 4x ≡ 3 (mod 9). The identity 1 = 9 − 2·4 says that −2 is an inverse of 4 modulo 9. Its standard nonnegative representative is 7, since −2 and 7 differ by 9. Multiplying by −2 gives x ≡ −6 ≡ 3 (mod 9). Check the candidate: 4·3 = 12 = 9 + 3.

Every integer 3 + 9t satisfies the original congruence, because multiplying the added term by 4 still gives a multiple of 9. Conversely, multiplying any alleged solution by the same inverse forces that residue. Thus the answer is one residue class, containing infinitely many integers. A negative coefficient from back-substitution is a valid inverse; discarding it because it is negative would discard a useful certificate.

Use a certificate rather than a guess

1. Find the inverse of 7 modulo 20 and solve 7x ≡ 9 (mod 20).

Hint for question 1

20=2·7+6 and 7=6+1; reverse those equations.

Full solution to question 1

1=7−6=7−(20−2·7)=3·7−20, so 3 is an inverse of 7 modulo 20. Multiplying 7x ≡ 9 by 3 yields x ≡ 27 ≡ 7 (mod 20). Check: 7·7=49 ≡ 9. The solution is unique modulo 20 because multiplication by 7 is reversible.

2. Explain why 6 has no inverse modulo 15 and exhibit two distinct inputs with the same output after multiplication by 6.

Hint for question 2

The gcd is 3. Try inputs differing by 15/3.

Full solution to question 2

gcd(6,15)=3, so an inverse would force the impossible equation 6u−15v=1, whose left side is divisible by 3. Inputs 0 and 5 are distinct modulo 15, while 6·0 ≡ 0 and 6·5=30 ≡ 0. The collision shows directly why a reversible multiplication map is impossible.

Reversal needs a gcd of one

Bézout gives an inverse exactly for numbers coprime to the modulus. It also exposes the obstruction when a shared factor produces collisions. This is the gate for solving simultaneous clock conditions and for choosing a toy public-key exponent. Tables are good checks, while the two-direction argument establishes the general rule.
