Boundary values recover an interior value

A holomorphic function has a striking rigidity: values on a surrounding circle determine every value inside. Cauchy's integral formula gives the reconstruction exactly. This lesson states its domain assumptions, checks a polynomial example and explains the mechanism, while keeping the proof debt of the full Cauchy theorem visible.

Background capsule. A function is holomorphic on an open set when its complex derivative exists at every point there. A counterclockwise circle C: z=c+Reit, 0≤t≤2π, has radius R>0 and bounds the closed disk |z−c|≤R. A contour integral uses ∫Cg(z)dz=∫g(z(t))z′(t)dt. The kernel 1/(z−a) is singular at a; it is still integrable along C when a lies strictly inside, because then a is off the path.

Reconstruct a quadratic at its center

Worked example. Let f(z)=z²+1, use the unit circle C centered at zero, and choose a=0. Then f(z)/z=z+1/z. Parameterize z=eit. The first term integrates to ∫02πie2itdt=0, while the second integrates to 2πi. Thus (1/2πi)∮Cf(z)/z dz=1=f(0). The zero term is not magic: e2it completes two periods and its positive and negative contributions cancel.

For a=1/2, the same formula predicts f(1/2)=5/4. One could compute by expanding around a: z²+1=(a²+1)+2a(z−a)+(z−a)². Divide by z−a. The first term contributes (a²+1)·2πi because the circle encloses a; the remaining polynomial terms have entire antiderivatives and integrate to zero. This is a special-case derivation that does not yet prove the formula for arbitrary holomorphic f.

Keep the target strictly away from the rim. On the unit circle and at a=1/2, the reverse triangle inequality gives |z−a|≥1/2, so the kernel's modulus is at most 2. That finite bound makes this interior reconstruction harmless along the contour despite its singularity at a inside. If a approaches the rim, the bound worsens; if a lies on the rim, the displayed ordinary formula has a singular integrand on its path and no longer applies. Changing the target therefore requires rechecking its location, not only substituting a different number.

State the theorem with its circle

Cauchy's integral formula, disk case. Let f be holomorphic on an open neighborhood of the closed disk |z−c|≤R. Let C be its counterclockwise boundary, and let |a−c|<R. Then

f(a)=12πi∮Cf(z)z−adz

The neighborhood assumption is stronger than merely knowing f on the circle: it permits Cauchy theory in the disk and on a slight enlargement. The theorem is invoked here; a full proof of Cauchy's theorem for arbitrary holomorphic f, including the analytic estimates behind shrinking a puncture, is deferred. The algebra below shows why the answer has this form once that theorem is available.

Write f(z)/(z−a)=f(a)/(z−a)+[f(z)−f(a)]/(z−a). The second quotient extends holomorphically across a, taking value f′(a); this extension uses differentiability, and holomorphy of it follows from standard Cauchy theory. Its integral around C is zero by Cauchy's theorem. The first quotient has integral 2πi because C winds once counterclockwise around a. Hence the formula. This is a theorem-dependent derivation, not an independent proof of Cauchy's theorem itself.

A derivative version follows under the same hypotheses by differentiating the formula with respect to a on compact subsets of the disk: f′(a)=(1/2πi)∮Cf(z)/(z−a)² dz. Justifying differentiation under this contour integral uses a uniform lower bound on |z−a| away from C. It is a consequence, not an assumption needed for the worked quadratic.

Use the kernel and audit the domain

1. Calculate. Evaluate ∮|z|=2(3z+2)/(z−i) dz, counterclockwise.

Hint for 1

Set f(z)=3z+2 and a=i; check |i|<2.

Worked solution for 1

The polynomial is entire, and a=i lies inside the radius-two circle. Cauchy's formula gives the integral 2πi f(i)=2πi(2+3i)=−6π+4πi. The numerator's constant and linear parts are both accounted for.

2. Explain. A proposed use of the formula sets f(z)=1/z on |z|=2 and a=1. Why does this fail even though f(a)=1 exists?

Hint for 2

Inspect every point of the closed disk, not merely a and the boundary.

Worked solution for 2

The disk contains zero, where 1/z is undefined. Thus f is not holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed disk, so the formula's hypothesis fails. Indeed the integrand 1/[z(z−1)] has poles at both zero and one; treating only the pole at a misses a contribution. Knowing f(a) is insufficient.

Boundary data with a domain guarantee

Under holomorphy near a closed disk, the boundary integral recovers an interior value. Polynomial calculations verify the mechanism directly; the universal theorem rests on Cauchy's theorem, which this article states but does not prove independently. A singularity anywhere in the disk can break the reconstruction hypothesis even if the desired interior value happens to exist.
