Can every derivative miss a positive function?

Knowing every derivative at a point sounds like complete local knowledge. It is not. We will build a function that is positive on either side of zero yet whose value and every derivative at zero vanish. Its Taylor series at zero is therefore identically zero and disagrees with the function at every nearby nonzero point.

Background capsule. A function is smooth if derivatives of every finite order exist and are continuous. It is analytic at zero if it agrees with its Taylor power series on some open interval around zero. Smoothness concerns each fixed derivative; analyticity also requires an infinite sequence of approximations to converge to the function. We write eu for the exponential and use that ey grows faster than any fixed power ym as y→∞. A short proof of the needed estimate appears below.

An almost invisible bump at the origin

Worked example. Define f(0)=0 and f(x)=exp(−1/x²) when x≠0. This f is positive at every nonzero real x. Yet f(0.5)=e−4≈0.0183 and f(0.2)=e−25≈1.39×10−11. A normal-scale graph near zero may look flat on the axis. That image cannot decide whether f actually vanishes there; the formula says it does not.

Start with the first derivative at zero. The quotient [f(h)−f(0)]/h=e−1/h²/h. Its absolute value becomes √y e−y after putting y=1/h². This tends to zero, so f′(0)=0. Away from zero, ordinary chain rule gives f′(x)=2x−3e−1/x². This derivative also approaches zero at zero despite its factor x−3, because the exponential wins.

Repeat differentiation: each derivative away from zero is a finite sum of integer powers of x−1 times e−1/x². The powers become larger, but for each fixed differentiation order there are only finitely many of them. Each term still tends to zero. That is the mechanism behind complete flatness.

Why all coefficients vanish

Statement. The displayed function is smooth on the whole real line and f(n)(0)=0 for every integer n≥0. Its Taylor series at zero is zero for every x, while f(x)>0 when x≠0. Hence f is not analytic at zero.

f(x)=e−1/x2x≠00x=0⇒f(n)(0)=0

Dominance lemma. For every fixed q≥0, yqe−y→0 as y→∞. Choose an integer m>q. Since ey≥ym/m! for y>0 by the positive exponential series, 0≤yqe−y≤m! yq−m→0. Thus for every fixed positive integer k, |x|−ke−1/x²→0 as x→0 by y=1/x². This is the exact estimate needed; saying “exponentials win” alone would not prove it.

Induction. At x≠0, write the n-th derivative as Pₙ(1/x)e−1/x², where Pₙ is a polynomial. This holds for n=0 with P₀=1. Differentiating once gives e−1/x²[−x−2Pₙ′(1/x)+2x−3Pₙ(1/x)], again a polynomial in 1/x times the exponential. The dominance lemma makes this expression tend to zero as x→0. Assume the derivative f(n)(0) is zero. Its derivative at zero is the limit of f(n)(h)/h; that quotient is again a finite sum of negative powers of h times the exponential, so the lemma makes it zero. Induction establishes every derivative at zero and continuity of each derivative there.

Every Taylor coefficient f(n)(0)/n! is zero. Therefore every finite Taylor polynomial and the infinite series sum are zero. For any neighbourhood of zero, choose x≠0 inside it: f(x)=e−1/x²>0. The disagreement proves nonanalyticity. This example does not contradict finite Taylor bounds: for each fixed order a suitable residual bound exists, but controlling all orders uniformly as n grows is another question.

Use the mechanism, not the picture

1. Calculate. For h(x)=x−4e−1/x² when x≠0, find its limit at zero and justify it with the lemma.

Hint for 1

Put y=1/x², so x−4=y².

Worked solution for 1

h(x)=y²e−y with y→∞. Choose m=3 in the lemma: 0≤y²e−y≤3!/y=6/y→0. Hence h(x)→0 from both sides.

2. Explain. Does the fact that all Taylor coefficients of f at zero vanish imply f is zero on (−0.1,0.1)? Give an exact witness.

Hint for 2

Choose any nonzero number in that interval and inspect the defining formula.

Worked solution for 2

No. At x=1/20=0.05, f(x)=e−400, which is strictly positive, though extremely small. Its Taylor series still gives zero at x=1/20 because every coefficient is zero. The witness disproves equality on the interval without relying on numerical rounding.

Smoothness has a strict boundary

Each fixed derivative can be perfectly well behaved at zero while the whole infinite Taylor series misses the function. A plotted curve, finite jet of derivatives, or finite Taylor error bound cannot establish analyticity. To recover a function from its power series, one must separately prove that the residual tends to zero as the polynomial degree grows throughout a neighbourhood.
