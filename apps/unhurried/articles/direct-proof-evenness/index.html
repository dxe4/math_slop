Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

Can two witnesses make a third?

Adding 4 and 8 gives 12: two even integers have made another even integer. That sounds obvious after a few examples, but a proof must handle every pair of even integers, including zero and negative values. The useful move is to unpack “even” twice, then build the witness demanded by the conclusion. This lesson gives a complete direct proof of one theorem and a pattern that can be reused carefully elsewhere.

Background capsule. An integer x is even exactly when x = 2a for some integer a. Integers are closed under addition: if a and b are integers, so is a + b. “Direct proof” means start from the hypothesis and reach the conclusion through justified steps. These facts, plus elementary algebra, are all we require.

Work one pair, then remove its special numbers

Worked example. Take −8 and 14. The first is 2(−4), the second is 2·7, and their sum is 6 = 2·3. The result is even. The calculation suggests something general: the witnesses −4 and 7 add to the result’s witness 3. The special pair helps us see a construction, but it cannot by itself prove a statement about every pair. We now replace the two numerical witnesses by arbitrary integers.

A common failure is to write “the sum of two even numbers is even” as the middle step. That is exactly the claim being proved, so it supplies no reason. Another is to choose one witness for both inputs. The numbers need not share a half; use separate names until the algebra combines them.

The complete direct proof

2a+2b=2(a+b)

Theorem. For all even integers x and y, the sum x + y is even.

  1. Take the given inputs. Let x and y be any even integers. “Any” means we keep their values unspecified; we are not choosing a random pair or limiting ourselves to 4 and 8.
  2. Unpack the two definitions. There are integers a and b such that x = 2a and y = 2b. They get separate names because the inputs may have different halves.
  3. Rewrite the sum. Substitution gives x + y = 2a + 2b. Taking out the common factor 2 gives 2(a + b), as in the displayed equality.
  4. Check the new witness. Adding two integers gives an integer, so a + b is allowed as a witness. We have written the sum as two times an integer. By the definition of evenness, x + y is even.

The proof uses the hypotheses only to obtain witnesses. It does not assume what it needs to establish. Its generality comes from taking arbitrary x and y, not from checking many examples. The algebraic factorization is the bridge from input witnesses to output witness. One can also show the sum of three even integers is even by applying this theorem twice, but that extra inference needs the first sum to count as an even integer.

Be cautious when changing the conclusion. “The sum of an even and an odd integer is even” is false: 2 + 3 = 5. A proof that blindly factors a 2 would fail because an odd integer has the form 2b + 1, leaving an extra 1. That failure case shows why definitions must be unpacked exactly.

A guided first step. With the same inputs, fill the gap: x − y = 2( ___ ). What else must you say before calling the difference even?

Check the missing step

The gap is a − b, since 2a − 2b = 2(a − b). Subtracting integers gives an integer, so this is an allowed witness. The difference is even. Identifying the expression and checking that it is an integer are separate parts of the argument.

Build new witnesses

Exercise 1. Prove directly that the product of two even integers is even. Use separate witnesses and identify the witness for the product.

Hint for exercise 1

Write x = 2a and y = 2b; factor one 2 from their product.

Full solution to exercise 1

Let x = 2a and y = 2b with integers a, b. Then xy = 4ab = 2(2ab). Since 2ab is an integer, it is the required witness. Therefore xy is even.

Exercise 2. Is the converse “if x + y is even, then both x and y are even” true? Explain with a specific pair.

Hint for exercise 2

Try two odd integers.

Full solution to exercise 2

No. Take x = 1 and y = 3. Their sum is 4 = 2·2, even. Neither input is even: their halves are 1/2 and 3/2, not integers. The theorem has not failed; its converse makes a different claim.

A complete little proof

Direct proof starts with arbitrary inputs meeting the hypothesis, unpacks their definitions, and constructs what the conclusion requires. Here the constructed witness is a + b. The result is a theorem for all even integer pairs, while the numerical pair was only a guide. For a claim whose forward direction is awkward, contrapositive proof offers another route.
