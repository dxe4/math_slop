Sets and membership

A set records which objects belong to a collection. In roster notation, braces surround its members: A={1,3,5}. Writing 3∈A says that 3 belongs to A; writing 2∉A says that 2 does not. Repeating a member or changing the listing order leaves the set unchanged.

Worked example. Let B={3,4}. The union A∪B contains every object in either set. The intersection A∩B contains exactly the objects in both sets.

A∪B={1,3,4,5}A∩B={3}

The empty set, written ∅, has no members. A subset C⊆A has all its members in A. The subset may equal the whole set; the symbol does not require a smaller collection.

Try one. With C={1,5}, find C∩B and decide whether C⊆A.

Worked answer

C and B have no common member, so C∩B=∅. Both 1 and 5 belong to A, hence C⊆A. Empty intersection and subset inclusion ask different questions.

Watch the object. The number 3 and the set {3} are different objects. Here 3∈A and {3}⊆A are both true, but they express different relationships.

Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

The question and needed ideas

A standard 52-card deck is shuffled, and one card is drawn. You win if it is red or a face card. Red includes hearts and diamonds; face cards are jacks, queens and kings. Adding 26 red cards to 12 face cards sounds plausible, but some red cards are face cards. A card can satisfy both win conditions.

Background capsule. “Or” is inclusive here: one or both conditions suffice. A∪B is the union, the outcomes in at least one event. A∩B is the intersection, the outcomes in both. If A and B share no outcomes, they are disjoint, and their probabilities add directly. The fractions in this lesson use a uniformly shuffled deck, making each individual card equally likely.

Work through the model

Worked example. Let A be red and B be face. There are 26 red cards and 12 face cards. Six cards are both: J, Q and K in each of hearts and diamonds. The sum 26+12 counts these six twice. Remove one copy of the overlap: 26+12−6=32 cards. The winning chance is 32/52=8/13.

The table totals 52 and makes the 32 winners visible as 20+6+6. This partition is more reliable than a diagram whose overlapping areas have no exact scale. It also gives the complement: 20 losing cards, so 1−20/52=32/52.

Another worked example: two badges. A box contains ten equally likely numbered tickets. Four tickets carry a star, five carry a circle, and two carry both badges. A ticket wins if it has either badge. Counting four plus five gives nine badge appearances, but the two double-badge tickets each appear twice. Subtract those two extra appearances to obtain seven winning tickets, so the chance is 7/10. The disjoint categories are two star-only, two both, three circle-only, and three neither. They total ten. If someone supplies only the two separate badge counts, the winning chance is not yet determined: the overlap is still needed.

The statement and its limits

Exact statement. For any two events A and B in one probability space, the union formula holds. Split A∪B into three disjoint pieces: A without B, A∩B, and B without A. Since probabilities of disjoint pieces add, their total equals P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B). This is a proof from additivity, not a diagram-only argument.

The same reasoning works with unequal outcome chances: replace counts by probability masses and partition the event in the same way. It does not require independence. Indeed, independence concerns whether P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B); it is a different condition from disjointness. In this deck example, A and B happen to be independent because (26/52)(12/52)=6/52, but that fact is accidental to these choices.

Failure to subtract overlap can even produce a number above one. If A is “card is red” and B is “card is not a club,” naive addition gives 1/2+3/4=5/4, which is impossible, while their union is merely the not-a-club event with chance 3/4. The intersection is the red event, and the formula returns 1/2+3/4−1/2=3/4.

P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)

One way to audit the card arithmetic is to count the complement first. A card loses only when it is black and not a face card. Each black suit has ten numbered or ace cards, giving twenty losers and thirty-two winners. This independent route agrees with the three-region partition. When two counting methods disagree, the usual culprit is a shared card or a missing category. The addition rule does not require a deck: a school club could count members who play chess or paint by the same disjoint-region argument, provided each person is counted once in the total.

Try two cases

Exercise 1. Roll one fair die. Find P(even or at least 5).

Hint for exercise 1

List each set and its shared member.

Full solution to exercise 1

Even is {2,4,6}, and at least 5 is {5,6}. The overlap is {6}. Their union is {2,4,5,6}, so its chance is (3+2−1)/6=4/6=2/3. Direct enumeration checks the subtraction.

Exercise 2. A student says, “If two events are disjoint, they are independent.” Test this with events A={1} and B={2} on a fair six-sided die.

Hint for exercise 2

Compare P(A∩B) with P(A)P(B).

Full solution to exercise 2

The events cannot occur together, so P(A∩B)=0. Yet P(A)P(B)=(1/6)(1/6)=1/36. The equality required for independence fails. Positive-probability disjoint events are dependent: learning A rules out B.

What to carry forward

For two events, ask first what “or” includes, then mark the shared outcomes once. The formula does not tell you the overlap automatically; you must calculate or model it. Conditioning asks a different question: after learning one event occurred, which denominator remains?
