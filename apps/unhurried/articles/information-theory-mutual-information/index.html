How useful is a noisy clue?

A hidden card is red or blue, equally likely. A lamp tries to indicate its color but sometimes gives the wrong signal. Seeing the lamp usually helps, yet it does not remove all uncertainty. Mutual information measures how much uncertainty about the hidden card is removed on average by observing the lamp. It is a property of their joint probability law, not of a single favorable prediction or a causal effect of the lamp on the card.

Background capsule. Entropy H(X) is average surprise in bits. Conditional entropy H(X|Y) averages the remaining surprise in X after Y is known, using the probability of each Y outcome as its weight. For positive probabilities, p(x|y)=p(x,y)/p(y). A joint table must sum to one. If a conditioning value has probability zero, its conditional fraction is undefined and its row contributes no weight to conditional entropy.

A lamp that is right three quarters of the time

Worked example. Let X be the fair hidden color and Y the displayed lamp color. Conditional on either hidden color, the lamp displays the correct color with probability 3/4 and the wrong color with probability 1/4. The four joint chances are 3/8 for red-red, 1/8 for red-blue, 1/8 for blue-red, and 3/8 for blue-blue. The lamp itself is fair: its red and blue column sums are each 1/2. Given a red lamp, the hidden card is red with probability (3/8)/(1/2)=3/4; a blue lamp gives the mirror calculation.

Before the lamp, H(X)=1 bit. After the lamp, each conditional distribution is (3/4,1/4), whose entropy is about 0.811 bit. Consequently the reduction is 1−0.811≈0.189 bit per use. That small number does not mean the lamp has only a 19% chance of being correct. Its accuracy is 75%; information and classification accuracy answer different questions.

The amount revealed on average

I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X|Y)

Exact finite identity. The chain rule gives H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X|Y)=H(X)+H(Y|X). Rearranging shows I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)−H(X,Y)=H(Y)−H(Y|X), so the measure is symmetric even though one conditional description may be easier to calculate. Expanding entropy also gives I(X;Y)=Σ p(x,y)log₂[p(x,y)/(p(x)p(y))] over positive joint cells. That is a KL divergence between the joint law and the product of its marginals. Since finite KL divergence is nonnegative, mutual information is nonnegative and equals zero exactly when the variables are independent. The KL lesson proves the needed inequality; this paragraph identifies the boundary of our derivation.

Mutual information can be positive when ordinary numerical correlation is zero. For numerical variables, covariance is Cov(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]; correlation divides that covariance by the product of their positive standard deviations. Let X be uniform on −1,0,1 and set Y=X². Y is determined by X, so I(X;Y)=H(Y)>0. Yet E[X]=0 and E[XY]=E[X³]=0, giving covariance and correlation zero. Both standard deviations are positive here. Correlation measures a particular linear association; mutual information detects this nonlinear dependence. Conversely, a high measured value from a tiny empirical table may be sampling bias rather than strong population dependence. The exact paired-sample investigation derives the two-observation estimator law and shows bias in either direction.

Two kinds of dependence

Exercise 1. A fair bit X is copied perfectly to Y. Compute H(X), H(X|Y), and I(X;Y). Repeat when Y is an independent fair bit.

Hint for exercise 1

Ask whether Y fixes X in each case. If it does not, calculate the remaining fair-bit entropy.

Full solution to exercise 1

In perfect copying, H(X)=1 bit and X is known once Y is observed, so H(X|Y)=0 and I(X;Y)=1 bit. With independent fair bits, seeing Y leaves X fair: H(X|Y)=1 bit and I(X;Y)=0. Both cases have H(X)=H(Y)=1; marginals alone cannot distinguish them.

Exercise 2. X is uniform on −1,0,1 and Y=X². Find the distribution and entropy of Y, then explain why a zero covariance would not imply zero mutual information here.

Hint for exercise 2

Y is zero once and one twice among the three equally likely X values.

Full solution to exercise 2

P(Y=0)=1/3 and P(Y=1)=2/3. Thus H(Y)=−(1/3)log₂(1/3)−(2/3)log₂(2/3)≈0.918 bit. Because Y is determined by X, H(Y|X)=0, so I(X;Y)=H(Y)≈0.918 bit. Meanwhile E[X]=0 and E[XY]=E[X³]=0, so covariance is zero. The dependence is real and nonlinear.

What the clue does and does not prove

Mutual information is average reduction in uncertainty under a specified joint law. In the lamp model it is about 0.189 bit, while the lamp's correct-output probability is 3/4. It does not name a causal direction: the same joint table fits descriptions in which the card causes the lamp or both respond to a shared mechanism. Nor can a small sample table reveal a population information value exactly. The next coding lessons use known source weights; the estimation lesson returns to finite samples.

Check the stopping condition by computing the marginal probabilities before any conditional entropy. If a table fails to normalize, all later information calculations are suspect. The MIT information and entropy text supports conditional information and the entropy identities. The lamp and square examples are original finite illustrations.
