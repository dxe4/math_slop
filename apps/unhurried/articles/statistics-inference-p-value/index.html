How surprising is this spinner result under a fair-spin model?

A supposedly fair spinner lands blue unusually often. The question is what the observed numbers allow us to say, and where the tempting shortcut fails.

Background capsule. A null hypothesis specifies a probability model for the data. A test statistic orders possible outcomes by how extreme they are for a predeclared question. “At least as extreme” must therefore be fixed before seeing the data, including the direction of the tail. Binomial counts arise from a fixed number of independent trials with the same success probability.

Worked example

Suppose ten independent spins of a fair two-color spinner yield eight blue results. Before spinning, the club decides to test only for an excess of blue. Under the null p=1/2, X, the blue count, follows Binomial(10,1/2). The upper-tail p-value is P(X≥8)=P(X=8)+P(X=9)+P(X=10)=(45+10+1)/1024=56/1024≈0.0547. The observed blue fraction is 80%, an effect of 30 percentage points relative to the null value. The p-value and that effect answer different questions.

The binomial coefficients 45, 10 and 1 count sequences with eight, nine and ten blue outcomes. Each of the 1024 length-ten color sequences has probability 1/1024 under independent fair spins. This makes the 56/1024 tail directly checkable without a smooth approximation.

The claim and its conditions

p−value=PH0(X≥8)=561024

The calculation conditions on the fair-spinner model and on the planned sampling and tail rule. It sums every null outcome at least as extreme as eight in the chosen direction. It is not P(the spinner is fair | eight blues); assigning probabilities to hypotheses would require a prior and an alternative model. Nor does 0.0547 mean there is a 5.47% chance of these exact eight blue results: the single-count probability is 45/1024. The tail includes counts 9 and 10. If the club instead had prespecified a two-sided rule using |X−5|≥3, symmetry would double this tail to 112/1024≈0.1094. Choosing whichever direction looks favorable after observing the data does not preserve the original test’s error calculation.

Proof boundary and failure case. The exact 56/1024 follows from ten iid Bernoulli trials under p=1/2 and a prespecified upper-tail statistic. Dependence among spins, a drifting spinner, optional stopping, or choosing a tail after inspection changes the sampling distribution. A small p-value measures incompatibility with this full model; it does not prove a physical mechanism or quantify practical importance.

If the observed count had been seven rather than eight, the same predeclared upper-tail rule would include counts 7 through 10 and yield a larger p-value. The test procedure is a mapping from every possible count to a tail area; interpreting just the observed number without that rule loses the reference distribution.

What if the club looks twice?

Now change the sampling plan before collecting data. Inspect the same accumulating sequence after 8 spins and after 16. At either look, reject fairness if that look's upper binomial p-value is at most .05. Stop on the first rejection; otherwise finish at 16. This rule has two chances to trigger. The final count includes the first eight spins, so these are dependent looks, even though all individual spins are independent.

At the first look, seven or eight blue results trigger: P(X8≥7)=9/256≈.0351563. Six do not, since the tail is 37/256>.05. At the final look, at least twelve blue trigger: P(X16≥12)=2517/65536≈.0384064. Eleven do not, since that tail is 6885/65536>.05. Each separate fixed-size test has null rejection probability below .05. Does that settle the probability that either look rejects?

Predict first. Let A mean the first threshold is crossed and B the final threshold is crossed. Would you add their probabilities? Multiply them? Count the overlap before choosing. The explorer enumerates the exact model law, not a batch of simulated tests.

A conditioning check

Condition on 7 blue among the first 8. This count already triggers early rejection. The remaining 8 spins are independent fair spins. The hypothetical final threshold needs at least 5 more blue, giving conditional probability 93/256 (≈ 0.363281).

The unconditional final probability is 2517/65536 (≈ 0.0384064). The conditioning probe explains dependence between cumulative looks; it does not change the predeclared rule, and an early-stopped record does not require actually observing its unused continuation.

With Bonferroni's .025 per look, the boundaries become 8 and 13; the union is 860/65536=215/16384≈.0131226. The correction controls the whole predeclared procedure; it does not force its exact error to equal .05.

Count on one shared sample space

Under the fair independent-spin null there are 216=65536 equally likely full records. A first-look rejection occupies 9×256=2304 of them: nine qualifying eight-spin prefixes, each with 256 continuations. A final-look rejection occupies 2517. Counting full records after an early rejection is a mathematical device. Those unused continuations need not actually be observed.

For the overlap, a prefix with seven blue has eight possible orders, and needs at least five blue among the remaining eight. There are 93 such continuations. The all-blue prefix needs at least four more blue, giving 163 continuations. Thus A∩B has 8×93+1×163=907 records. Inclusion–exclusion now gives the actual error of the complete rule:

P0(A∪B)=2304+2517−90765536=391465536

This is about .059723, above .05. It is the probability of any rejection under the stipulated fair null, not the probability that fairness is true after a rejection. Neither adding the two probabilities without overlap nor the independent-tests formula describes this rule. For example, conditional on seven blue at the first look, the final threshold has probability 93/256≈.363281; unconditionally its probability is only 2517/65536. The cumulative looks share the early spins.

An exact joint-count formula for any two fixed looks

Write the predeclared sizes as n1<n2. Let i be blue at the first look and j the final blue count. There are C(n1,i) ways to place the early blue and C(n2−n1,j−i) ways to place the additional blue. Here C(n,k) is the binomial coefficient counting choices of k positions out of n. The product, divided by 2n₂, is the joint probability. It is zero when the counts cannot occur. Sum these weights over each of the four threshold patterns: early only, final only, both, neither.

This calculation uses independence of the two disjoint blocks of spins. It does not assume independence of the two cumulative counts. Equivalently, given the first count i, the additional count is Binomial(n2−n1,1/2). This gives the explorer's conditioning probe, including probabilities zero or one when the required additional count is impossible or already assured. The unconditional procedure's error stays fixed while that probe changes.

Predeclare an error budget for both looks

One simple repair is Bonferroni: allow .025 at each of the two predeclared looks. For a fixed sample size, an upper-tail binomial p-value rejects on a tail whose null probability is at most its cutoff; if no count reaches the cutoff, it never rejects. Therefore each rejection event has probability at most .025. The union bound gives P0(any rejection)≤.025+.025=.05, with no independence assumption between the looks.

For the 8/16 plan, the corrected boundaries are eight blue and thirteen blue. The early event has 256 full records; the final event has 697. Their overlap is 93: an all-blue prefix followed by at least five more blue. The union is 256+697−93=860 records, or 215/16384≈.0131226. This is below the .05 bound, not equal to it. Discrete counts make the cutoff conservative. A third look or a rule chosen after inspection would need its own analysis; the stated two-look budget does not cover extra opportunities.

Repeated fixed-size p-values lose a general .05 guarantee when used this way. They need not exceed .05 for every possible schedule: the changed task below gives a counterexample to that stronger claim. The independent-tests lab addresses a different selection rule, with fresh independent samples for different tests. Its product formula remains appropriate for that model.

Optional: a different process with an anytime error bound

Can one predeclare a rule valid at arbitrarily many looks? Here is one mathematical construction for these independent spins. Keep the fair null probability 1/2, and fix an alternative probability 3/4 before seeing the sequence. The likelihood ratio after n spins with Xn blue is Ln=3Xₙ/2n, starting at L0=1. It is the alternative model's likelihood of that ordered record divided by its null likelihood; it is not a posterior probability.

A blue spin multiplies L by 3/2; another color multiplies it by 1/2. Given the entire past under the independent fair null, the next conditional multiplier mean is (1/2)(3/2)+(1/2)(1/2)=1. Values are nonnegative and integrable at every fixed time. Thus L is a nonnegative martingale: its next conditional mean is its present value. This depends on the stated null and the preselected alternative; mechanically dependent or drifting spins need not satisfy it.

Predeclare rejection when L first reaches or exceeds 20. Let that crossing time be τ. At any deterministic horizon H, stop the process at the earlier of τ and H. The finite stopped conditional-mean argument gives mean 1. On a crossing it has value at least 20, and elsewhere it is nonnegative, so 1≥20P0(τ≤H). These crossing events increase as H increases. Taking their probability limit gives P0(ever cross 20)≤1/20=.05. This is the relevant case of Ville's inequality. It passes a limit through increasing events, not an unjustified limit through expectations.

The bound is an inequality, not a claim that the crossing probability equals .05. This likelihood-ratio rule differs from repeatedly comparing ordinary binomial p-values with .05. For example, seven blue in eight spins has one-sided p-value 9/256<.05, but L8=2187/256<20. The rule must be chosen before inspecting the record. Allow about 15 additional minutes for this optional argument.

Check the whole procedure

1. Calculation. With five fair independent spins and a predeclared upper-tail test, find the p-value after observing four blue results.

Hint for exercise 1

Count outcomes with X=4 or X=5 out of 2^5 equally likely sequences.

Full solution to exercise 1

There are C(5,4)=5 sequences with four blues and C(5,5)=1 with five. Thus P(X≥4)=(5+1)/32=6/32=0.1875. That is a null-tail probability, not a probability that fairness is true.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. A club examines the data first, chooses whichever color is overrepresented, and reports that color’s one-sided tail as if planned. What changed?

Hint for exercise 2

Compare the set of outcomes that would have triggered a report before and after allowing color choice.

Full solution to exercise 2

The reporting rule now allows either color to be selected by the observed imbalance. Its rejection set includes both tails, so a one-direction calculation no longer describes the full procedure. For the ten-spin result eight of either color, the symmetric two-tail probability under the fair null is 112/1024, assuming the absolute-deviation rule was specified.

The observed 80% blue is an effect estimate for these ten spins, 30 points above fairness. A small or large null-tail probability does not tell whether that difference would matter for a game. That judgment needs an application and perhaps more precise estimation.

A changed schedule needs a fresh count

3. Transfer task. Predeclare looks at 6 and 12 fair independent spins. At either look reject if the upper binomial p-value is at most .05. Find both boundaries and the exact probability of any rejection. Does it exceed .05? Now use Bonferroni's .025 at each look. Do the boundaries change? Explain why the result does not contradict the 8/16 example or the union bound.

Hint for task 3

For six spins, compare tails at five and six blue. For twelve, compare tails at nine and ten. Work on all 4096 twelve-spin records. The all-blue six-spin prefix needs at least four further blue to reach ten.

Full solution to task 3

The six-spin tails at five and six are 7/64 and 1/64. The twelve-spin tails at nine and ten are 299/4096 and 79/4096. Thus the .05 boundaries are six and ten. The early event has 64 full records and the final event has 79. Their overlap has C(6,4)+C(6,5)+C(6,6)=15+6+1=22. The union has 64+79−22=121 records, giving 121/4096≈.0295410, below .05.

The .025 boundaries are also six and ten: 1/64 and 79/4096 are already below .025, while the preceding tails exceed even .05. Bonferroni changes neither rejection set for this schedule. Discreteness can make two different probability cutoffs select exactly the same attainable counts. The uncorrected two-look rule has no general .05 guarantee, but need not exceed .05 on every schedule. The corrected guarantee is ≤.05, not equality or a promise that each boundary must move.

4. Procedure audit. In the original 8/16 plan, someone reports 1−(1−9/256)(1−2517/65536) for the chance of any rejection. Identify the unproved step. Then explain why dividing .05 equally between two predeclared looks does not need that step.

Hint for task 4

Write the union as a sum minus an intersection. What intersection is hidden inside the product formula?

Full solution to task 4

The product formula substitutes P(A)P(B) for P(A∩B), which requires independence. The final count includes the first eight spins, and the exact overlap is 907/65536; it is not that product. Bonferroni instead uses P(A∪B)≤P(A)+P(B)≤.025+.025. This bound holds regardless of the overlap.

What you can carry forward

Write the null model, test statistic, tail, and sampling plan before interpreting a p-value. Report the observed effect alongside the tail probability. A p-value is conditional on a model and selection rule; it is neither the probability the null is true nor a stand-alone decision about importance.

A valid fixed-size test does not automatically supply a valid repeated-look rule. Count the joint rejection event for the complete plan, or use a predeclared bound that covers its opportunities. A guarantee of at most .05 is not a claim of equality.

A fair-spinner null is an idealized model. If spins are mechanically linked or the wheel changes between trials, the equal-probability sequence count fails. The arithmetic remains correct for the stipulated model but stops answering the physical question.
