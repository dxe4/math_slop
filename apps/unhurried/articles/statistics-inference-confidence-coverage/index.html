What does the 95% belong to?

Many jars have the same hidden color proportion. The question is what the observed numbers allow us to say, and where the tempting shortcut fails.

Background capsule. The unknown parameter p is fixed in a frequentist sampling model; a confidence interval is calculated from random sample data. Coverage asks whether the interval contains p across repeated samples generated in the same way. A normal approximation treats a standardized sample proportion as approximately bell-shaped when sample size and success counts are adequate; it is not an exact finite-sample law.

Worked example

A fictional spinner has an unknown blue probability. In 100 independent spins, 60 are blue, so p-hat=0.60. A simple Wald interval uses p-hat plus or minus 1.96 times the estimated standard error sqrt(0.60×0.40/100)=0.0490. Its endpoints are about 0.504 and 0.696. This calculation says which interval this procedure produced from these data. It does not say the probability that this now-fixed interval contains p is 95% under this frequentist model. If p were exactly 0.5, this particular interval would miss it.

The observed interval just clears 0.5 at its lower endpoint. A different count of blue outcomes could shift both endpoints, including one that covers 0.5. This is the random feature in the repeated procedure; the spinner’s underlying p is held fixed in the coverage calculation.

The claim and its conditions

Pp(p∈C(X))≈0.95

Imagine repeating the 100-spin experiment, calculating a new interval each time, and marking cover or miss against one fixed p. An advertised 95% confidence procedure aims for coverage near 95% over those repetitions, at each p in its claimed range. For the Wald procedure, that number is only approximate and can be poor near p=0 or p=1 or with small n. If exact binomial coverage is crucial, one must choose and verify a different procedure, such as an inversion of binomial tests; the present calculation does not prove uniform 95% coverage. The event “p is inside C(X)” is random before observing X because C(X) depends on X.

Proof boundary and failure case. The approximate symbol matters. The usual 1.96 multiplier describes a normal reference distribution, and estimated standard error adds another approximation. For n=10 with zero blue spins, the Wald interval collapses to [0,0], even though positive p could easily produce zero successes. That failure shows why the rule cannot be advertised as exact or reliable at the boundary. Selection of a convenient interval after looking at results can also alter a predeclared coverage claim.

For p away from 0 and 1 and sufficiently large n, the approximate normal pivot (p-hat−p)/sqrt(p(1−p)/n) is near standard normal under iid Bernoulli sampling. The central normal region has probability about 0.95 when its absolute value is at most 1.96. To obtain the Wald rule, first replace the unknown p in the standard error by p-hat, then invert the resulting inequality. Inverting the original p-dependent pivot without that plug-in instead yields the score/Wilson construction. The plug-in makes the Wald coverage only approximate, especially near boundaries.

Construct an exact procedure by inverting tests (45–60 additional minutes)

The Wald example tells us what a convenient approximate rule returns. We now build a different rule from exact binomial tails and prove its coverage. Keep the sampling plan fixed: take exactly two iid Bernoulli trials. Their success count K has probabilities (1−p)², 2p(1−p), p² for 0,1,2. We use a nominal 75% procedure so that its complete three-interval family has manageable exact endpoints.

Distinguish a candidate parameter u, a hypothetical observed count k, and the fixed true parameter p used when calculating repeated-sampling coverage. For each candidate u, define the inclusive tails Fᵤ(k)=Pᵤ(K≤k) and Gᵤ(k)=Pᵤ(K≥k). With α=1/4, give each tail a rejection budget α/2=1/8. Reject u only when Fᵤ(k)<1/8 or Gᵤ(k)<1/8. Equality is retained.

C(k)={u∈[0,1]:Fu(k)≥18,Gu(k)≥18}

This inversion keeps all real candidates that the test does not reject. It is not a collection restricted to a numerical grid. The instrument's rational controls probe these continuous sets; a parameter between grid points is still included or excluded by the same exact inequalities.

Predict before changing the probes. At true p=1/4, which of the three possible intervals misses? Does exact coverage have to equal 75%? Will changing only the candidate u change coverage of the whole repeated procedure at that true p?

Three different statements. The general test-inversion theorem guarantees coverage at least 3/4. The complete real-parameter analysis of this particular procedure proves coverage strictly above 7/8 everywhere, with unattained infimum 7/8. The 21-point probe grid has an attained minimum 351/400, at p=7/20 and 13/20. A finite grid calculation does not prove the continuum statement.

This activity enumerates exact probabilities under a stipulated fixed-n model. It generates no observed samples. The fixed true p is a coverage probe, not a posterior distribution or an unknown value estimated by the controls.

Derive all three continuous intervals

For k=0, Gᵤ(0)=1 and Fᵤ(0)=(1−u)². Retaining F≥1/8 gives u≤1−√(1/8). For k=1, Fᵤ(1)=1−u² and Gᵤ(1)=1−(1−u)²; retaining both gives 1−√(7/8)≤u≤√(7/8). For k=2, Fᵤ(2)=1 and Gᵤ(2)=u², giving u≥√(1/8). Thus

Why test inversion proves repeated coverage

The argument works for any fixed number n of iid Bernoulli trials and any predeclared 0<α<1, using inclusive binomial tails. Hold the true p fixed. Because Fₚ(k) is nondecreasing in k, the event Fₚ(K)<α/2 is an initial segment K≤j, if it is nonempty. Its probability is Fₚ(j)<α/2. Similarly, Gₚ(k) is nonincreasing in k, so the event Gₚ(K)<α/2 is a terminal segment, with probability below α/2, or it is empty. Their union therefore has probability at most α.

The key is an equality of events: p is excluded from C(K) exactly when the test of that same true p rejects. Hence

Pp(p∉C(K))=Pp(test of p rejects)≤α

So coverage is at least 1−α for every fixed p. We do not bound the chance that any candidate gets rejected; false candidate values are supposed to be removable. There is no extra correction over all the candidate values in this inversion argument. Nor does it assign a probability distribution to the fixed true parameter after observing data. Berkeley's test–confidence-set duality notes state the general event identity.

Exact does not mean equal to the nominal percentage

At p=1/4, only C(2) misses, with probability p²=1/16, so coverage is 15/16. At p=1/2, all three intervals contain p and coverage is 1. Neither equals the nominal 3/4. The generic theorem supplies a guarantee; we can analyze the full behavior of this small procedure more sharply.

Every coverage value is strictly above 7/8. For example, on [0,a), (1−p)²>(1−a)²=7/8, while on [a,b), 1−p²>1−b²=7/8; the right-hand pieces are symmetric. Coverage is 1 on [b,c] and at p=0,1. Its infimum, or greatest lower bound, is 7/8, approached at a from the left, b from the left, c from the right and d from the right. No p attains 7/8, so there is no attained global minimum. These conclusions use the piecewise formula and exact boundaries, not rounded endpoints.

By contrast, the finite p=j/20 grid has an attained minimum 351/400 at j=7 and 13. That is an exact result about twenty-one probes, not the infimum across every real p. Keep the generic nominal guarantee ≥3/4, the sharper fixture infimum 7/8, and the finite-grid minimum 351/400 separate.

NIST's exact-binomial documentation gives the binomial-tail equations for interval endpoints. The closed intervals, equality convention, complete real-p coverage and changed calculations here are derived locally. No normal approximation or simulated coverage count establishes these guarantees.

Try two checks

1. Calculation. For 400 independent trials with 200 successes, calculate the Wald 95% interval to three decimals.

Hint for exercise 1

Use p-hat=0.5, estimated SE=sqrt(0.25/400)=0.025.

Full solution to exercise 1

The margin is 1.96×0.025=0.049. Endpoints are 0.5−0.049=0.451 and 0.5+0.049=0.549. These numbers are approximate interval endpoints under iid Bernoulli sampling; 95% describes a procedure’s approximate repeated coverage.

2. Interpretation or counterexample. A friend says: “Because the interval is 95%, there is a 95% probability the unknown p lies between my observed endpoints.” Explain the interpretation issue and give the relevant repeated experiment.

Hint for exercise 2

Decide what remains random after the data and endpoints are fixed.

Full solution to exercise 2

In the stated frequentist model, p is fixed and the observed endpoints are fixed; the containment statement is either true or false, though unknown. The 95% refers to a procedure across fresh iid samples: calculate a new interval each time and count how often it includes the same p. A probability for p after observing data would require an explicit probability model for p and a different analysis.

Coverage should be checked across a grid of possible p values because a procedure can work near p=0.5 yet fail near zero. Counting cover/miss marks in a finite simulation illustrates the procedure; a finite run cannot prove exact 95% coverage.

Transfer tasks for the exact-inversion investigation

3. Zero successes under a changed fixed plan. Take exactly ten iid Bernoulli trials and observe zero successes. Use the predeclared 95% equal-tailed construction: retain u when both inclusive binomial tails are at least 1/40. Derive the full interval, state whether its endpoints are included, and explain how it differs from the collapsed zero-count Wald interval.

Hint for exercise 3

Fᵤ(0)=(1−u)¹⁰ and Gᵤ(0)=1. Solve (1−u)¹⁰≥1/40 for 0≤u≤1. Do not replace the exact root by a rounded value when deciding inclusion.

Full solution to exercise 3

The upper-count tail is always 1 and imposes no restriction. The other inequality gives u≤1−(1/40)^(1/10), so C(0)=[0, 1−(1/40)^(1/10)]≈[0,0.308497108]. Both endpoints are included: u=0 passes, and at the upper endpoint the lower-count tail equals 1/40, which is retained. Positive p can produce zero successes. This interval accounts for that possibility, whereas the Wald plug-in standard error at zero successes vanishes and gives [0,0]. The percentage describes repeated use of the fixed procedure, not a posterior probability for the observed interval.

4. Change the requested object to a one-sided bound. Keep the ten-trial plan and zero observed successes, but predeclare a one-sided 95% upper confidence bound. Use the single-tail rule C⁺(k)={u:Fᵤ(k)≥1/20}. Derive the zero-count bound and compare it with exercise 3. Then test candidate u=3/10 under both procedures using its exact zero-count probability. Does a possible miss contradict the one-sided guarantee?

Hint for exercise 4

The full 5% rejection budget belongs to the one lower-count tail. Use 1/20 rather than 1/40. Compare (7/10)¹⁰ with both cutoffs by integer cross-products.

Full solution to exercise 4

The retained values satisfy (1−u)¹⁰≥1/20, giving C⁺(0)=[0, 1−(1/20)^(1/10)]≈[0,0.258865551]. Equality is retained, so the upper endpoint is included. The upper bound is smaller than the equal-tailed one because the same 5% budget is spent on one tail rather than split between two. The general single-tail test-inversion proof gives coverage at least 95% for this predeclared bound across fresh fixed-size samples.

For u=3/10, the zero-count probability is (7/10)¹⁰=282475249/10¹⁰. It is strictly greater than 1/40 because 40×282475249>10¹⁰, and strictly less than 1/20 because 20×282475249<10¹⁰. Thus zero successes retains 3/10 in the two-sided interval and excludes it in the one-sided bound. This possible observed miss does not contradict a repeated-sampling guarantee: the event has probability about 0.02825 under p=3/10. Neither the coverage guarantee nor the selected interval assigns a probability distribution to the fixed parameter.

What you can carry forward

Attach the percentage to the method, sampling model, and parameter range over which coverage was checked. An observed interval reports plausible values by a chosen procedure, with approximation limits. When n is small or p near a boundary, inspect an interval method with demonstrable coverage rather than trusting a convenient symmetric formula.

For the 60-of-100 result, report the endpoints and method together: “Wald interval under an iid Bernoulli model.” If a different construction is used, its endpoints and actual coverage behavior may differ even on the same data.
