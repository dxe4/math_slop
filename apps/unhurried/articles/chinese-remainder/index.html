Can two clocks specify one moment?

A machine reports that an event happened at position 2 in a three-step cycle and position 4 in a five-step cycle. One answer is 14: it leaves remainder 2 on division by 3 and remainder 4 on division by 5. Are there other answers, and what makes the pair of readings reliable? This lesson handles two positive coprime moduli m and n, meaning gcd(m,n)=1.

Recall locally that x ≡ a (mod m) says m divides x−a. An inverse of n modulo m is a number u with nu ≡ 1 (mod m); one exists exactly when gcd(n,m)=1, by Bézout's identity. We need just that inverse fact and ordinary divisibility. The theorem gives a single residue class modulo mn, not a unique integer in all of Z: adding mn changes neither reading.

Find the meeting point without scanning forever

Worked example. For x ≡ 2 (mod 3) and x ≡ 4 (mod 5), write x=4+5t. The first condition becomes 4+5t ≡ 2 (mod 3), or 1+2t ≡ 2. Thus 2t ≡ 1 (mod 3). Since 2·2 ≡ 1 (mod 3), multiply by 2 to find t ≡ 2 (mod 3). Choose t=2: x=14. Choosing t=2+3k yields x=14+15k, so all displayed solutions have the same residue modulo 15.

Construction and uniqueness

Two-modulus Chinese remainder theorem. For any integers a,b and positive coprime m,n, there is exactly one residue class modulo mn satisfying x ≡ a (mod m) and x ≡ b (mod n). Write x=b+nt. The remaining condition is nt ≡ a−b (mod m). Since gcd(n,m)=1, choose u with nu ≡ 1 (mod m). Taking t ≡ u(a−b) (mod m) constructs a solution. This proves existence, and it is constructive once the inverse is known.

x≡b+nu(a−b)(modmn)

For uniqueness, let x and y satisfy both readings. Then m divides x−y and n divides x−y. Because gcd(m,n)=1, their product mn divides x−y. To justify that last step without hiding it, write x−y=mk. Since n divides mk and n is coprime to m, Bézout gives integers r,s with rm+sn=1. Multiply by k: k=rmk+snk. Both terms on the right are divisible by n, so n divides k. Hence x−y=mnℓ. Thus x and y are the same residue modulo mn. This proves exactly the theorem claimed.

Failure case. Coprimality cannot be omitted. Conditions x ≡ 0 (mod 4) and x ≡ 1 (mod 6) conflict: every multiple of 4 is even, but every number congruent to 1 modulo 6 is odd. Conditions x ≡ 0 (mod 4) and x ≡ 2 (mod 6) do have solutions, such as 8, yet uniqueness is modulo 12, the least common multiple, rather than modulo 24. The general noncoprime theorem needs a compatibility test; this lesson proves only the coprime case.

Worked example: a negative reading on one clock

Find the least nonnegative integer satisfying x ≡ −1 (mod 4) and x ≡ 1 (mod 5). Write x = 1 + 5t. Modulo 4, the first condition becomes 1 + t ≡ −1, hence t ≡ 2. Taking t = 2 gives x = 11. Indeed 11 − (−1) = 12 is divisible by 4, and 11 − 1 = 10 is divisible by 5.

The moduli are coprime, so uniqueness is modulo 20. All solutions are 11 + 20k for integer k; the representative 11 is the only one between 0 and 19. Negative readings are ordinary residue labels. Replacing −1 by 3 modulo 4 describes exactly the same condition, without changing the solution class.

Solve and test a boundary

1. Solve x ≡ 3 (mod 4), x ≡ 2 (mod 7), giving the least nonnegative answer and the full solution class.

Hint for question 1

Set x=2+7t and solve 7t ≡ 1 (mod 4).

Full solution to question 1

Set x=2+7t. Modulo 4 this is 2+3t ≡ 3, so 3t ≡ 1. Since 3·3 ≡ 1 (mod 4), t ≡ 3 (mod 4). Choosing t=3 gives x=23. Every solution is 23+28k because changing t by 4 changes x by 28. Checks: 23=4·5+3 and 23=7·3+2.

2. Decide whether x ≡ 1 (mod 6), x ≡ 3 (mod 9) is solvable. Explain without trying a large list of numbers.

Hint for question 2

Both moduli are divisible by 3. Compare the requested residues modulo 3.

Full solution to question 2

The first condition forces x ≡ 1 (mod 3), because 6 is divisible by 3. The second forces x ≡ 0 (mod 3), because both 9 and 3 are divisible by 3. A number cannot have two different remainders modulo 3, so no solution exists. This is a counterexample to using the coprime formula without its hypothesis.

One class from two compatible clocks

For coprime m and n, one modular inverse builds a simultaneous solution and divisibility proves it unique modulo mn. The result is exact even when a or b is negative. A finite timeline can check a specific answer, but cannot replace the construction and uniqueness argument. The noncoprime situation needs its own compatibility condition and is outside this theorem.
