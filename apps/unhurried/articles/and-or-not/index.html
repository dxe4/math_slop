A dinner invitation with three possible replies

A host writes, “Come if you can bring tea or biscuits.” A guest bringing both seems welcome, yet everyday “or” sometimes means “choose one.” Logic cannot settle the host’s intention; it can make the two readings precise. We will use the inclusive or: at least one of the two statements is true, possibly both. If the host really meant exactly one, that needs a different rule.

A proposition is a sentence with a definite truth value in a specified situation. Let P mean “the guest brings tea” and Q mean “the guest brings biscuits.” Their truth values may change between situations, but within one row of a truth table each is either true (T) or false (F). The connectives and, or, and not make new propositions from them. A question or an unfilled instruction is not yet a proposition.

Our small goal is to evaluate these three connectives in all possible cases. The four rows below exhaust the possibilities for two truth-valued inputs. This is a local prerequisite capsule: you need only distinguish a statement from its truth value and read T and F. No earlier lesson is required.

Make every case visible

Worked example. First suppose the guest brings tea but no biscuits. P is true and Q false. “Tea and biscuits” is false because one requirement is missing; “tea or biscuits” is true because tea is present; “not tea” is false. Repeat this reasoning row by row.

The first row is the useful surprise: bringing both satisfies inclusive or. The last row shows why “not P” alone does not imply Q. If the guest brings neither, tea is absent but biscuits are absent too. A truth table is a ledger of stipulated meanings, not a survey of how people usually speak.

One equivalence, proved by cases

Exact statement. For any two propositions P and Q, the negation of their conjunction has the same truth value as the disjunction of their negations: ¬(P ∧ Q) ≡ (¬P ∨ ¬Q). The symbol ≡ here means equality of truth value under every assignment, not that the two printed strings are identical.

¬(P∧Q)≡(¬P∨¬Q)

Complete proof. If both P and Q are true, P ∧ Q is true, so its negation is false; neither ¬P nor ¬Q is true, so the right side is false. In each of the other three rows, at least one input is false. Hence P ∧ Q is false and its negation true; the false input supplies a true negation on the right. Both sides agree in all four possible rows. This proof relies on the stated two-valued propositional semantics. It makes no claim about vague predicates such as “tall” before a truth condition is fixed.

The nearby false rule ¬(P ∨ Q) ≡ (¬P ∨ ¬Q) fails when P is true and Q false. The left side is false, since at least one item is present; the right side is true, since biscuits are absent. To deny “tea or biscuits,” the guest must bring neither. The correct right side uses and: ¬P ∧ ¬Q.

Two valuations to solve

1. Let P be false and Q true. Evaluate (P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬P, showing each intermediate truth value.

Hint for question 1

Work from the parentheses and the negation before applying and.

Full solution to question 1

P ∨ Q is T because Q is T. ¬P is T because P is F. Therefore T ∧ T is T. In the dinner story, biscuits without tea satisfy “at least one” and “no tea.”

2. A colleague claims ¬(P ∧ Q) means “neither P nor Q.” Give a row that disproves this and write the correct reading.

Hint for question 2

Try one true input and one false input.

Full solution to question 2

Take P=T and Q=F. Then P ∧ Q=F, so ¬(P ∧ Q)=T. But “neither P nor Q” is false because P is true. The negated conjunction means “at least one of P and Q is false”; neither means ¬P ∧ ¬Q.

For an extra check, build the four rows for “exactly one”: (P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬(P ∧ Q). It is true only in the middle two rows. This formula records a specific rule the host could have chosen. It is not interchangeable with inclusive or whenever both inputs are possible.

Keep the words tied to a table

Conjunction needs both inputs true; inclusive disjunction needs one or both; negation reverses a truth value. A four-row table can prove an equivalence involving two propositions because it covers every assignment to them. It can also refute an alleged equivalence with a single differing row. The table says nothing about whether a real invitation uses inclusive or; that is a question of what the speaker intended.

A satisfying stopping point is the De Morgan equivalence and one counter-row. When you meet an if–then sentence, the same table method will expose a different surprise: an implication is false in only one row.
