Rates and derivatives

An average rate compares output change with input change across an interval. An instantaneous derivative is the limit of those average rates as a nonzero input step tends to zero, when the finite limit exists. The derivative may be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example. For f(x)=x², compare the inputs a and a+h, with h nonzero. Expanding the square and cancelling gives the average rate 2a+h.

(a+h)2−a2h=2a+h

As h tends to zero from either direction, this expression tends to 2a. Therefore f′(a)=2a. At a=3, the instantaneous rate is 6. Using a finite step h=0.1 gives an average rate of 6.1, which is a different quantity.

Try one. What is the derivative at a=−2? What does its sign mean?

Worked answer

The same argument gives f′(−2)=−4. Near −2, a small positive input change decreases x². The derivative is the signed local rate, so the negative answer is appropriate.

Watch the limit. Substituting h=0 into the original quotient would divide by zero. Simplify for nonzero h first, then take the limit. A function can also have corners where the two directions give incompatible rates.

Question and background

A straight road does not turn; a small roundabout turns faster per metre than a large one. Signed curvature describes this local turning of an oriented plane curve. It keeps track of left versus right turns, which an ordinary radius alone cannot record. Its unit is inverse length, so changing metres to centimetres changes the numerical value but not the geometric bend.

Local capsule. For vectors v=(v₁,v₂) and w=(w₁,w₂), det(v,w)=v₁w₂−v₂w₁. A C² curve γ(t) has velocity γ′ and acceleration γ″. It is regular where ||γ′||>0. Its unit tangent is T=γ′/||γ′||, and arc length s grows at rate ds/dt=||γ′||. Rotate T left through 90 degrees to get N=(-T₂,T₁).

Curvature must divide turning by distance, not by clock time. Otherwise a walker who speeds up would seem to make the path itself more curved. That is why the formula below has a third power of speed in its denominator.

A worked example

Worked example. Take the counterclockwise circle γ(t)=(Rcos t,Rsin t), R>0. Its velocity is (-Rsin t,Rcos t) and acceleration is (-Rcos t,-Rsin t). The determinant det(γ′,γ″)=R², while ||γ′||³=R³. Thus κ=1/R. For a clockwise traversal, γ(t)=(Rcos t,-Rsin t), the determinant is −R² and κ=−1/R. The trace is the same circle; orientation changes the sign.

As a contrasting example, γ(t)=(t,0) has γ″=(0,0), hence κ=0. A circle with twice the radius has half the magnitude of curvature. The numerical values match the intuitive scale: a gentle large circle turns less over each unit of distance.

For the parabola γ(t)=(t,t²), velocity is (1,2t), acceleration is (0,2), and determinant is 2. Its signed curvature is 2/(1+4t²)^{3/2}. It is greatest at the vertex and tends toward zero as |t| grows. A picture can suggest that trend; the formula gives the exact statement.

Compare two points on an ellipse

Take γ(t)=(2cos t,sin t). The derivatives are γ′=(−2sin t,cos t) and γ″=(−2cos t,−sin t). Their determinant is 2sin²t+2cos²t=2. The squared speed is 4sin²t+cos²t, always positive, so the curvature formula applies at every parameter value. It gives κ(t)=2/(4sin²t+cos²t)3/2.

At t=0 the point is (2,0), speed is one, and κ=2. At t=π/2 the point is (0,1), speed is two, and κ=2/8=1/4. The ellipse therefore turns much more sharply at its rightmost point than at its top, even though both locations belong to the same smooth closed curve. Unlike a circle, it has no single constant curvature or radius describing every point.

The determinant is the same at the two points; the speed correction creates the different answers. Comparing only accelerations or only the numerators would miss this distinction. Reversing this traversal would negate both signed curvatures while preserving their magnitudes.

The statement and its boundary

Exact statement. At any point where a plane curve γ is C² and regular, its signed curvature for the left normal N is:

κ=det(γ′,γ″)‖γ′‖3

Here is the derivation. Write v=||γ′||. Then T=γ′/v. Differentiating gives dT/dt=γ″/v−γ′v′/v². Taking determinant with T kills the second term because det(γ′,γ′)=0. Therefore det(T,dT/dt)=det(γ′,γ″)/v². Since dT/ds=(dT/dt)/v, we obtain det(T,dT/ds)=det(γ′,γ″)/v³. The derivative of a unit vector is perpendicular to it, so dT/ds=κN and det(T,N)=1. This proves the displayed formula under the stated conditions.

An increasing regular reparameterization preserves κ: the numerator and denominator both gain the third power of the positive clock derivative. A decreasing one reverses its sign. If speed is zero, the displayed quotient is undefined; simplification at nearby points cannot silently assign a curvature at the stalled parameter.

Try it yourself

Exercise 1. a scaled parabola. Find κ at t=0 and t=1 for γ(t)=(t,3t²).

Hint for exercise 1

Differentiate twice and compute one determinant.

Full solution to exercise 1

γ′=(1,6t), γ″=(0,6), so det(γ′,γ″)=6 and ||γ′||³=(1+36t²)^{3/2}. Thus κ(t)=6/(1+36t²)^{3/2}. At t=0 it is 6; at t=1 it is 6/37^{3/2}. The turn is sharper near the vertex.

Exercise 2. a stalled parameter. Consider γ(t)=(t²,t⁴). Can the formula be applied at t=0, even though the trace lies on y=x² for x≥0?

Hint for exercise 2

Inspect γ′(0), before substituting into the curvature quotient.

Full solution to exercise 2

γ′=(2t,4t³), hence γ′(0)=(0,0). The regularity hypothesis fails and the quotient has zero denominator, so κ is undefined for this parameter at zero. On the regular branch x>0, the graph parameter (x,x²) has curvature 2/(1+4x²)^{3/2}, tending to 2 as x→0⁺. That one-sided limit describes the branch's bend; it does not make the original quotient defined at t=0.

What to carry forward

Signed curvature is turning per unit distance, with a sign tied to orientation. For a regular C² plane curve it can be computed without first solving for arc length. At a corner there is no single tangent; at a stopped parameter the quotient is undefined. Those are genuine boundaries of this local formula.

A moving tangent-normal frame packages the same turning into two differential equations. The frame makes the sign convention explicit and gives another way to reconstruct local geometric information.
