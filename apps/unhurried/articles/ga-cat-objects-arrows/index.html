Functions and composition

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the set of allowed inputs; its codomain is the declared set containing the outputs. Different inputs may share an output. For f(x)=x² on the real numbers, both 2 and −2 produce 4.

Worked example. Let f(x)=2x+1 and g(x)=x². The expression f(g(3)) first applies g to 3, producing 9, and then applies f to 9, producing 19. Composition writes this as (f∘g)(3).

f(g(3))=f(9)=19

For a composition to be defined, the output of the inside function must be an allowed input for the outside function. Here both functions accept every real number, so that condition holds.

Try one. Compute g(f(3)). Does changing the order preserve the result?

Worked answer

First f(3)=7, then g(7)=49. This differs from f(g(3))=19. Composition generally depends on its order; the circle symbol names the operation, rather than promising that the two orders agree.

Watch the notation. In f(3), the parentheses name the input to f. They do not mean f multiplied by 3. Read the expression from the inside out.

When do routes form a category?

Consider four places A, B, C, D and one-way roads a:A→B, b:B→C, c:A→C and d:C→D. A route is a finite sequence of roads whose ends match. The empty route at A stays at A; there is one at every place. We can follow a route by another compatible route. This modest system already has the structure of a category.

Background capsule. A directed graph has vertices and directed edges. A path records edges in order, so the route using a then b differs from the single road c even though both start at A and finish at C. Writing q∘p means first travel p, then q. Two arrows compose only when the destination of p equals the source of q. A category consists of objects, arrows with specified sources and targets, identity arrows, and associative composition. Here the objects are places, but categories can instead have sets, groups or other mathematical objects.

List actual arrows

Worked example. The graph has four edges, but its path category has additional arrows ab:A→C, bd:B→D, cd:A→D and abd:A→D, where letters are written in travel order. In conventional composition notation, ab means b∘a. The direct edge c and two-edge path ab are parallel arrows, not equal arrows. Both cd and abd go from A to D, yet they record different trips. Empty paths 1A, 1B, 1C, 1D are arrows too.

This finite acyclic graph has only finitely many paths. In a graph with a directed cycle, the same construction still works but may have infinitely many paths: going around the cycle different numbers of times yields different arrows. Finite objects therefore do not automatically mean finite arrows.

Why path concatenation obeys the laws

Exact statement and full proof. For any directed graph, vertices as objects and finite directed paths as arrows form a category. Source and target of a path are its first and last vertices. Composition is concatenation of compatible edge lists. The identity at a vertex is the empty list based there. For paths p:A→B, q:B→C and r:C→D, concatenation gives

r∘(q∘p)=(r∘q)∘p

Indeed, both sides are the same ordered edge list: all edges of p, then q, then r. Neither bracketing changes an edge or its position. The source is A and target D on both sides. Concatenating an empty list before or after a path leaves that path unchanged, so 1B∘p=p and p∘1A=p. The empty list must carry its base vertex: otherwise the empty path at A could be mistaken for the one at B, destroying the source and target information. These checks prove all category axioms, including identities and associativity, for the entire path construction.

The category keeps routes distinct unless an explicit relation identifies them. In particular, our graph does not say b∘a=c. Drawing the two routes with the same endpoints does not make the corresponding diagram commute. One can later impose an equality between paths, but then one is constructing a different category and must check the relation behaves under composition.

Test the construction

1. Calculation. In the graph above, list every arrow A→D and calculate d∘(b∘a) and (d∘b)∘a.

Hint for question 1

From A, either take c to C or a to B; continue until D.

Full solution for question 1

The A→D arrows are the edge lists cd and abd. The route through B has d∘(b∘a)=abd. Grouping the last two roads first gives (d∘b)∘a=abd too. The route cd is a distinct arrow, even though its endpoints agree.

2. Explanation. Someone calls a:A→B followed by c:A→C a composite c∘a. Is that legal? If not, give one legal composite involving c and an identity.

Hint for question 2

Compare a's target with c's source.

Full solution for question 2

No. After a one is at B, but c starts at A, so c∘a is undefined. The composite c∘1A is legal and equals c; also 1C∘c=c. A category does not demand that every pair of arrows compose, only pairs with matching endpoints.

What the language buys you

A category records which arrows connect which objects, and what happens when compatible arrows are composed. In a path category, arrows are literal routes and associativity is just the fact that parentheses do not alter an edge list. This is a complete example, rather than an analogy for categories. A graph alone supplies no equality between distinct paths with common endpoints; to ask whether routes agree after processing, continue to commuting diagrams.
