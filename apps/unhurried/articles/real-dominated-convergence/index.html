When may a limit move inside an integral?

Pointwise convergence says that at each fixed input, function values eventually settle. It does not prevent a thin spike from carrying the same area forever. Dominated convergence adds a single integrable bound to rule out that escape. We will test its hypotheses, prove a useful finite-measure bounded special case using a named lemma, and clearly separate that from the full theorem.

Background capsule. “Almost everywhere” means except on a set of measure zero. An integrable function g belongs to L¹ when ∫|g| dμ<∞. A measurable f is one whose threshold sets are measurable. We assume a measure space and the Lebesgue integral from simple-function integration. The full Fatou lemma and its proof are not supplied here; we will not claim a proof of the full dominated-convergence theorem.

A spike that keeps its area

Worked example. On [0,1], define fₙ(x)=n when 0<x<1/n, and zero elsewhere. For every fixed x>0, eventually n>1/x and then x lies outside the spike, so fₙ(x)=0. At x=0 all values are zero too. Thus fₙ→0 pointwise everywhere. Its integral, however, is rectangle height n times width 1/n, exactly one for every n. The limiting function has integral zero. A plot with fixed pixel spacing may miss the narrow interval entirely, so the exact calculation matters more than the displayed shape.

Could there be an integrable g≥|fₙ| for every n almost everywhere? Because there are countably many n, combine their exceptional null sets into one null set. Outside it, on each dyadic band (2−(k+1),2−k] with k≥1, the index n=2k−1 has 1/n>2−k, so g≥2k−1 throughout that band. Its integral there is at least (2k−1)2−(k+1)≥1/4. Summing over disjoint bands gives an infinite integral. Thus no common L¹ dominator exists. This direct argument avoids using the theorem we are about to state to verify its own hypothesis.

The theorem and one bounded case

Dominated convergence theorem. If measurable fₙ converge almost everywhere to a measurable f and there is a measurable g∈L¹ with |fₙ|≤g almost everywhere for every n, then f∈L¹ and

limn→∞∫fndμ=∫fdμ

The notation means limₙ ∫fₙ dμ = ∫f dμ; the limit is of the numerical integrals. In our spike example fₙ and f are measurable and converge pointwise, but the required integrable g is absent. The theorem is a sufficient condition, not a necessary condition for every individual sequence.

Bounded finite-measure special case. Suppose μ(X)<∞, all fₙ and f are measurable, |fₙ|≤M for one finite constant M, and fₙ→f almost everywhere. Then |f|≤M almost everywhere. Fix η>0 and let Eₙ={x:|fₙ(x)−f(x)|>η}. Define BN=∪n≥NEₙ. The sets BN decrease, and their intersection is null by almost-everywhere convergence. Continuity from above of finite measures gives μ(BN)→0. For n≥N, split the integral of |fₙ−f| over BN and its complement. On the complement the difference is at most η; on BN it is at most 2M almost everywhere. Hence ∫|fₙ−f|≤ημ(X)+2Mμ(BN). First choose η small, then N large: the right side tends to zero. Therefore |∫fₙ−∫f|≤∫|fₙ−f|→0.

This argument uses continuity from above, a standard measure lemma: if A₁⊇A₂⊇… and μ(A₁)<∞, then μ(∩Aₙ)=lim μ(Aₙ). It follows from countable additivity by partitioning A₁ into disjoint losses, but that foundational proof is outside this short gateway. We have proved the special case conditional on that lemma, not the general theorem. For arbitrary integrable g, the usual proof needs Fatou’s lemma or equivalent machinery to control nonconstant tails.

The finite-measure condition in the special case matters. On ℝ, hₙ=1[n,n+1] tends pointwise to zero and is bounded by 1, but ∫hₙ=1. Here μ(ℝ)=∞ and constant 1 is not integrable. This is another honest failure case, distinct from the shrinking spike.

Check every hypothesis

1. Classify. On [0,1], let uₙ(x)=xⁿ. Does dominated convergence justify passing the limit through the integral? Compute both sides.

Hint

Use the constant bound 1 and remember the exceptional endpoint x=1.

Worked solution

Each uₙ is measurable, 0≤uₙ≤1, and g=1 is integrable on [0,1] with integral one. For x<1, xⁿ→0; at x=1 the limit is one. The limit is zero almost everywhere because {1} is null. Dominated convergence gives ∫uₙ→0, matching the direct integral ∫₀¹xⁿdx=1/(n+1)→0. Its pointwise limit has integral zero.

2. Find the missing assumption. On ℝ, let vₙ=1[n,n+1]. Which dominated-convergence condition fails, and why does |vₙ|≤1 not fix it?

Hint

Ask whether the constant function one belongs to L¹(ℝ).

Worked solution

The functions are measurable and converge pointwise to zero, but 1 has infinite integral over ℝ, so it is not a valid L¹ dominator. In fact no integrable common dominator can exist: if g≥vₙ almost everywhere for each n, countable union of null exceptions aside, g≥1 on every [n,n+1], n≥1. Thus ∫g≥Σn≥11=∞. Each ∫vₙ=1, so the conclusion would fail.

A bound must carry finite total mass

Pointwise settling is not enough for integral convergence. A shared integrable envelope rules out mass hiding in shrinking or escaping regions. We proved a bounded finite-measure case with a stated measure lemma and applied the full theorem as a named result; the full proof and measure construction belong to a longer course.
