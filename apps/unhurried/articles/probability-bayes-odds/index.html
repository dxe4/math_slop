Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

A red marble is a clue. Which bag was used?

Imagine a craft table with two bags. Bag A contains mostly red marbles; bag B contains mostly blue ones. A friend chooses one bag out of sight, draws one marble, and shows you red. You want to know the chance that the hidden bag was A. The tempting answer is “the fraction of red marbles in A.” That answers a different question: how likely red would be if A had already been chosen.

Background capsule. A probability is a number from 0 to 1. The notation P(A) means the chance of event A before a new clue. P(R | A), read “probability of red given A,” means that we consider only draws made from A. A conditional probability changes the denominator. Multiplying P(A) by P(R | A) gives the chance of both A and red. We need only fractions and this rule.

Suppose A is chosen 30% of the time and B 70%. Within A, 80% of marbles are red; within B, 20% are red. The bag frequencies are stipulated parts of this toy experiment, not guesses about an unknown real-world process. After seeing red, should A become more likely than B?

Count a hundred possible plays

Worked example. Picture 100 repetitions of the same rule. We expect 30 choices of A and 70 of B. Of the 30 A plays, 80%—or 24—show red. Of the 70 B plays, 20%—or 14—show red. These are natural frequencies: small whole-number groups that make the changing denominator visible. A hundred gives whole counts here, but the argument works for any probabilities.

Once red is shown, all 62 non-red plays are irrelevant to this question. Among the 38 red plays, 24 came from A. Thus P(A | R) = 24/38 = 12/19, about 63.2%. The clue has raised A from 30% to about 63%, because red is four times as likely under A as under B. Yet red does not make A certain: 14 of the red plays still came from B.

The optional lab changes A’s prior from 1% to 99% and each red chance from 0% to 100%. At 30%, 80%, and 20%, its proportions match the table; its 10,000-case counts are 100 times as large. The table suffices.

Why the fraction works

Let R mean “the shown marble is red.” The bags are mutually exclusive and exhaustive: exactly one of A and B was chosen. The red outcomes therefore split into two groups, red from A and red from B. This gives an exact identity under the stated probability model:

P(A|R)=P(R|A)P(A)P(R|A)P(A)+P(R|B)P(B)

In our example the numerator is 0.8 × 0.3 = 0.24 and the denominator is 0.24 + 0.2 × 0.7 = 0.38. Dividing yields 0.24/0.38 = 12/19. The numerator represents red and A; the denominator represents every route to red. This follows from P(A | R) = P(A and R)/P(R).

Hypothesis and boundary. The formula needs P(R) > 0. If both bags have a 0% chance of red, red cannot occur under this model and P(A | R) is undefined: it is a zero denominator, not 0% or 50%. If only A has 0% red and B can show red, seeing red rules out A, provided B had a positive prior chance. The lab should say “undefined under these settings” for an impossible red observation.

These are expected proportions. Finite simulated draws fluctuate; the algebraic identity remains exact under its assumptions.

Try the update yourself

1. Calculation. A is chosen on 20 of 100 plays and B on 80. Red occurs on half of A plays and one quarter of B plays. After red, what fraction of plays came from A?

Hint for question 1

Count red plays separately for A and B before forming the new denominator.

Full solution to question 1

A contributes 20 × 1/2 = 10 red plays. B contributes 80 × 1/4 = 20. Of 30 red plays, 10 came from A, so P(A | R) = 10/30 = 1/3. The answer is greater than the 1/5 prior but less than one half because B was chosen much more often.

2. Explanation and edge case. Both bags contain no red marbles. A friend nevertheless reports a red draw. What does the formula return? Is it legitimate to replace the answer with 50%?

Hint for question 2

Find the chance of red by adding the red contributions of both bags.

Full solution to question 2

Both contributions are zero, so P(R) = 0 and the formula has denominator zero. P(A | R) is undefined under the model. Assigning 50% would silently introduce a new rule, such as an unreliable report or another bag. To analyse that new situation, specify it first.

Stop with the new denominator

Bayes’ rule asks, “Of all ways this clue could appear, what share came from the hypothesis of interest?” For our bags, the answer is 24 out of 38 red plays. The prior bag choice matters as much as the color composition: even a strong clue can have a modest effect when its favored bag was rare to begin with. The word odds is another convenient form—posterior odds equal prior odds multiplied by a likelihood ratio—but the count table already contains the full reasoning.

For a next idea, change the prior while keeping the bags fixed and ask when a red clue fails to make A more likely than B. For reference, Penn State’s open lessons on conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem give the general definitions. This original bag example remains a finite, stipulated model; it does not establish how to choose probabilities in a real investigation.
