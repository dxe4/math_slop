Fractions and percentages

A fraction a/b divides a by b; the denominator b must be nonzero. For positive fractions representing parts of a whole, the denominator tells you the size of each part and the numerator counts those parts. Changing both by the same nonzero factor keeps the value: 6/8 and 3/4 describe the same amount.

Worked example. To add 1/3 and 1/4, first describe both using twelfths. One third is four twelfths, and one quarter is three twelfths, giving seven twelfths altogether.

13+14=412+312=712

A percentage means a fraction out of one hundred. So 15% of 80 is (15/100)×80=12. When a quantity grows by 15%, multiply its old value by 1.15.

Try one. What is 3/4 of 28? What fraction of 28 remains?

Worked answer

Divide 28 into four equal parts of 7, then take three: 3×7=21. The remaining amount is 7, which is 1/4 of 28. The two fractions add to one whole.

Watch the units. Adding numerators and denominators directly would give 1/3+1/4=2/7, which is false. Convert to equal-sized parts before adding.

What would convince you that a sequence approaches zero?

The numbers 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, … get smaller. It is tempting to say “eventually they become zero,” but every member of the sequence is positive. Approaching zero means something different: after some point, all subsequent terms lie within any positive distance from zero that you request.

Our sequence is an = 1/n, where n is a positive integer: 1, 2, 3 and so on. A sequence is simply a numbered list. The subscript n records position in the list, not multiplication. For positive numbers, making a denominator larger makes its reciprocal smaller.

We will use ε, the Greek letter epsilon, for a positive tolerance. You choose ε first. Then we must choose a positive integer N so that every term whose index n is at least N is smaller than ε. The order of these choices is the heart of the definition.

Choose a tolerance, then find the tail

Worked example. Suppose your tolerance is 0.1. The tenth term is 1/10 = 0.1, which is equal to the tolerance rather than strictly below it. Starting at N = 11 works: 1/11 is less than 0.1, and every later term is no larger than 1/11.

We can turn this observation into one rule. Write ⌊x⌋ for the floor of x: the greatest integer no larger than x. For example, ⌊3.7⌋ = 3 and ⌊10⌋ = 10. Choose N = ⌊1/ε⌋ + 1. This is always an integer strictly greater than 1/ε.

The lab allows tolerances from 0.01 to 1. Its highlighted points illustrate the rule; the displayed finite collection is not the proof. A very small tolerance may require a tail beginning beyond the visible window. The exact bound still works there.

Compare two valid answers. For ε=0.1, both N=11 and N=100 work. The first is economical; the second discards more initial terms but still controls every retained term. A convergence proof asks for existence of a valid tail, rather than an optimal one. By contrast N=10 fails because its first retained term equals the tolerance. The strict inequality must hold throughout the tail, including its starting index.

A promise covering infinitely many terms

Definition and exact statement. The sequence 1/n converges to zero. Written with its quantifiers, the claim is:

∀ε>0,∃N∈ℕ,∀n≥N:|1n−0|<ε

Read ∀ as “for every” and ∃ as “there exists.” The vertical bars mean absolute value, the distance from zero. Here 1/n is positive, so |1/n − 0| is just 1/n. The set ℕ denotes positive integers in this lesson.

Proof. Let ε be any positive real number. Choose N = ⌊1/ε⌋ + 1. By the definition of floor, N > 1/ε. All numbers involved are positive, so taking reciprocals reverses this strict inequality: 1/N < ε. For every integer n ≥ N, we also have 1/n ≤ 1/N. Therefore 0 < 1/n ≤ 1/N < ε, exactly as required.

This proves the statement for every positive ε, not merely the settings permitted by the lab. It uses the elementary property that real numbers have integer floors, which relies on the fact that the positive integers are not bounded above in the real numbers. We take that background property as given.

The same N covers the entire remaining tail. We do not choose a fresh tolerance to excuse each term. Nor do we need to find the smallest possible N; any valid choice proves convergence. There is no single finite N that makes the whole tail satisfy every positive tolerance simultaneously. N is allowed to depend on ε.

Try the quantifiers in both directions

1. Calculate. Use the rule to choose N for ε = 0.03, treated as exactly 3/100. Verify the guarantee without rounding.

Hint

Compute the floor of 100/3, then compare 1/N with 3/100 by cross multiplication.

Worked solution

Since 33 < 100/3 < 34, the rule chooses N = 34. We have 1/34 < 3/100 because 100 < 102. For every n ≥ 34, 1/n ≤ 1/34 < 3/100. No decimal approximation is needed.

2. Explain. The sequence 0, 1, 0, 1, … has infinitely many terms equal to zero. Does it converge to zero?

Hint

Choose ε = 1/2 and ask whether every sufficiently late term is within that tolerance.

Worked solution

No. Whatever N we choose, an even index n ≥ N has term 1. Its distance from zero exceeds 1/2. Having infinitely many good terms is weaker than having an entire good tail.

Close forever after some point

A limit is a precise promise about all sufficiently late terms. For 1/n, the rule N = ⌊1/ε⌋ + 1 fulfills that promise for every positive tolerance. The terms need never equal their limit.
