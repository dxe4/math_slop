Counting choices

Begin by naming one complete outcome. If you choose one of three drinks and then one of two snacks, an outcome is a drink–snack pair. Each drink has two snack choices, so there are 3×2=6 pairs. This counts combinations of choices; it does not require a probability model.

Worked example. Arrange three distinct postcards in a row. There are three choices for the first place, two remaining choices for the second, and one for the last. The result is 3×2×1=6 arrangements, written 3!.

3!=3×2×1=6

For a positive integer n, n! multiplies every integer from 1 through n. The convention 0!=1 counts the single empty arrangement. Factorials count arrangements of distinct objects when each is used exactly once.

Try one. How many two-letter codes can you make from A, B and C when repetition is forbidden?

Worked answer

Choose the first letter in three ways and the second in two ways, giving six codes: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA and CB. If repetition were allowed, the second position would have three choices too, giving nine.

Watch the rule. If different first choices allow different numbers of second choices, count those branches separately and add. A single fixed product needs the stated number of options at every stage.

How much can a polynomial approximation miss?

A polynomial is easy to evaluate, so it is tempting to replace sin x by x near zero. But “near” is vague until we know how large the error can be. Taylor's theorem attaches a bound to a polynomial built from derivatives. We will make a concrete certified approximation and explain the theorem's hypotheses.

Background capsule. The n-th Taylor polynomial of f at a is Pₙ(x)=Σ from k=0 to n of f(k)(a)(x−a)k/k!. Here k! is the product 1·2·…·k, with 0!=1. The residual Rₙ(x)=f(x)−Pₙ(x) is the actual error, which may be positive or negative. A bound on |Rₙ| is a guarantee. We use derivatives and the fundamental theorem; the proof below establishes an integral remainder when f has enough continuous derivatives, while the familiar Lagrange form needs its own theorem.

A numerical receipt for sin x

Worked example. At a=0, sin 0=0, cos 0=1, the second derivative −sin is zero, and the third derivative −cos is −1. The cubic Taylor polynomial is P₃(x)=x−x³/6. At x=0.5 it gives 0.5−0.125/6≈0.4791667. The true sin(0.5) is about 0.4794255, so the actual error is about 0.0002589.

For a certified bound, use the fourth derivative sin x. Its absolute value is at most 1 everywhere, giving |R₃(0.5)|≤0.5⁴/4!=0.0625/24≈0.0026042. This is valid but loose. A tighter receipt notices that the fourth derivative sin t is at most |t| for t between 0 and 0.5, because |sin t|≤|t|; integrating that sharper envelope produces an error ≤0.5⁵/5!≈0.00026042. The observed error lies below it, close to the certificate.

The first bound is enough for correctness. The sharper one matters if we promise three decimal places: 0.00026042 is less than 0.0005, so rounding P₃(0.5) to three decimals gives the same rounded result as sin(0.5), provided the rounded value is not near a midpoint. Here P₃ rounds to 0.479 and the error interval stays below 0.479427, so it remains below 0.4795.

Where the bound comes from

Statement. Suppose f has continuous derivatives through order n+1 on the interval between a and x, and |f(n+1)(t)|≤M there. Then its Taylor polynomial Pₙ at a satisfies the displayed error bound. For x<a, interpret the interval between them with the same absolute length.

|f(x)−Pn(x)|≤M|x−a|n+1(n+1)!

Integral-remainder proof for x≥a. The fundamental theorem gives the base case R₀(x)=f(x)−f(a)=∫ₐˣf′(t)dt. Suppose Rn−1(x)=1/(n−1)! times ∫ₐˣf(n)(t)(x−t)n−1dt. Integrate I=1/n! times ∫ₐˣf(n+1)(t)(x−t)ndt by parts: the boundary term is −f(n)(a)(x−a)n/n!, and the remaining integral is Rn−1. Thus I=Rn−1−f(n)(a)(x−a)n/n!=Rₙ. This establishes the formula f(x)=Pₙ(x)+I. Taking absolute values and using |f(n+1)|≤M gives |Rₙ|≤(M/n!)∫ₐˣ(x−t)ndt=M(x−a)n+1/(n+1)!. Reversing the oriented interval gives the same absolute bound for x<a. This is a derivation under continuous-derivative assumptions, not a proof for every function that happens to have n derivatives at a.

For x≠a, under the stated continuous-derivative assumptions, the Lagrange statement also says Rₙ=f(n+1)(ξ)(x−a)n+1/(n+1)! for some ξ strictly between a and x. At x=a the remainder is zero directly. We do not need to locate ξ or assert it is unique. The integral proof supplies the bound directly. It also reveals that a small bound depends jointly on a small interval and control of a higher derivative.

Boundary. Having many derivatives at one point alone gives no uniform M on a surrounding interval. Even derivatives of every order at a do not by themselves guarantee the infinite Taylor series equals f nearby; the next lesson gives an exact counterexample.

Compute and certify

1. Calculate. Use P₂(x)=1+x+x²/2 for eˣ at x=0.2. Give a valid error bound using M=e0.2<1.23 for the third derivative on [0,0.2].

Hint for 1

Substitute x=0.2 and n=2 into M|x|³/3!.

Worked solution for 1

P₂(0.2)=1+0.2+0.04/2=1.22. The bound is 1.23×0.2³/6=1.23×0.008/6=0.00164. Hence e0.2 lies between 1.21836 and 1.22164; since its third derivative is positive, the integral remainder is positive and the sharper one-sided interval is [1.22,1.22164].

2. Explain. If |f(4)|≤5 on [0,1], can a cubic Taylor approximation at zero miss f(1) by more than 1/4?

Hint for 2

Use n=3, M=5 and |x−a|=1.

Worked solution for 2

No, provided f has the required continuous derivatives. The theorem gives |R₃(1)|≤5·1⁴/4!=5/24≈0.2083, which is less than 1/4. The sign of the error is not determined by the absolute derivative bound alone.

A receipt has assumptions

A Taylor polynomial matches derivatives at its centre; the remainder theorem controls how far that matching reaches when a higher derivative is bounded over the whole interval. A finite-degree error bound says nothing by itself about the limit as degree tends to infinity. To claim an infinite Taylor series recovers a function, show the residual tends to zero.
