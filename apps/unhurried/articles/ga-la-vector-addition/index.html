Coordinates and slope

An ordered pair (x,y) specifies a position in the coordinate plane: x measures horizontal displacement and y measures vertical displacement from the origin. The order matters. The points (2,5) and (5,2) usually occupy different places.

Worked example. From P=(1,2) to Q=(4,8), the horizontal change is 4−1=3 and the vertical change is 8−2=6. The slope is vertical change divided by horizontal change, giving 6/3=2.

m=y2−y1x2−x1=63=2

Use the same order in both subtractions. Reversing the points gives (2−8)/(1−4)=−6/−3=2 again. A horizontal line has slope zero. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so this quotient does not define a finite slope.

Try one. Find the slope from (−1,4) to (2,−2).

Worked answer

The horizontal change is 2−(−1)=3. The vertical change is −2−4=−6. Dividing gives slope −2. The negative sign means that y decreases as x increases along this line.

Watch the units. If x is time in hours and y is distance in kilometres, the slope has units of kilometres per hour. The scales carry mathematical information.

Two walks, one displacement

You walk three blocks east and one north, then one block west and four north. Where are you relative to your starting point? The route has bends, but its displacement is simply two east and five north. A vector records this net change, independent of the winding route that produced it.

Background capsule. Fix horizontal east and vertical north directions, with one block as the unit. An ordered pair (a,b) means a units horizontally and b vertically; negative entries point west or south. A point is a location, while a vector is a displacement. We can draw the same vector with its tail at any point. Coordinates depend on our chosen axes and unit, but the displacement does not. The zero vector (0,0) changes no position.

Read the journey by components

Worked example. The first leg has displacement u=(3,1), the second v=(−1,4). Starting at (0,0), the first arrival is (3,1); starting there, the second arrival is (2,5). Thus u+v=(2,5). If the second leg is taken first, you reach (−1,4) and then (2,5) again. Addition forgets the route order when only the final displacement matters.

Twice the first leg gives 2u=(6,2); its reverse is −u=(−3,−1). A half-leg gives ½u=(3/2,1/2). Scaling a vector changes a displacement, not an absolute destination. If you begin at (7,2), adding u lands at (10,3), yet u still has coordinates (3,1).

Why component addition works

Exact statement and proof. In the real coordinate plane with fixed horizontal and vertical unit directions, successive displacements (a,b) and (c,d) have net displacement

(a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d)

To prove it, start at any point (x,y). The first displacement lands at (x+a,y+b). The second lands at ((x+a)+c,(y+b)+d). Associativity of real-number addition rewrites this as (x+(a+c),y+(b+d)). Subtract the starting coordinates: the net change is (a+c,b+d). This argument does not assume the starting point is the origin.

Scalar multiplication is defined componentwise: t(a,b)=(ta,tb). It agrees with repeating a displacement when t is a positive integer, reversing it when t is negative, and dividing it into equal parts for rational t. Real scalars extend the same coordinate rule. For example, 2(u+v)=2(2,5)=(4,10), while 2u+2v=(6,2)+(−2,8)=(4,10). In general t(u+v)=tu+tv because multiplication distributes over addition separately in each coordinate.

These laws explain why a translated drawing of an arrow is still the same vector. The starting point can change, but the two differences between head and tail stay equal. In a different rotated coordinate system, the pair of numbers may change; the geometric arrow does not. Later, a basis will make that distinction precise. This lesson proves the coordinate rules in the plane; it does not assert axioms for every abstract vector space.

Try two independent checks

1. Calculation. Starting at (5,−2), take displacement p=(−4,3), then twice q=(2,−1). Find the final point and net displacement.

Hint for question 1

Compute p+2q first; only then add the starting point.

Full solution for question 1

Twice q is (4,−2). Hence p+2q=(−4,3)+(4,−2)=(0,1). From (5,−2) we land at (5,−1). The final point (5,−1) and the net displacement (0,1) answer different questions.

2. Explanation. A friend says the vectors from (0,0) to (2,1) and from (7,−3) to (9,−2) are different because they have different starting points. Decide whether the claim is correct, and say what would change if these were position vectors instead.

Hint for question 2

Subtract each arrow's tail coordinates from its head coordinates.

Full solution for question 2

The first arrow changes position by (2−0,1−0)=(2,1). The second changes it by (9−7,−2−(−3))=(2,1). They represent the same displacement vector. If the ordered pairs named points or position vectors based at a fixed origin, (2,1) and (9,−2) would indeed name different locations.

What the arithmetic remembers

Vector addition combines net changes; scalar multiplication repeats, reverses, or resizes one. The component formulas were proved from coordinate displacements and work for any real entries, including negative or zero values. The path itself disappears: a long detour and a straight walk can have the same displacement. This is useful when predicting a destination, but it would be a failure if you used the displacement alone to infer distance traveled.

You can stop once you can distinguish a route, a final point, and a net vector. For another use of the same component arithmetic, see how two basis arrows determine a linear map.
