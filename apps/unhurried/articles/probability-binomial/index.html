The question and needed ideas

Flip a fair coin ten times and count heads. The event “exactly three heads” contains many sequences: H H H T T T T T T T is one, but so is T H T H T T H T T T. Counting only the first sequence would miss most ways to get the same count. The binomial distribution turns the sequence tree into probabilities for counts.

Background capsule. A trial has success or failure. Here success means head. Let X be the number of successes in n trials. The notation C(n,k), also written “n choose k,” counts selections of k trial positions from n. The factorial n! multiplies 1·2·…·n, with 0!=1. A binomial model requires a fixed n, two outcomes per trial, a common success chance p, and independence across trials.

Work through the model

Worked example. For ten fair flips, a particular sequence with three heads and seven tails has probability (1/2)³(1/2)⁷=1/1024. There are C(10,3)=10·9·8/(3·2·1)=120 choices of head positions. The sequences are disjoint, so P(X=3)=120/1024=15/128, about 0.1172.

For a biased coin with p=0.3, the same count probability is 120(0.3)³(0.7)⁷, about 0.2668. The position count stays 120, but each sequence now has probability p³(1−p)⁷. We do not need to enumerate all 1024 sequences to compute it.

As a quick boundary check, P(X=0)=(1−p)ⁿ because there is just one all-failure sequence; P(X=n)=pⁿ. For p=0 all mass is at zero, and for p=1 all mass is at n. These extreme cases are valid in the formula with the usual convention that a zero exponent contributes one to the product.

The statement and its limits

Exact statement. Under the four binomial assumptions, every sequence with k successes has probability pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ by independence and common p. There are C(n,k) such sequences: order n distinct positions in n! ways, but divide by k! reorderings of chosen positions and (n−k)! reorderings of unchosen positions. Adding equal probabilities of these disjoint sequences proves the displayed formula.

The probabilities for k=0 through n sum to one because those events partition all trial sequences; equivalently their sum is (p+(1−p))ⁿ=1 by the binomial theorem. Writing X as a sum of success indicators I₁+···+Iₙ gives E[X]=np from linearity. Independence makes covariance terms vanish, giving Var(X)=np(1−p); that last variance identity needs a separate indicator calculation and is not inferred merely from the count formula.

If you draw tokens without replacement, the conditional chance of success after the first draw changes with what was drawn, and the trials are dependent. The binomial formula is then generally wrong. A fixed number of draws is not enough by itself.

P(X=k)=(n!k!(n−k)!)pk(1−p)n−k

Here is the moment calculation in elementary terms. Let Iᵢ equal one when trial i succeeds and zero otherwise. Then E[Iᵢ]=0(1−p)+1p=p, and because Iᵢ²=Iᵢ, Var(Iᵢ)=E[Iᵢ²]−E[Iᵢ]²=p−p²=p(1−p). The count X is I₁+···+Iₙ, so linearity gives E[X]=np. To see its variance, expand (X−np)² as a sum of squared individual deviations plus pairs of cross-products. Independence makes each cross-product have mean E[Iᵢ−p]E[Iⱼ−p]=0 for distinct trials. Only n individual variances remain, yielding np(1−p). Without independence, those cross-products need not vanish.

Try two cases

Exercise 1. A fair coin is flipped four times. Find the chance of exactly two heads.

Hint for exercise 1

Choose two head positions from four.

Full solution to exercise 1

C(4,2)=4!/(2!2!)=6. Each two-head sequence has probability (1/2)⁴=1/16. Hence P(X=2)=6/16=3/8. The six sequences can be listed to check the count: HHTT, HTHT, HTTH, THHT, THTH, TTHH.

Exercise 2. A bag has two red and two blue tokens. Draw twice without replacement and count red draws. Would a Binomial(2,1/2) model give the correct chance of two reds?

Hint for exercise 2

Compare the second-red chance after a first red with 1/2.

Full solution to exercise 2

No. The true chance of two reds is (2/4)(1/3)=1/6. A Binomial(2,1/2) formula would give (1/2)²=1/4. After a red is removed, only one of three remaining tokens is red, so independence fails: the conditional red chance changes after the first draw, despite both draw positions having marginal red chance 1/2.

What to carry forward

The binomial model is a count model for a very specific repeated experiment. Its elegance comes from equal sequence probabilities within each success count. Check the trial mechanism before using it. When n grows, a normal curve can sometimes approximate the count bars, but that later step is never exact for finite n.
