Can a modest repeated push make a large motion?

For a mass scaled to one, consider x″+ω0²x=F cos(ωt), where ω0>0 is the natural angular frequency and F is forcing per unit mass. The right-hand side feeds energy to an otherwise undamped oscillator. If the forcing frequency matches ω0, the usual bounded sinusoidal guess fails; a growing factor t appears. That is exact resonance in this ideal model.

Local background. The derivative of cos(ωt) is −ω sin(ωt); differentiating twice multiplies it by −ω². A particular solution solves the forced equation, while any solution of the homogeneous equation may be added to satisfy initial conditions. The word amplitude here means the size of a sinusoidal component, not an assertion that every transient has that size.

Find the resonant response

Worked example. Set ω=ω0 and try xp(t)=Ct sin(ω0t). Differentiation gives xp″+ω0²xp=2Cω0cos(ω0t). Thus C=F/(2ω0). For ω0=2 and F=1, xp=(t/4)sin(2t). This particular solution starts with x(0)=x′(0)=0, so it is also the solution for a mass initially at rest. At peak phases its envelope grows like t/4, although it crosses zero repeatedly.

Where energy enters, and where it leaves

For undamped off-resonance forcing, substituting A cos(ωt) gives A=F/(ω0²−ω²). That formula is invalid at ω=ω0 because its denominator vanishes; it does not describe an “infinite amplitude” solution at a finite time. The correct resonant particular solution is

xp(t)=F2ω0tsin(ω0t)

Its magnitude is at most |F|t/(2ω0), and reaches that envelope at an unbounded sequence of peak phases. For F≠0, the undamped ideal model therefore has an unbounded long-time response. A finite observation interval still has finite displacement.

Add linear damping: x″+2γx′+ω0²x=F cos(ωt), γ>0. Write a steady particular solution a cos(ωt)+b sin(ωt). Equating cosine and sine coefficients gives (ω0²−ω²)a+2γωb=F and (ω0²−ω²)b−2γωa=0. Solving yields an amplitude

A(ω)=|F|/√[(ω0²−ω²)²+(2γω)²]. For fixed γ>0 and ω>0 this is finite. At ω=ω0, A=|F|/(2γω0). The homogeneous transient decays because its characteristic roots have negative real parts; the response approaches the finite periodic particular solution. This statement assumes constant positive damping, constant forcing amplitude, and the linear model.

The energy E=((x′)²+ω0²x²)/2 satisfies E′=F x′cos(ωt)−2γ(x′)². The first term is instantaneous input power and can change sign; the dissipation term is always nonpositive. Their sum need not have a fixed sign, so a plot of displacement alone is not a proof about monotone energy. At resonance without damping, the well-timed input can accumulate over many cycles. With damping, average input can balance loss in steady state.

Actual oscillators can leave the linear regime at large displacement, and a driven spring can break. The unbounded ideal response is a warning about a model's assumptions, not a promise of physical infinity.

Test two frequency claims

1. Nonresonant forcing

Exercise 1. Find a particular solution of x″+9x=2 cos(2t). Then say what happens to the proposed form if the forcing frequency becomes 3.

Hint for 1

Substitute A cos(2t); its second derivative is −4A cos(2t).

Worked solution for 1

(9−4)A=2, so A=2/5 and xp=(2/5)cos(2t). At frequency 3 the coefficient would be 9−9=0, so no finite A of this form solves the equation. A resonant particular solution for F=2 and ω0=3 is (t/3)sin(3t).

2. Damping at resonance

Exercise 2. For x″+0.4x′+4x=cos(2t), compute the steady amplitude. Does the resonant response grow without bound?

Hint for 2

Here ω0=2 and 2γ=0.4, so γ=0.2.

Worked solution for 2

At ω=2, A=1/(2γω0)=1/(2·0.2·2)=1.25. The homogeneous transient decays because damping is positive, leaving a bounded sinusoid of amplitude 1.25. The undamped t-growth formula does not apply.

The precise resonance claim

Matching frequencies produce a linearly growing envelope in an undamped linear oscillator. Positive linear damping yields a finite steady frequency response. The difference follows from substituting actual solutions and accounting for energy input and loss. Continue to homogeneous spring motion for the root classification behind the transient.
