How many tiles does a mural need?

A community room plans a paper-tile mural on a rectangular panel. The first useful question is not “What is the answer?” but “What decision must the answer support?” Here it is the number of square tiles to prepare. We can build a compact model that exposes the assumptions behind that number, checks the units, and leaves room to revise it.

Background capsule. Area of a rectangle is width times height. A metre multiplied by a metre gives a square metre (m²). A centimetre is one hundredth of a metre, so 20 cm is 0.20 m. An approximation sign means a quantity is estimated under chosen assumptions; it does not excuse an unexplained step. We need multiplication, division, rounding upward, and these unit facts.

Suppose the panel is 3 m wide and 2 m high. Tiles are 20 cm squares. We assume complete coverage, negligible grout, no overlap, and no cuts wasted at the boundary. Each assumption is a proposed description of the task, not a mathematical truth. A useful model note records them before anyone buys material.

Make the estimate checkable

Worked example. The panel area is 3 × 2 = 6 m². A tile side is 0.20 m, so its face area is 0.20 × 0.20 = 0.04 m². Dividing gives 150 tiles. In this particular example the dimensions divide exactly: 3/0.2 = 15 tiles along the width and 2/0.2 = 10 along the height. Their product is also 150. This second calculation tests the first rather than adding a new assumption.

If the panel is instead 3.1 m wide, the raw area ratio is 6.2/0.04 = 155. But a grid of whole 20 cm tiles needs 16 columns and 10 rows, or 160 tiles, if cut pieces are not reused. The area ratio now understates procurement. The choice between area and grid models depends on whether reusable cuts, seams, and a partial edge are allowed. A drawing or small physical trial would resolve that design detail.

What the formula actually says

Let W and H be panel dimensions in metres and let a be tile side in metres. Under full coverage with no waste, the continuous area estimate is

Narea≈WHa2

The numerator and denominator are both in m², so their quotient has no physical unit; a count is dimensionless. This dimensional check is exact. The approximation between the quotient and the number of physical tiles depends on the coverage assumptions. For an axis-aligned grid using whole tiles and no reuse of offcuts, the count is instead ceiling(W/a) × ceiling(H/a), where ceiling rounds up to the next integer. That expression is exact for that specified grid rule, but not necessarily the cheapest cutting plan.

A modelling cycle has distinct moves: frame the decision, choose variables, state assumptions, compute a consequence, compare against a check, and revise. For this mural, count dimensions independently and try a paper mock-up at the edge. If the check disagrees, locate which assumption failed. “The model was wrong” is less useful than “the continuous-area assumption omitted a partial column.”

This is a bounded claim about a toy rectangular panel. It does not predict labour, price, colour arrangement, breakage, or a curved wall. Those may matter to the real decision and would require measured inputs and perhaps another model.

Test the model

1. Calculate. A 2.4 m by 1.8 m panel receives 30 cm square tiles. Find both the area estimate and the whole-tile grid count. Explain their agreement or difference.

Hint for question 1

Convert 30 cm to 0.30 m; compare the panel dimensions with multiples of that side.

Full solution to question 1

Panel area is 2.4 × 1.8 = 4.32 m². Tile area is 0.30² = 0.09 m². The quotient is 4.32/0.09 = 48. The width fits 2.4/0.3 = 8 and the height fits 1.8/0.3 = 6, giving 8 × 6 = 48 whole tiles. There is no partial boundary in this case, so the two rules agree.

2. Diagnose. The tile side is reported as “20” without a unit, while the panel is 3 m by 2 m. A teammate computes 6/400. What went wrong, and what check would you request?

Hint for question 2

Inspect the units of numerator and denominator before dividing.

Full solution to question 2

Six has unit m²; 400 would have square units of an unspecified tile-side unit. Dividing them is not a meaningful count until the units match. If “20” means centimetres, convert to 0.20 m and use 6/0.04 = 150. I would ask for the tile specification, then verify dimensions by counting 15 columns and 10 rows on a scaled drawing.

A useful stopping point

A model earns trust through a visible decision, assumptions, units and a check, not through a complicated equation. The area quotient was exact arithmetic yet only an approximate procurement model. Its failure at a partial edge shows why a boundary case is useful. Next, scale a room drawing to see how dimensions change at different powers, or vary assumptions to find which input drives the answer.
