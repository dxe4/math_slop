What does “one piece” mean without a picture?

Two separated intervals are visibly in two pieces, but pictures are unreliable for complicated spaces. A topological separation replaces visual judgment with two disjoint, nonempty, relatively open sets covering the space. We will use that definition to prove that a continuous map cannot tear a connected domain into a disconnected image.

Background capsule. If X is a space and A⊆X, a set W⊆A is open relative to A when W=A∩U for some set U open in X. For f:X→Y, its image f(X) consists of outputs f(x). Continuity means preimages of open sets are open. A separation of X is a pair U,V of disjoint, nonempty open subsets of X whose union is X. A space with no separation is connected. All topologies in the examples are ordinary metric or their subspace topologies.

Test two subsets of the line

Worked example. Let A=[0,1]∪[2,3]. The sets [0,1] and [2,3] are open relative to A: intersect A with (−1,1.5) or (1.5,4), respectively. They are nonempty, disjoint and cover A, so A is disconnected. Each component separately is connected because it is an interval.

For [0,1], try to draw a dividing line at c=1/2. The left part [0,c) and right part (c,1] leave out c. Assigning c to one side destroys openness of that side at c. This is a clue to the interval theorem. A proof uses completeness: if an interval were separated into U and V, take points u∈U and v∈V with u<v, and let c be the supremum of U∩[u,v]. Relative openness of whichever side contains c forces points of that side across the proposed boundary, a contradiction. Details require checking c cannot lie in neither side and using a neighborhood of c; the core fact is the least-upper-bound property.

A continuous map may identify different points and even collapse everything to one point. It may not create a separation in its image. For example, f(t)=(cos 2πt,sin 2πt) takes [0,1] continuously onto a circle; it joins the two endpoint images, yet the image is connected.

Pull an alleged split backwards

Exact statement. For a continuous map f:X→Y of topological spaces, if X is connected, then f(X), with the subspace topology inherited from Y, is connected:

(X connected∧f continuous)⇒f(X) connected

Proof. Suppose instead that f(X) has a separation A,B. Since these are open relative to f(X), write A=f(X)∩O and B=f(X)∩P for open O,P in Y. The sets f−1(O) and f−1(P) are open in X by continuity. They are disjoint: no f(x) can lie in both A and B. Both are nonempty because A and B each contain some output of f. They cover X because every output lies in A∪B. This is a separation of X, contradicting its connectedness. Hence the image is connected. The proof uses only definitions and is valid in general topological spaces.

The converse is false. A constant map from A=[0,1]∪[2,3] to a one-point space is continuous and has connected image, while A is disconnected. Nor does connectedness mean every two points can be joined by a path in arbitrary spaces; path connectedness is a stronger condition. For intervals and circles in the examples, explicit paths exist. We avoid inferring connectedness merely from a drawing that appears unbroken.

Why do intervals stay connected? The supremum argument sketched above can be made exact. Choose u<v on opposite sides, let c=sup(U∩[u,v]). If c∈U, openness of U supplies points of U just right of c (unless c=v, but v∈V), contradicting the bound. If c∈V, openness of V gives a left neighborhood free of U, contradicting the supremum property. Thus neither case works. This proof assumes real completeness; in a rational interval a missing irrational point can produce a separation.

Find or forbid a separation

1. Is B=(−2,−1)∪(1,2) connected as a subspace of ℝ? Specify a separation using ambient open sets.

Hint for question 1

Choose one ambient interval around each component and intersect with B.

Full solution for question 1

Let U=B∩(−3,0)=(−2,−1) and V=B∩(0,3)=(1,2). Both are nonempty and open relative to B, disjoint, and their union is B. They form a separation, so B is disconnected. The gap is expressed by relative openness, not just by its width.

2. A proposed continuous surjection h:[0,1]→{0,1}, with {0,1} carrying the usual subspace topology of ℝ, sends some inputs to each value. Explain why it cannot exist.

Hint for question 2

The two singleton subsets of {0,1} are relatively open. Pull them back.

Full solution for question 2

The target {0,1} is separated by {0}={0,1}∩(−1/2,1/2) and {1}={0,1}∩(1/2,3/2). Surjectivity makes both preimages nonempty. If h were continuous, those preimages would be disjoint open subsets covering [0,1], separating an interval. Since [0,1] is connected, this is impossible.

Carry the hypothesis with the claim

A connected domain has a connected image under a continuous map. The theorem does not run backwards and does not say all connected spaces admit paths. The interval-connectedness argument relies on completeness of ℝ; the image theorem needs only topology. These distinctions support the cut-point test for an interval and a circle.
