Can an equation reveal a tangent?

A tangent is a line giving the first-order direction of a curve near a point. When a curve is described by F(x,y)=0, its derivatives often reveal that line without solving globally for y. We will derive the rule at a regular point and see exactly why it can fail at a singular one.

Background capsule. The partial derivative Fx differentiates while y is held fixed; Fy holds x fixed. Their pair ∇F=(Fx,Fy) is the gradient. For a differentiable polynomial F, a small displacement (h,k) from p=(a,b) changes F by approximately Fx(p)h+Fy(p)k. More precisely, the omitted error divided by √(h²+k²) tends to zero. A point is smooth for this plane curve when F(p)=0 and ∇F(p)≠(0,0).

Read the tangent from a circle

Worked example. Let F=x²+y²−5 and p=(1,2). First check F(p)=1+4−5=0. The partial derivatives are Fx=2x and Fy=2y, so ∇F(p)=(2,4), which is nonzero. The candidate tangent is 2(x−1)+4(y−2)=0, equivalently x+2y=5. Its direction vector (2,−1) is perpendicular to (2,4), because their dot product is 4−4=0.

We can check the line without relying on a drawing. Put x=1+2t and y=2−t. The line equation holds for every t, while F(1+2t,2−t)=5t². The residual begins at order t², not order t. Thus moving along this line changes the curve equation more slowly near t=0 than a generic direction does. The line is a first-order match, though its other points are generally not on the circle. This is what tangent means here.

Derive the rule and mark its boundary

Exact first-order tangent statement. Let F be a real polynomial, p=(a,b) satisfy F(p)=0, and assume ∇F(p)≠0. The linearized tangent line is

Fx(p)(x−a)+Fy(p)(y−b)=0

To derive it, take a differentiable path γ(t)=(x(t),y(t)) on F=0 with γ(0)=p. The chain rule yields 0=dF(γ(t))/dt at t=0, hence Fx(p)x′(0)+Fy(p)y′(0)=0. Every actual velocity is therefore perpendicular to the gradient. Since the gradient is nonzero, this perpendicular set is a unique line through the origin in velocity space; translate it through p to obtain the displayed line. If Fy(p)≠0, the implicit-function theorem additionally guarantees a local differentiable graph y(x) with slope −Fx/Fy. If Fy=0 but Fx≠0, solve locally for x as a function of y instead. We use the theorem for local existence, not a proof of it.

The path argument proves that any existing differentiable branch has the stated direction. The local graph theorem supplies existence and uniqueness of that smooth local branch under the nonzero-gradient hypothesis. When both derivatives vanish, the displayed equation becomes 0=0 and selects every point, so it gives no tangent. That does not prove there is no tangent: the cusp y²=x³ has a limiting x-axis direction despite its zero gradient. Its local branch analysis belongs in the singularity lesson.

Compute and test the boundary

1. Calculation. Find the tangent to xy−1=0 at (2,1/2), and verify the point lies on the curve.

Hint for question 1

Fx=y and Fy=x. Insert the coordinates before writing the tangent equation.

Full solution for question 1

At (2,1/2), xy−1=2·(1/2)−1=0. The gradient is (1/2,2), so the tangent is (1/2)(x−2)+2(y−1/2)=0. Multiplying by two and simplifying gives x+4y=4. It passes through the stated point because 2+4(1/2)=4.

2. Explanation. For F=(y−x²)², the gradient vanishes at every point of the parabola. Does that mean the parabola has no tangent at (1,1)?

Hint for question 2

Compare the zero set of F with that of y−x², then parameterize its points near x=1.

Full solution for question 2

No. Squaring changes the defining polynomial but not its zero set: F=0 exactly when y=x². Parameterize near (1,1) by (1+t,(1+t)²)=(1+t,1+2t+t²). The first-order direction is (1,2), so the tangent is y−1=2(x−1). The gradient of the squared equation vanishes because it carries a repeated factor; this particular defining equation fails the nonzero-gradient test.

A condition on the equation matters

At a zero with nonzero gradient, the gradient is normal to the tangent and the linear equation is justified by the chain rule and local graph theorem. A zero gradient makes this test inconclusive; it does not by itself settle the geometry of the underlying set. Stop once you can check F(p)=0, compute ∇F(p), and say explicitly whether the smooth-point hypothesis holds.
