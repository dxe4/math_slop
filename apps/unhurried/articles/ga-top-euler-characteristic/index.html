Networks and degree counts

A finite simple undirected graph consists of vertices and edges. Each edge joins two different vertices, with at most one edge for each pair. “Undirected” means that AB and BA name the same connection. A drawing shows these relationships; its line lengths need not carry any information.

Worked example. Use vertices A, B, C and D, with edges AB, AC and BC. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. A, B and C each have degree 2, while D has degree 0 because it is isolated.

2+2+2+0=6=2×3

Every edge contributes one to the degree at each of its two ends. Adding all degrees therefore gives twice the number of edges. An isolated vertex still belongs to the graph and contributes zero to the sum.

Try one. Add the edge CD. What changes?

Worked answer

C's degree rises to 3, and D's rises to 1. A and B stay at 2. The degree sum becomes 8, and there are now four edges, again giving twice the edge count.

Watch the convention. A repeated connection or an edge from a vertex to itself requires a different graph convention. Keep the simple-graph assumptions explicit when translating a real network.

Counting choices

Begin by naming one complete outcome. If you choose one of three drinks and then one of two snacks, an outcome is a drink–snack pair. Each drink has two snack choices, so there are 3×2=6 pairs. This counts combinations of choices; it does not require a probability model.

Worked example. Arrange three distinct postcards in a row. There are three choices for the first place, two remaining choices for the second, and one for the last. The result is 3×2×1=6 arrangements, written 3!.

3!=3×2×1=6

For a positive integer n, n! multiplies every integer from 1 through n. The convention 0!=1 counts the single empty arrangement. Factorials count arrangements of distinct objects when each is used exactly once.

Try one. How many two-letter codes can you make from A, B and C when repetition is forbidden?

Worked answer

Choose the first letter in three ways and the second in two ways, giving six codes: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA and CB. If repetition were allowed, the second position would have three choices too, giving nine.

Watch the rule. If different first choices allow different numbers of second choices, count those branches separately and add. A single fixed product needs the stated number of options at every stage.

Why does a plane drawing keep giving two?

Draw a square and one diagonal. It has four corners, five line segments and three regions if you include the open region outside the square: 4 − 5 + 3 = 2. Bending an edge or adding a diagonal changes the counts but preserves this result.

Background capsule. A graph has vertices (marked points) and edges joining them. Here it is finite, has at least one vertex, and is connected: you can travel along edges between any two vertices. A plane drawing puts its vertices and edges in the plane with no edge crossings except at a shared endpoint; a non-loop edge is a simple arc, and a loop is a simple closed curve. A face is one connected region left after removing the drawing from the plane. Count the unbounded outside region as one face. A spanning tree uses all vertices, stays connected and has no cycle. You can build one by joining a new vertex at each step.

Build a square one edge at a time

Worked example. Label four vertices 1, 2, 3, 4 around a square. Start with the path 1–2–3–4. The path is a spanning tree. Add 4–1 to enclose a face, then 1–3 to split it. Here are the exact counts:

A proof by growing from a tree

Exact statement. For every finite, connected plane graph with at least one vertex, as defined above, with V vertices, E edges and F faces including the outside face,

V−E+F=2

Proof conditional on one geometric lemma. We use this plane-separation lemma, whose proof is not included here: a finite plane tree has just the outside face, and adding a noncrossing edge inside one face of a connected plane drawing, with endpoints on the drawing, splits that face into exactly two. It is the unproved geometric input.

Choose a spanning tree T. It has V − 1 edges. Choose a root. Every other vertex has a unique path from it; a second path would make a cycle. Order the remaining vertices so each parent comes before its child. Each new vertex is then joined by exactly its parent edge, giving one starting vertex and V − 1 added edges. By the plane-separation lemma, the tree has F = 1. Hence its count is V − (V − 1) + 1 = 2.

Now restore the omitted edges, one at a time, following their positions in the original crossing-free drawing. The interior of each new edge lies in one face of the current drawing. Its endpoints are already joined through the tree, so it creates a cycle (a loop counts as a one-edge cycle). The stated separation lemma says that this addition raises F by one. It also raises E by one and leaves V fixed. The quantity V − E + F cannot change. After all edges are restored, it is still two.

The counting proof is conditional on the plane-separation lemma. Tracing a cycle suggests the lemma, but examples do not prove it for every allowed curve.

Loops and repeated edges also count. A noncrossing loop attached to one vertex counts as one edge and encloses a face: V = 1, E = 1, F = 2. Two distinct noncrossing edges between the same pair of vertices make a lens: V = 2, E = 2, F = 2. Both obey the formula. A crossing in an edge interior needs a new vertex to meet the plane-drawing conditions.

Try a new drawing

1. Calculation. Draw a pentagon with vertices 1 through 5 in order. Add diagonals 1–3 and 1–4 inside, without crossing. Find V, E and F including the outside face. Explain the face count by starting from the path 1–2–3–4–5.

Hint for question 1

Start with four path edges and one face. Restore edges 5–1, 1–3 and 1–4, each of which divides an existing face.

Full solution for question 1

There are V = 5 vertices and E = 5 pentagon sides + 2 diagonals = 7 edges. The path has F = 1; adding 5–1, 1–3 and 1–4 gives F = 4. Indeed, three inside triangles and the outside face are present. The check is 5 − 7 + 4 = 2.

2. Counterexample to an overbroad claim. Draw two separate triangles, far apart. Someone says “every crossing-free graph has V − E + F = 2.” Count carefully and explain the missing hypothesis.

Hint for question 2

The two triangles share one outside region, but each has its own inside region.

Full solution for question 2

The drawing has V = 6, E = 6 and F = 3: two bounded interiors and one shared outside. Hence V − E + F = 6 − 6 + 3 = 3. The graph is not connected, so it has no spanning tree containing all six vertices. The connectedness hypothesis fails, so the theorem does not apply.

Know what the two belongs to

Given the unproved plane-separation lemma, the spanning-tree count proves the formula for connected plane graph drawings. Include the outside face. This is a gateway to Euler characteristic, not a classification of surfaces. A drawing on a torus needs a different setting and hypotheses.
