Substitution and equations

A letter in a formula stands for a value. Substitution replaces that letter everywhere with a chosen number. Parentheses help keep a negative input together: if f(x)=x²+2x, then f(−3)=(−3)²+2(−3)=9−6=3. Squaring happens before adding.

Worked example. To solve 3x+5=20, subtract 5 from both sides and then divide both sides by 3. Applying the same operation to both sides preserves the equality; the divisor here is nonzero.

3x+5=20⇒3x=15⇒x=5

Substitute the answer back: 3×5+5=20. This checks the proposed value against the original equation. The distributive rule also lets you expand a bracket: 2(x+4)=2x+8.

Try one. Solve 2(x+4)=18 and check the result.

Worked answer

Divide both sides by 2 to obtain x+4=9. Subtract 4 to get x=5. Checking gives 2(5+4)=2×9=18, as required.

Watch the sign. The values (−3)²=9 and −3²=−9 differ: the second expression applies the minus sign after squaring 3. Keep the parentheses when substituting negative values.

A fixed distribution need not be a destination

A playlist changes Calm (C) to Bright (B) with chance 1/3 and B to C with chance 2/3. A stationary distribution stays unchanged after one chime. Whether every other initial distribution approaches it is a separate question.

Background capsule. A row distribution π=(c,b) has nonnegative entries c+b=1. With row convention, one step is πP. A stationary distribution solves πP=π: applying the transition rule leaves its distribution unchanged. A chain is irreducible if every state can eventually reach every other with positive probability. It is aperiodic if its returns are not locked into a common cycle length greater than one. In a finite irreducible aperiodic chain, the distribution from any start converges to its unique stationary distribution; that theorem is stated here, while the two-state calculation below is proved directly.

Balance the flows

Worked example. The transition rows are C→(2/3,1/3) and B→(2/3,1/3). Remarkably, both rows are identical. Whatever mixture c,b we start with, the next distribution is (2/3,1/3), because c+b=1. Hence π=(2/3,1/3) is stationary, and every start reaches it after exactly one step. Its Calm probability satisfies c=(2/3)c+(2/3)b=(2/3)(c+b)=2/3.

For a less instant example, change the rule to C→(3/4,1/4) and B→(1/2,1/2). At equilibrium the flow from C to B, c/4, must equal the flow from B to C, b/2. Thus c=2b and c+b=1, giving c=2/3, b=1/3 again. Starting certainly in C gives Calm probabilities 1, 3/4, then (3/4)(3/4)+(1/4)(1/2)=11/16. These approach 2/3: subtracting 2/3 from the next Calm chance multiplies the difference by 1/4. We can prove convergence directly in this example because repeated multiplication by 1/4 sends any initial error to zero.

Equation, theorem boundary, counterexamples

π=πP,∑irπi=1

These equations define stationarity for a finite transition matrix. A finite chain always has at least one stationary distribution, but it may have several. If irreducible, it has exactly one. If also aperiodic, distributions from every starting state converge to it. For finite chains irreducibility supplies positive recurrence automatically. We use this standard theorem only as a stated boundary; solving πP=π alone is elementary algebra and does not prove the theorem.

Periodicity blocks convergence in the two-state alternation A→B, B→A. The stationary row is (1/2,1/2), yet a start at A alternates between (1,0) and (0,1). Reducibility permits nonuniqueness: with P equal to the identity, every starting row is stationary. In that example the distribution does converge—it simply remains where it began—so reducibility alone does not mean failure of convergence. The point is that no single unique long-run row is forced by the equation.

Failure case. A ten-chime histogram near (2/3,1/3) does not prove the model equation πP=π. Even under a stationary distribution, individual paths keep changing mood rather than settling into one state.

Optional investigation: can a stationary chain keep circulating?

Why two-state opposite flows balance

For two states, c=cPCC+bPBC rearranges to cPCB=bPBC: opposite stationary flows balance. With more states, excess inflow from one neighbor can leave through another. Total flow balance need not imply pairwise balance.

Extra background. In a finite time-homogeneous chain, Pᵢⱼ is the chance of moving from i to j in one step. Start with a stationary row π, so the marginal distribution remains π at every time. The adjacent-pair probability Jᵢⱼ=Pr(Xₜ=i,Xₜ₊₁=j)=πᵢPᵢⱼ is a probability per step. Its row totals and column totals both equal π. We call Jᵢⱼ−Jⱼᵢ the net current from i to j. Detailed balance requires that difference to be zero for every pair.

Three states, unchanged occupancies, directional paths

Use new labels A,B,C and the transition rows (1/2,1/3,1/6), (1/6,1/2,1/3), and (1/3,1/6,1/2). Each column sums to 1, so π=(1/3,1/3,1/3) is stationary. All entries are positive, and every state has a self-transition. The directional effect below is therefore unrelated to the earlier periodicity or reducibility counterexamples.

At stationarity, A→B has pair probability (1/3)(1/3)=1/9, while B→A has (1/3)(1/6)=1/18. The same excess 1/18 circulates along A→B→C→A. Every vertex receives as much total probability as it sends. Nevertheless, the stationary record ABCA has probability (1/3)(1/3)³=1/81, while its reversal ACBA has probability (1/3)(1/6)³=1/648 under the same rule: a ratio of eight.

Stationarity and detailed balance are different equations

Stationarity: ∑iπiPij=πjDetailed balance: πiPij=πjPjifor every i,j

Detailed balance implies stationarity: summing its equality over i gives ∑ᵢπᵢPᵢⱼ=πⱼ∑ᵢPⱼᵢ=πⱼ, since the last sum is row j of P and equals 1. The converse fails for the circulating example, whose AB and BA pair flows already disagree. The balanced inspector preset has diagonal entries 1/2 and off-diagonal entries 1/4. It has the same π and holding chances, but each opposite pair flow is 1/12; both three-edge loops then have probability (1/3)(1/4)³=1/192.

Derive the reversed transition rule

Keep the forward chain stationary. For a currently observed state i with πᵢ>0, the probability that its preceding state was j is the adjacent joint probability divided by the current marginal:

Pij*=Pr(Xt−1=j|Xt=i)=πjPjiπi

This P* is stochastic: its entries are nonnegative and its row-i sum is (∑ⱼπⱼPⱼᵢ)/πᵢ=1 by stationarity. Also πP*=π, because ∑ᵢπᵢP*ᵢⱼ=πⱼ∑ᵢPⱼᵢ=πⱼ. The stationary weights matter. P* equals the transpose of P in our uniform presets, but that shortcut is generally wrong for nonuniform π.

Positive-support boundary. If πᵢ=0, conditioning on the stationary event Xₜ=i is undefined by this ratio. Restrict to S={i:πᵢ>0}. This set is closed under P: for j outside S, 0=πⱼ=∑ᵢπᵢPᵢⱼ forces Pᵢⱼ=0 for every i inside S. The reversed kernel and path identities below are defined on that positive stationary support. Zero transition probabilities are permitted there.

Reverse the whole path, including its initial weight

For a word x₀,…,xₙ, its forward stationary probability is πx₀ times the product of its n transition probabilities. Under P*, the reversed word begins with weight πxₙ. Each reversed transition has factor πxₖPxₖ,xₖ₊₁/πxₖ₊₁. Multiplying those factors cancels consecutive positive π terms:

∏k=0n−1πxkπxk+1=πx0πxn

The reversed word's initial πxₙ therefore leaves exactly the forward πx₀ and the original transition factors. This proves

Prπ,P(x0,…,xn)=Prπ,P*(xn,…,x0)

Only positive stationary weights were cancelled, never transition probabilities. An impossible path and its reversed counterpart can both have probability zero. The identity holds for every length and also shows that a stationary forward chain read backward has the Markov path law with initial π and kernel P*.

Same-kernel reversibility. The chain is reversible in its stationary law when every word and its reversal have equal probability under the same P. Detailed balance gives P*=P on positive support, so the identity proves that symmetry for all lengths. Conversely, if same-kernel reversal symmetry holds for every word, applying it to two-observation records i,j gives πᵢPᵢⱼ=πⱼPⱼᵢ: detailed balance. Stationarity alone and one accidentally matching word do not establish this property.

Initialization boundary. The homogeneous reverse rule above uses the forward stationary law. With a nonstationary initial distribution, let μₜ be the forward distribution at time t. Where μₜ₊₁(j)>0, the backward chance of i given next state j is μₜ(i)Pᵢⱼ/μₜ₊₁(j), generally depending on t. On a fixed finite horizon, the reversed process still has a Markov description with those time-dependent backward rows and initial law μ at the final time. Question 4 demonstrates the distinction.

Source scope. Aldous and Fill, Reversible Markov Chains and Random Walks on Graphs, §3.1 and §3.1.1, gives detailed balance, stationary path symmetry and the reversed-chain definition. Its opening setup is finite and irreducible. Our explicit positive-support calculation needs stationarity but not irreducibility; the examples, inspector and changed tasks are original. No hitting-time result from that chapter is used.

Solve and distinguish

1. Calculation. For C→(3/4,1/4) and B→(1/2,1/2), solve for the stationary row using flow balance and normalization.

Hint for question 1

Set the C-to-B flow equal to the B-to-C flow.

Full solution to question 1

Write π=(c,b). Stationarity requires c/4=b/2, so c=2b. Since c+b=1, 3b=1, b=1/3 and c=2/3. Direct check: new Calm chance (2/3)(3/4)+(1/3)(1/2)=1/2+1/6=2/3; Bright is 1/3. Thus πP=π.

2. Explain a counterexample. A two-state chain swaps A and B with certainty. Find a stationary row and describe the distributions from a start at A.

Hint for question 2

After one swap, the two coordinates exchange places.

Full solution to question 2

The row (1/2,1/2) is stationary because swapping equal entries changes nothing. From A, the rows are (1,0), (0,1), (1,0),… and have no limit. The chain is irreducible but has period two, so the aperiodicity condition for convergence fails. Stationarity and convergence from a specified start are distinct assertions.

4. Change the initial law, keep the circulating kernel. Return to the three-state circulating P, but start at A with probability 1 instead of π. Compute Pr(X₀=A|X₁=B) and compare it with the stationary reverse entry P*BA. Then compute Pr(X₁=A|X₂=B). What do these two times establish about using the stationary homogeneous reverse kernel for this start?

Hint for question 4

At the first time the earlier state was fixed. At the second time, use μ₁=(1/2,1/3,1/6). Compute the joint probability μ₁(A)PAB and the full marginal μ₂(B) before dividing. Both conditioning events must have positive probability.

Full solution to question 4

Since X₀=A certainly and Pr(X₁=B)=1/3>0, Pr(X₀=A|X₁=B)=1. The stationary reverse entry is P*BA=(πAPAB)/πB=1/3; it does not apply to this nonstationary start.

At the next time, μ₂(B)=(1/2)(1/3)+(1/3)(1/2)+(1/6)(1/6)=13/36. The joint event X₁=A,X₂=B has probability (1/2)(1/3)=1/6. Consequently Pr(X₁=A|X₂=B)=(1/6)/(13/36)=6/13. The full previous-state row given B at time 2 is (6/13,6/13,1/13). The same backward source state B therefore has previous-A chance 1 at one time and 6/13 at another. This demonstrates time dependence, not a failure of the reversed process to be Markov. On a fixed finite horizon, use the appropriate time-dependent backward rows and the final forward marginal as the reversed initial law. The stationary formula is recovered when every μₜ equals π.

What has been proved here

We solved two small balance systems and directly proved convergence for one two-state rule through shrinking error. The general finite-chain convergence statement relies on irreducibility and aperiodicity and is supplied as a theorem with its hypotheses, not derived by those examples. When presented with a new transition matrix, first check its rows, then solve πP=π, then inspect communication and period before making a long-run claim.
