Vectors and small matrices

A vector in the coordinate plane can be represented by an ordered pair. Add vectors component by component: (1,2)+(3,−1)=(4,1). A scalar is an ordinary number used to scale a vector, so 2(1,2)=(2,4). These operations describe displacements using the chosen coordinate axes.

Worked example. A two-by-two matrix has two columns. Let its first column be (2,1) and its second be (0,3). Applied to a vector (x,y), it produces x times the first column plus y times the second.

(2013)(12)=(27)

Here (1,2) maps to 1(2,1)+2(0,3)=(2,7). The columns tell you what happens to the unit vectors (1,0) and (0,1); scaling and addition then determine every other input.

Try one. Where does the same matrix send (−1,1)?

Worked answer

Use −1(2,1)+1(0,3)=(−2,−1)+(0,3)=(−2,2). Equivalently the first output is 2x and the second is x+3y; substituting x=−1 and y=1 gives the same pair.

Watch the convention. We write input vectors as columns. Each output uses an entire row's products and sum, rather than multiplying corresponding entries independently.

Fit without needlessly magnifying sensitivity

When more observations than unknowns are available, their equations may be inconsistent. Least squares chooses coefficients that minimize the sum of squared residuals. A familiar route forms AᵀA and solves the normal equations. A QR factorization instead uses orthogonal coordinates before solving a triangular system. Both yield the same exact full-rank solution, but AᵀA can square the matrix’s condition number in finite precision.

Background capsule. For A with m rows and n columns, m≥n, and b with m entries, the residual is r=Ax−b. Its Euclidean norm squared is the sum of component squares. A matrix has full column rank when its columns are linearly independent. In a thin QR factorization A=QR, Q has n orthonormal columns (QᵀQ=I) and R is invertible upper triangular. The 2-norm condition number κ₂(A) is the ratio of largest to smallest singular value for full-rank A.

A two-measurement fit

Worked example. Suppose a one-parameter model predicts the same value x for observations 1 and 3. Then A is the column [1,1]ᵀ and b=[1,3]ᵀ. The objective is (x−1)²+(x−3)². Expanding gives 2x²−8x+10, whose derivative 4x−8 vanishes at x=2. The residual is [1,−1]ᵀ and has norm √2.

Here Q=[1/√2,1/√2]ᵀ and R=[√2]. The QR equation Rx=Qᵀb becomes √2x=(1+3)/√2=2√2, so x=2. The residual is orthogonal to Q: Qᵀ[1,−1]ᵀ=0. That geometry explains why no movement along the column of A can reduce the residual further.

For a sensitivity warning, consider columns a₁=[1,0]ᵀ and a₂=[1,ε]ᵀ with small positive ε. They are nearly parallel, so separating their coefficients from measured data is inherently delicate. Forming AᵀA gives [[1,1],[1,1+ε²]]. Its determinant is ε², much smaller than typical entries. In a short-precision calculation, 1+ε² may round to 1 and the computed normal-equation matrix can appear singular. QR does not remove the intrinsic coefficient sensitivity, but it avoids creating this squared-conditioning intermediate.

Derive the reduction and condition identity

A=QR,Rx=QTb,κ2(ATA)=κ2(A)2

For full-rank A=QR, decompose b into QQᵀb plus the perpendicular residual (I−QQᵀ)b. Orthogonality gives ||Ax−b||² = ||Rx−Qᵀb||² + ||(I−QQᵀ)b||². The second term does not depend on x, and invertible R lets us set the first term to zero. This proves Rx=Qᵀb gives the unique least-squares minimizer. Equivalently, its residual is orthogonal to every column of A, yielding normal equations in exact arithmetic.

The eigenvalues of AᵀA are the squares of A’s positive singular values. Therefore the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue, which is κ₂ for this symmetric positive definite matrix, equals (σ_max/σ_min)² = κ₂(A)². This is an exact matrix identity for full-column-rank A. It indicates why errors in constructing and solving normal equations can be magnified. It is not a universal numerical error bound saying that QR always delivers a particular number of correct digits; implementation, scaling, data error and rank thresholds matter.

If A is rank deficient, R is not invertible and the coefficient minimizer is not unique. If columns are nearly dependent, QR can still produce a large coefficient error from small perturbations in b because the original problem is ill-conditioned. A small residual alone does not establish accurate coefficients. Stable Householder QR is common; classical Gram–Schmidt without care may lose orthogonality in finite precision. This lesson’s exact derivation assumes an exact Q and R and labels the implementation issue separately.

Read the geometry and the arithmetic

Exercise 1. Fit a common value x to observations 2, 2, and 5. Give A, Q, R, the QR equation, solution and residual norm.

Hint for exercise 1

A is a column of three ones; its norm is √3.

Solution to exercise 1

A=[1,1,1]ᵀ, Q=A/√3 and R=[√3]. Then Qᵀb=(2+2+5)/√3=3√3, so √3x=3√3 and x=3. The residual Ax−b=[1,1,−2]ᵀ has squared norm 1+1+4=6 and norm √6. Its components sum to zero, confirming orthogonality to A.

Exercise 2. For A with columns [1,0]ᵀ and [1,ε]ᵀ, ε>0, calculate AᵀA and its determinant. If arithmetic rounds 1+ε² to 1, what false conclusion might the normal-equation matrix suggest?

Hint for exercise 2

Compute each column dot product and then the 2×2 determinant.

Solution to exercise 2

AᵀA=[[1,1],[1,1+ε²]], whose determinant is (1)(1+ε²)−1=ε²>0. The exact A is full rank. If 1+ε² rounds to 1, the stored matrix becomes [[1,1],[1,1]] with determinant zero, falsely suggesting rank deficiency. This illustrates an intermediate arithmetic failure; it does not claim QR makes the original near-dependence disappear.

Preserve geometry before solving

Thin QR turns a full-column-rank least-squares problem into a triangular solve by separating the component of b in the column space of A from its orthogonal remainder. Forming AᵀA squares the 2-norm condition number and can squander precision. QR is a numerical choice, not a cure for uncertain data or genuinely near-dependent columns. Revisit conditioning versus algorithm to name those two limits separately.
