What does a promise say on a dry day?

Consider a rule posted beside an outdoor event: “If it rains, the event moves indoors.” On a rainy day with the event still outside, the rule has plainly failed. On a dry day, an indoor event might happen for another reason; the rule itself has not been broken. Formal logic uses that narrow sense of violation for the connective material implication.

Let P stand for “it rains” and Q for “the event moves indoors.” P is the antecedent and Q the consequent. A proposition has a fixed true or false value in each situation. The symbols ¬, ∨, and → mean not, inclusive or, and if–then. A local prerequisite: inclusive or is true whenever either input is true, including when both are true. We will not assume that a rule caused its conclusion; causation requires information beyond this truth table.

We will determine exactly when P → Q is false, compare it to ¬P ∨ Q, and distinguish a valid contrapositive from an invalid converse. The ordinary event gives a concrete interpretation, but the proof applies to any two propositions.

Worked example. The last two rows often sound odd when described as “true promises.” Here “true” means the specified truth-functional sentence has no violating row. It does not mean someone made a good forecast, gave a causal explanation, or intended the dry-day outcome. A contract can add exceptions and time conditions; our P and Q suppress those details.

For a worked instance, suppose it does not rain and organizers move indoors to repair the field. P=F and Q=T. The posted rule P→Q is true under material implication. “If indoors, then rain,” written Q→P, is false in precisely this instance. The direction matters.

Why implication equals not-P or Q

Exact statement. For two-valued propositions P and Q, P → Q is logically equivalent to ¬P ∨ Q. The equivalence describes truth values under every assignment, not a causal mechanism.

P→Q≡¬P∨Q

Complete proof by cases. If P is false, ¬P is true, so ¬P ∨ Q is true regardless of Q; the rule has no violated requirement, so P→Q is true. If P is true, ¬P is false, and the disjunction has exactly Q’s truth value. The rule also has Q’s truth value: a true antecedent requires a true consequent. Thus the expressions agree for both choices of P and both choices of Q.

The contrapositive ¬Q → ¬P is equivalent to P → Q. Using the same violation test, ¬Q → ¬P is false exactly when ¬Q is true and ¬P false—that is, Q false and P true. That is the original false row. By contrast the converse Q→P has its false row when Q true and P false. The dry-day indoor event above disproves equivalence with the converse. Reversing an arrow is not a proof step.

Separate implication from converse

1. Take P=F and Q=F. Evaluate P→Q, Q→P, and ¬Q→¬P. Explain why the agreement here proves little.

Hint for question 1

Check whether an implication has a true antecedent and false consequent.

Full solution to question 1

All three are true: P→Q and Q→P have false antecedents, while ¬Q→¬P is T→T. One row of agreement cannot prove equivalence. The contrapositive agrees in every row by the case argument; the converse fails in a different row.

2. A shop says, “If a customer has a receipt, we issue a refund.” A customer receives a refund without a receipt. Has the stated implication been violated? Does that case refute its converse?

Hint for question 2

Use P=“has receipt” and Q=“gets refund.”

Full solution to question 2

Here P=F and Q=T. P→Q is true: the rule requires a refund only when a receipt is present. The converse Q→P is false because the customer gets a refund but has no receipt. A real shop may have more policies; this evaluates only the single formal sentence.

Try inventing one ordinary-language sentence whose intended meaning includes time, cause, or probability. Its material implication translation may be too weak. That limitation is useful: formalization starts by choosing precisely what the symbols say.

The arrow has one failure mode

P→Q fails only when P holds and Q does not. Its truth table is exactly that of ¬P∨Q, and its contrapositive is equivalent. The converse is a different claim. The result has a complete four-case proof under classical propositional semantics; it does not settle conversational implications or causation.

Stop when you can identify a violating row and distinguish all three sentences. The next useful move is to put “for every” or “there exists” before a rule, where the domain and the order of quantifiers become part of its meaning.
