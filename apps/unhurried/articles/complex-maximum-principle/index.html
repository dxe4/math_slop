Why a holomorphic peak cannot hide inside

For a nonconstant holomorphic function, the largest modulus on a closed disk occurs on its boundary. The word “modulus” matters: complex numbers have no usual greater-than order, but their lengths do. The disk and continuity assumptions matter too, because the maximum must exist before we can locate it.

Background capsule. The modulus |a+bi|=√(a²+b²) is the distance to zero. A closed disk |z−c|≤R is compact: a continuous real-valued function on it attains a maximum. Cauchy's integral formula says that for f holomorphic on a neighborhood of a closed disk, f(a)=(1/2πi)∮f(z)/(z−a)dz at interior a. On a centered circle this becomes an average of boundary values. We use this theorem and a standard equality case for the triangle inequality; the latter says an average of vectors has full common magnitude only when their directions agree.

Test a polynomial on the unit disk

Worked example. Let f(z)=z+1 on |z|≤1. The triangle inequality gives |z+1|≤|z|+1≤2. At boundary point z=1 equality holds, so the maximum is exactly 2. At center z=0 the modulus is 1. The inequality proves the bound for every point, which a sampled contour picture could not do.

For f(z)=z², |f(z)|=|z|²; its largest value on the disk is 1 and occurs at every boundary point. An interior value, including zero, is strictly smaller. For a constant function f(z)=3i, however, every interior point also has the maximum modulus 3. Thus a correct maximum principle must allow the constant case rather than claim the maximum occurs only at the edge.

From a mean to a maximum

Disk maximum-modulus theorem. If f is continuous on |z−c|≤R and holomorphic on the open disk |z−c|<R, then

max|z−c|≤R|f(z)|=max|z−c|=R|f(z)|

To see the local engine, take a small circle |z−a|=ρ entirely inside the disk. Cauchy's formula, applied on this smaller circle, gives f(a)=(1/2π)∫02πf(a+ρeit)dt. Therefore |f(a)|≤(1/2π)∫|f(a+ρeit)|dt. This mean-value inequality is proved from the cited Cauchy formula and triangle inequality.

Suppose |f| has a global maximum M at an interior point a. Every value on each sufficiently small circle has modulus at most M, while its average is f(a) of modulus M. Equality in the integral triangle inequality forces those values to have the same phase as f(a) almost everywhere; continuity makes the equality hold everywhere. Hence f is constant on each such circle and on a neighborhood of a. The identity theorem, a standard consequence of Cauchy theory, then makes f constant throughout the connected open disk. Equivalently, if f is nonconstant, no interior maximum is possible. This argument states explicitly where the identity theorem enters; it is not proved independently here.

The boundary equality follows because compactness supplies a maximum on the closed disk, and a nonconstant function cannot attain it inside. If f is constant, boundary and interior values are equal anyway. The function 1/z on the unit disk is an instructive failure case: it is singular at zero and unbounded near it, so neither continuity on the disk nor holomorphy inside holds.

Bound a function and audit a peak

1. Calculate. Find the maximum of |2z−1| on |z|≤2, and identify a point attaining it.

Hint for 1

Use |2z−1|≤2|z|+1, then find a boundary point where the two terms point the same way.

Worked solution for 1

For |z|≤2, |2z−1|≤2|z|+1≤5. At z=−2, the value is |−5|=5. Thus the maximum is 5. The polynomial is entire, so the disk theorem also predicts that a boundary point suffices, while the triangle inequality supplies its exact value.

2. Explain. Why is f(z)=1/(z−1/2) on |z|≤1 no counterexample to the theorem when its modulus becomes arbitrarily large near 1/2?

Hint for 2

Check whether f is defined and holomorphic throughout the open disk.

Worked solution for 2

The point 1/2 is inside the unit disk and is a pole of f. The function is not defined there, is not continuous on the closed disk, and is not holomorphic on the open disk. The maximum-modulus theorem therefore makes no claim about this example; the unbounded behavior is compatible with its hypotheses being false.

Check the closed disk and the open one

Continuity on the closed disk gives an attained maximum; holomorphy inside moves that maximum to the boundary unless the function is constant. Cauchy's local mean-value formula supplies the core inequality. This lesson uses the identity theorem for the final constancy step, so a complete proof of all underlying Cauchy theory lies beyond this page. The boundary rule cannot be applied across an interior pole.
