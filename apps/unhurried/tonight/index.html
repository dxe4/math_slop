The idea ↗
One good question · about 10 minutes · no prerequisites

A small dinner-party mystery.

Five people meet for dinner. Count only acquaintances within this group, and assume knowing someone goes both ways. Could all five people know a different number of the others?

You can settle this without listing every possible dinner party. Take a moment to choose “yes” or “no” and say what would convince you. Paper is useful; an account or a timer is not.

Try a few counts

Each person can know 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 of the other guests. For all five counts to be different, the group would need one person with each count. Draw five dots and connect two dots when those people know each other. Can you arrange a dot with no lines and another with four?

Build your own dinner party

Check a pair when those two guests know each other. Try to make every count different. You can also change the group size or connect everyone at once.

A worked setting: A knows B, B knows C, and C knows D. These are mutual connections. E knows nobody in this group.

A and D share a count, as do B and C. Add a connection between A and E on paper: which two counts change?

Each network you try is one example. Finding matching counts in many examples can suggest a rule; an argument is still needed to cover every possible network.

A hint, if you want one

A guest who knows all four others must know the guest who supposedly knows nobody.

Worked answer

No. The counts 0 and 4 cannot occur together: a person with four acquaintances knows everybody else, including any person claimed to have zero. So at most four of the five possible counts can occur in this group. Five people assigned to at most four counts force two people to share a count. This is the pigeonhole principle: when more objects are put into fewer categories, a category receives at least two objects.

We have proved that some two guests have the same count. We have not identified which guests they are, and they need not know each other.

Does it still work with six?

Six guests could each have 0 through 5 acquaintances, giving six possible counts. Decide whether six distinct counts are possible before opening the answer. Which step in the five-person reasoning changes?

Worked answer for six guests

Six distinct counts would require every count from 0 to 5. A guest with five acquaintances knows all the other guests, so nobody can have zero. There are therefore at most five usable counts for six people, and two guests must share one. More generally, with any group of n people, where n is at least 2, counts range from 0 to n−1. The two extremes cannot occur together, leaving at most n−1 counts for n people. The same conclusion follows.

Where the proof stops. It relies on mutual acquaintance and on counting only people in the group. If the relation is one-way, such as “follows,” the result can fail: among three people A, B and C, let A follow nobody, B follow A, and C follow A and B. Their outward counts are 0, 1 and 2.

A satisfying place to stop

You found an impossibility by spotting two extremes that cannot coexist. That is a complete mathematical argument; there is no need to begin a course tonight.
